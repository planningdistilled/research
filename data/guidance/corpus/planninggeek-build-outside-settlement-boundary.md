## Summary

A practitioner explainer on whether housing outside a settlement boundary now has a route under the August 2026 National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF). It walks through the Annex B definition of "settlement", the S4 route inside settlements, and the S5 categories outside them, especially S5(1)(e) limited infilling and S5(1)(j) evidenced unmet need. It argues that "physically well-related" is not the same as "adjoining" and is judged on roads, footpaths, development pattern, topography and landscape breaks, citing the Buntingford appeal (24 Sep 2026) as a site-specific example. It stresses that S5 does not apply in the Green Belt (GB6 to GB8 apply instead), that grey belt is still Green Belt, and that a five-year housing land supply shortfall or a Housing Delivery Test (HDT) below 75% opens the route but does not override landscape, flood, access or design harm.

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Decide settlement status using the 2026 Annex B definition, not only the old policies-map line; note the special qualification for villages lying within and defined as part of the Green Belt.
- If outside a settlement, identify the exact S5 category rather than relying on general "housing need".
- Treat "physically well-related" as a judgement about the actual relationship (connections, settlement form, topography, landscape breaks), not adjacency to the boundary.
- Do not transplant an S5 edge-of-settlement argument into the Green Belt; assess under GB6 to GB8 and, for major housing, the GB8 Golden Rules.
- Treat a supply shortfall as an argument that opens S5(1)(j), not as a permission: landscape (N4 in National Landscapes, formerly Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty), flood, access, heritage and design can still decide the case.
- Treat the S5(4) exceptional-circumstances route as deliberately harder.

## Positions against our propositions

- **SH2 (agrees):** "There is also a special qualification for villages which lie within and are defined as part of the Green Belt." (section "First question: is the site actually outside the settlement?"); and "S5 does not apply. The proposal must be assessed under the separate Green Belt policies GB6 to GB8" (table "The quick answer").
- **S5 (agrees):** "A site may be physically well-related because of its relationship to the built-up area, road and footpath connections, the pattern of nearby development, topography and the way the settlement is likely to grow." (section "Does an edge-of-settlement site have to touch the boundary?"); and on S5(4): "If none applies, S5 has a separate exceptional-circumstances route, but that is deliberately harder" (section "What if the LPA has a healthy five year supply?").
- **SUP (agrees):** "A five year supply shortfall therefore gives you a potentially powerful planning argument. It does not make landscape harm, flooding, unsafe access or a badly designed scheme disappear." (section "How does a lack of five year housing land supply help?").
- **GB (agrees):** "It still does not guarantee permission. The site must actually meet the grey-belt definition" (section "What if the land is grey belt?") — passing GB7(1)(g) is not approval.
- **METH (silent):** the source cites the Buntingford appeal as an illustrative example and says "That was a site-specific judgement, not a rule that roads can never separate a development from a settlement." (section "Does an edge-of-settlement site have to touch the boundary?"). This is the ordinary point that a single appeal's planning judgement is not a rule; it does not critique counting appeal outcomes, the evidential weight of appeal decisions, or an empirical coding approach, and the page itself relies on an appeal as evidence of how S5(1)(j) is applied. Not a METH stance (adversarial check, 2 Oct 2026).
