## Summary

Written before the 2026 Framework was published, so it applies the December 2024 NPPF (paragraphs 143 and 155, footnote 7). The post reviews three challenges to inspectors' grey belt decisions. In Wrotham Parish Council v Secretary of State (Lieven J, 30 January 2026), the footnote 7 "strong reason" test was held to apply to the development actually proposed, not to hypothetical development. The judge also said that refusing permission after positive grey belt findings would be "plainly contrary" to the purpose of grey belt. In Boningdale Homes (Bishops Wood, South Staffordshire), the government conceded that the inspector was wrong to treat a heritage constraint on part of the site as a "strong reason for restricting" development. In O15 (Warwick District), the government was minded to concede that the inspector was wrong to test "fundamentally undermine the purposes" against the site itself; she had treated a village-edge site as countryside encroachment. Ricketts concludes that the tests are fairly clear and that the litigation shows the system working.

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Apply the footnote 7 "strong reason" test to the specific development proposed, not to any hypothetical development of the site.
- Do not treat a constraint on an undeveloped part of a site as a strong reason for restricting the development actually proposed.
- Judge "fundamentally undermine the purposes (taken together) of the remaining Green Belt" across the plan area, not by the effect on the site itself.
- Do not refuse permission once grey belt findings have been made in the scheme's favour by falling back on very special circumstances (VSC). Under the 2024 NPPF that was held to defeat the purpose of grey belt.

## Positions against our propositions

- **GB, agrees.** "It is not an answer that the developer can fall back on broader Green Belt policies and show very special circumstances. The point of the introduction of the Grey Belt is to avoid that policy requirement." (Wrotham, Lieven J, quoted). This applies the December 2024 NPPF, and it is a remark in support of reading footnote 7 against the development actually proposed, not a holding that permission must follow. It says that once a site passes the grey belt tests, Green Belt harm cannot be brought back through a very special circumstances (VSC) requirement. That fits our point that passing GB7 is not approval: the losses we coded came through other S5(2) policy triggers (design, transport, heritage and so on), and openness was not weighed again. It does not say that a scheme passing the grey belt tests must be permitted.
- **GB, silent (scale-of-assessment point only).** "I have characterised the site as countryside rather than as part of the village." (O15, Warwick, para 19 of the inspector's 22 December 2025 decision, December 2024 NPPF para 155a). The quote is verbatim, but the error the Secretary of State was minded to concede is the scale of the test: the inspector judged "fundamentally undermine the purposes (taken together) of the remaining Green Belt across the area of the plan" by encroachment on the site itself, not across the remaining Green Belt. Ricketts does not say the countryside-not-village characterisation was wrong, and says nothing about whether grey belt is usually won at a village edge or whether passing the test leads to approval. Lesson for our analysis: site-level encroachment alone does not fail the GB7 "remaining Green Belt across the area" criterion. That is consistent with our GB proposition, not a qualification of it.
- **METH, silent (not a stance).** The closing paragraph ("The NPPF grey belt tests are relatively clear in my view but inevitably there are uncertainties and/or misinterpretations.") is about the policy wording, not about how decisions are studied. Ricketts says nothing about counting appeal outcomes or the weight of appeal decisions as precedent; he says the limited litigation shows "the system working as it should". Our own note, not his: two of the three inspector decisions he discusses (Boningdale, O15; both December 2024 NPPF) were conceded or likely to be conceded as unlawful, so a coded decision may later be quashed. Worth checking whether any decisions in our database have been challenged.
