## Summary

Chapter 15 sets transport policy in the August 2026 National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF). TR3 is the location policy that GB7(1)(g)(iii) points to. Development that could generate significant movement should be in locations that "limit the need to travel, particularly by private car, and offer a genuine choice of transport modes", judged in the context of the area and the type of development. In rural areas, opportunities to improve walking, wheeling, cycling and public transport should be taken. TR3(2) makes the Connectivity Tool a required input "alongside other relevant quantitative or qualitative evidence". TR4 (street design) asks for priority to walking, continuous footways and lit routes, and for measures to minimise conflict between pedestrians and vehicles. TR6 keeps the "severe" network impact and "unacceptable" highway safety refusal tests.

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Judge location sustainability by whether the site limits car dependence and offers a genuine choice of modes, scaled to the area and the type of development (TR3(1)(a)).
- Use the Connectivity Tool together with other quantitative or qualitative evidence when assessing connectivity (TR3(2)).
- In rural areas, look for chances to improve walking, wheeling, cycling and public transport that the development can support (TR3(1)(e)).
- Expect schemes to provide continuous, adequately lit footways and to minimise conflict between pedestrians and vehicles (TR4(1)(b), (c)). Use Manual for Streets and the Design and Placemaking guidance (footnote 46).
- Refuse on transport grounds only for a "severe" network impact or an "unacceptable" highway safety impact, after mitigation (TR6(4)).

## Positions against our propositions

- **TR, not applicable (policy text, no stance).** This page is the TR3 policy itself, not commentary on how it is applied. TR3(1)(a) sets the test (limit the need to travel and offer a genuine choice of modes, in context); our TR proposition is an empirical finding about how inspectors decide that test in practice (route quality over distance). The text says nothing about how route quality and distance should be weighed, so it neither agrees nor qualifies. TR3(2) makes the Connectivity Tool one evidence input "alongside other relevant quantitative or qualitative evidence"; it does not say the tool's score outweighs site-specific route evidence, so it does not cut against the proposition either. Worth noting in our write-up that the Connectivity Tool should be used. Quotes: "This means the location should limit the need to travel, particularly by private car, and offer a genuine choice of transport modes for residents and users, unless the nature of the development would make this impractical" (TR3(1)(a)); "The Connectivity Tool should be used alongside other relevant quantitative or qualitative evidence in assessing the connectivity of particular locations proposed for development." (TR3(2)).
- **TR, agrees (in part).** TR4 treats continuous footways and lighting as separate features and asks for pedestrian-vehicle conflict to be minimised. That is consistent with lighting not making up for a missing footway. But TR4 is about the design of the scheme, not about access routes to services. Quotes: "continuous footways and segregated cycle facilities which are adequately lit" (TR4(1)(b)); "Minimise the scope for conflict between pedestrians, cyclists and vehicles" (TR4(1)(c)(i)).
