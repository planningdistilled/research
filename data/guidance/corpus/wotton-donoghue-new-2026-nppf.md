# The new 2026 NPPF: What changed and what does it mean for you? (Wotton Donoghue Architects, Bristol)

## Summary

A short client blog from a Bristol architecture practice, published three days after the August 2026 National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF). It notes the switch from paragraph numbers to policy codes and picks out five policies: HC6 (community facilities), L3 (minimum densities near well-connected stations: 35 and 45 dwellings per hectare within 800m), HO5 (40% of homes on major sites to meet M4(2), the Building Regulations accessible and adaptable standard), TR3 (sustainable locations) and GB7 (development near stations not inappropriate in the Green Belt). It also covers the August 2026 Biodiversity Net Gain (BNG) regulation changes. On TR3 it ties sustainable location to the government's connectivity tool score, which rises the closer a site is to public transport and services. It gives no method for applying any of the tests.

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Nothing aimed at officers; it is advice to applicants.
- Expect transport connectivity (as scored by the national connectivity tool) to carry more weight in decisions.
- Expect councils to be less able to apply stricter local connectivity tests than the national one.
- Treat GB7 station sites as a new Green Belt opportunity, subject to the listed criteria and the GB8 "golden rules".

## Positions against our propositions

- **TR (silent; checked 2 Oct 2026, earlier coded "qualifies"):** "Put simply, the closer to public transport/walking distance to services the higher the score on the connectivity tool. It allows for nationwide standardisation and limits the ability for local councils to introduce more restrictive connectivity tests." (TR3 section). This describes how the government connectivity tool scores sites, in a three-sentence client summary. It does not say how TR3 or GB7(1)(g)(iii) is applied to a specific site, and it does not address whether the quality of the walking route or the distance decides. The remark about "more restrictive connectivity tests" is about local plan-level tests, not site-level route evidence. It does not disagree with our reading. At most it hints that proximity scores may count for more, which is worth a lead (see below) but is not a stance.

## Leads

- The government connectivity tool (introduced late 2025): how inspectors have treated its score against site-specific walking-route evidence in TR3 and GB7(1)(g)(iii) decisions.
- Uses "H05" for the policy we have as HO5; small labelling slip.
