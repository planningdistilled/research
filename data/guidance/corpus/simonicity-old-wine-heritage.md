## Summary

A post by Ricketts, with analysis by his Town Legal colleague Tom Brooks, comparing HE6 (proposals affecting designated heritage assets) with paragraphs 212 to 215 of the December 2024 NPPF. It concludes that the tests have not changed in substance. "Great weight" has become "substantial weight", which the government says is not a substantive change. HE6(3) adds that harm is "a matter of considerable importance and weight", which matches the existing statutory duty. Removing the "less than substantial harm" category makes no practical difference, because the decision-maker must still decide whether harm is substantial (HE5(2)(c)). Changes that may help developers: express support for positive effects, and "important" public benefits that now include long-term reuse of a vacant listed building and energy-efficiency or low-carbon heating measures. "Optimum viable use" has gone. The post also notes King Media Ltd v Secretary of State (Lieven J, 25 September 2026), decided under the old NPPF. The judge held that a decision-maker need not say precisely where on a scale a heritage harm lies.

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Give substantial weight to the conservation of a designated heritage asset, whether the effect is positive, harmful or a total loss (HE6(1)). Treat any harm as a matter of considerable importance and weight (HE6(3)).
- Decide whether any harm is substantial. "Substantial harm" means seriously affecting a key element of the asset's significance (HE5(2)(c)).
- For harm that is not substantial, weigh it against the public benefits (HE6(4)). Treat reuse of a vacant listed building and energy-efficiency or low-carbon heating measures as "important" public benefits.
- Apply HE6(5) and HE6(6) to substantial harm or total loss as before.
- Support proposals that have a positive effect (HE6(2)).

## Positions against our propositions

- **A2, agrees (indirect).** "we do not see NPPF 2026 changes as substantively changing the tests for assessing the impact of a proposed development on the significance of a designated heritage asset" (summary paragraph). If the national heritage tests are unchanged, local heritage policies written to the 2024 tests are not "inconsistent with the Framework" on that point. That supports our view that heritage policies keep full weight under Annex A paragraph 2.
- **DIV, silent.** The post says nothing about councils and inspectors reaching different results on the same tests. It quotes King Media v Secretary of State: "There is no obligation on a decision maker to set out precisely where on a scale of harm to heritage assets a particular development lies". That is a High Court point about how much an inspector must say in a decision, from an advertisement discontinuance appeal under the old NPPF. Ricketts says his firm acted for the claimant and does not comment further. It does not address our divergence finding. At most it is a coding caution: a decision may lawfully leave the degree of heritage harm unstated, so some letters will not support a finer code.
- **METH, silent.** The post does not discuss counting appeal outcomes, the precedent weight of appeal decisions, or empirical analysis of decisions. Its remark that "Therefore, in practice, the removal of less than substantial harm as an express category is inconsequential." (key changes list) is about the HE6 test, not about our method. The only practical point for coding is a note, not a disagreement: the substantial / not-substantial split survives under HE5(2)(c), so heritage findings should still record which one applies.
