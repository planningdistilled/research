# Is your appeal decision written in the stars?

## Summary
In this Planning Inspectorate (PINS) blog post, an Inspector Profession Lead argues that "headline rates of allowed appeals don’t tell the full story". For section 78 (s78) planning appeals in 2025/26, the allowed rates were: written representations 30% of 9,522; hearings 53% of 416; inquiries 72% of 159; overall 31% of 10,097. She puts the gap down to case selection rather than the quality of decisions. Written representations cases are simpler, and in 70% of them the inspector reached the same decision as the council, "Both the LPA and inspector are usually assessing the evidence against the same relevant policy context". Hearing and inquiry cases are complex, are often non-determination appeals, and the inspector decides them "with far more information than was available to the LPA". The post says nothing about the 2026 NPPF as such. It notes the new expedited written representations route (Part 1), under which appeals are decided only on the evidence the council saw.

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do
- Do not read the choice of procedure as a measure of the chance of success. An inquiry "wouldn’t actually be any more likely to succeed simply because there is an opportunity to present evidence orally".
- Submit the best available evidence at application stage, because Part 1 appeals allow no new evidence.
- It notes, about inspectors rather than councils, that new inspectors' decisions are read by experienced inspectors during training, and that quality assurance continues after that.

## Positions against our propositions
- **METH, qualifies.** "why headline rates of allowed appeals don’t tell the full story" (introduction). The outcome rates we derive should be read alongside the procedure type and whether the appeal was against non-determination. Allowed rates differ sharply by procedure (30% / 53% / 72%) because of which cases go down each route, not because of the merits of a policy test.
- **DIV, silent (context only).** The post says "this contributes to the bigger variance between the LPA and inspector decisions, and the higher allowed rates for these few appeals" (Section "A numbers game?"). This is about overall outcomes (allowed or dismissed) by procedure, not about how councils and inspectors find on the same policy test. The direction is also the opposite of ours: PINS explains why inspectors *allow* more hearing and inquiry appeals than councils approved, while DIV is about councils passing tests that inspectors then fail. So it does not qualify DIV. It is still a useful caution: before we present a test-level divergence as a difference in reading the policy, we should check whether the inspector had new evidence (hearing, inquiry or late material) that the council did not.
