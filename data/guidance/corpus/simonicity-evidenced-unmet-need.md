## Summary

A close look at S5(1)(j): development outside settlements that meets an "evidenced unmet need" and is physically well-related to an existing settlement. Ricketts sets out the S5 "seesaw". Within categories a to j, permission should be granted unless the adverse effects substantially outweigh the benefits. Outside them, permission should be granted only "in exceptional circumstances". He identifies two readings of the housing words in (j). Under the "wider" reading, other evidenced needs such as affordable housing can count. Under the "narrow" reading, unmet housing need means only no five-year housing land supply (5YHLS) or a Housing Delivery Test (HDT) result below 75%. He says that for grey belt only the narrow reading can apply, because of the GB7 footnote. He discusses Annex A paragraph 3. He sets an inspector's decision of 14 September 2026 at Kingston Bagpuize (Vale of White Horse), which took the wider reading, against Baroness Taylor's House of Lords answer of 15 September 2026, which points to the narrow one. He concludes that the courts may have to decide and that the government should clarify the policy.

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Outside settlements, decide first whether the scheme falls within S5(1) categories a to j. If it does not, permission requires exceptional circumstances in which the benefits substantially outweigh the adverse effects.
- For employment uses, test "unmet need" against E2(2): market signals, and whether existing allocations meet the specific locational requirements.
- For housing, be aware that whether affordable-housing need alone counts as "evidenced unmet need" is contested. One inspector has said yes. The government's answer in the House of Lords suggests no.
- For grey belt housing, treat unmet need as meaning no 5YHLS or HDT below 75% (GB7 footnote).
- Apply Annex A paragraph 3: for five years after a plan is adopted, a standard-method figure above the plan requirement is not evidence of unmet need.

## Positions against our propositions

- **S5, agrees.** "If your development does not fall within one of the ten categories listed, a to j, the national policy is that planning permission should only be granted “in exceptional circumstances" (opening section on S5). This supports our view that falling outside every category (S5(4)) is a very high bar.
- **S5, agrees.** "Linguistically, I’m genuinely unsure which is right." (discussion of the narrow and wider interpretations of category j). This supports our point that S5(1)(j) has competing readings. Here the dispute is over "evidenced unmet need", alongside the "physically well-related" limb we flagged.
- **SUP, agrees.** "it does not mean that planning permission for housing will be automatically granted" (Baroness Taylor's answer of 15 September 2026, quoted). This supports our view that a supply shortfall opens the gate but does not decide the case.
- **S5, agrees (not SUP).** "indicating that the absence of a five year deliverable supply and HDT outcomes are not the only means by which an unmet need can be proven to exist" (the Kingston Bagpuize inspector's decision letter of 14 September 2026, quoted by Ricketts, who says he is "genuinely unsure" which reading is right). This is one side of the competing readings of S5(1)(j), here on the "evidenced unmet need" limb. On the wider reading, a significant affordable-housing need together with a marginal five-year supply brought the scheme within category (j). It does not cut against SUP. SUP says a supply shortfall opens the gate but does not decide the case. It does not say a shortfall is the only thing that opens the gate, and nothing quoted says the supply position or the affordable need decided the appeal. Practical note for our S5(1)(j) analysis: affordable-housing need is a contested second way through the gate.
- **S5, agrees (not METH).** "which would imply that that decision letter to be in error (although this interpretation question would ultimately be for the courts)" (after Baroness Taylor's answer). This is about one interpretive dispute: the wider and narrow readings of "evidenced unmet need" in S5(1)(j). Ricketts says the government's answer points one way and the Kingston Bagpuize decision the other, and that the courts would settle it. That supports our point that S5(1)(j) has competing readings. It is not a critique of counting appeal outcomes or of treating appeal decisions as evidence of how the Framework is applied, so it is not coded against METH. Practical note: the decisions coded as passing S5(1)(j) on affordable-housing need alone may rest on a reading the government does not share.
