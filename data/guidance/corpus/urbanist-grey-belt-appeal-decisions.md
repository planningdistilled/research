## Summary

A London architecture practice's running digest of 24 grey belt decisions (council and Planning Inspectorate appeal decisions), written by a chartered town planner. Important caveat: every case is decided under the **December 2024** National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) paragraphs 143, 155 and 156 (now GB2, GB7(1)(g) and GB8 in the August 2026 Framework); the page was last modified in March 2026, before the August 2026 Framework. Each case ends with a "key lesson". Its overall message is that grey belt is "not a carte blanche": classification as grey belt is necessary but not sufficient, housing-supply shortfall helps but does not override Green Belt, design or landscape harm, and sites adjoining villages and enclosed by development do best.

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Treat grey belt status as opening the door only; the scheme still has to pass Golden Rules (affordable housing), sustainable location, design and landscape tests.
- Expect a site that plays a strong role against sprawl to fail even with a "dismal" housing land supply (Beaconsfield).
- Settle town-versus-village questions early: several outcomes (Bagshot, Daws Heath, Smallfield) turned on the place being a village, so purposes (a), (b) and (d) did not apply.
- Assess grey belt site by site rather than relying only on strategic parcel reviews (Navestock).
- Note that inspectors accepted weak walking links as "sustainable location" for small rural schemes (Over Alderley, Copthorne) under the 2024 test.

## Positions against our propositions

- **GB — agrees.** "Despite acknowledging a "dismal" housing supply, the inspector concluded that this did not outweigh the harm to the Green Belt." (Case 1, Beaconsfield). And: "Grey Belt classification alone is insufficient for approval; developments must meet the Golden Rules for affordable housing, provide significant public benefits, and be located in a sustainable location with strong infrastructure and transport connections." (Case 9, Leighton Buzzard, key lesson). Village-edge pattern: "Where a site clearly satisfies the criteria - particularly adjoining a village, delivering affordable housing, and addressing local need - it can be approved without resorting to the tilted balance, even in Green Belt locations." (Case 16, Daws Heath, key lesson).
- **SUP — agrees.** "Grey Belt status and housing need may open the door to development, but do not override poor design or landscape harm." (Case 22, Higher Rads End, key lesson).
- **TR — not applicable (different test).** "Although pedestrian and public transport access was limited, the inspector considered the location sufficiently sustainable given nearby settlements, cycle routes, and short car journey distances." (Case 19, Over Alderley). This is the firm's paraphrase of a decision under the December 2024 Framework (para 155 "sustainable location" limb, read with the old rural-transport paragraphs), for two replacement dwellings. It does not discuss walking-route quality against distance, and it predates TR3 / GB7(1)(g)(iii), so it neither supports nor contradicts our TR proposition. Kept as a lead: check whether August 2026 decisions still accept cycle or short car trips where walking access is weak.
- **METH — agrees (with a caution).** "inspectors are applying the Grey Belt policy with growing consistency" (introduction). It too reads appeal decisions as the evidence of how policy is applied, but from a hand-picked set of about 24 cases, mixing council and appeal decisions, with no outcome rates. Our larger coded dataset is the same approach done more systematically.
