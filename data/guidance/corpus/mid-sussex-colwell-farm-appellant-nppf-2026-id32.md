## Summary

The appellant's planning witness (SLR Consulting, for Miller Homes) gives a post-17-August submission on appeal 6005786 (80 homes, edge of Haywards Heath; the council cannot show a five-year housing land supply, or 5YHLS). The PDF has a text layer and extracted without needing OCR. It argues that S5(1)(j), together with HO7, replaces the old paragraph 11(d) tilted balance with a stronger presumption. On this reading, one S5(1) category is enough, "physically well-related" is met by adjoining the settlement boundary, and S5(1)(j) carries no separate accessibility test. It applies Annex A paragraph 2 broadly: countryside and settlement-boundary policies (DP6, DP12, DP15), emerging plan policies, and the heritage policies DP34 and DP35 all go to "very limited" weight. It gives the conflict with the development plan only very limited negative weight. Appendix A (i-transport) argues the Annex B 800 m walking distance applies only to stations and hot-food takeaways, and that there is no cap on walking distance to services. Appendix B (Blake Turnbull) maps the old heritage tests onto HE5 to HE7.

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

(Advocacy for the appellant, not guidance. It shows how promoters are reading the Framework.)
- Treat S5(1) categories as alternatives: "Compliance with one criterion is sufficient."
- Treat S5(2) refusal triggers as part of the balance, not a switch that turns off the tilted balance as the old footnote 7 did.
- Give "very limited" weight to any local policy, or part of a policy, more restrictive than S5, including heritage policies.
- Assess walking to services case by case with no distance cap, using the Department for Transport (DfT) Connectivity Tool alongside other evidence.

## Positions against our propositions

- **S5, agrees** (the category is the whole case). "Compliance with one criterion is" sufficient (para 2.14). The appellant's whole case on principle rests on fitting S5(1)(j).
- **S5, qualifies** (reading of S5(1)(j)). "S5(1)(j) does not include any express requirements itself in respect to accessibility, beyond" being physically well-related (para 4.9). This is the permissive reading of "physically well-related": adjoining a settlement boundary is enough, and accessibility is not part of the test.
- **SUP, qualifies** (emphasis, not a contrary test). The appellant reads the absence of a five-year housing land supply (5YHLS) as triggering S5(1)(j) for edge-of-settlement sites, and then leaves the decision to a strongly tilted balance: "growth at the edge of settlements should be permitted unless the new tilted balance is not satisfied: in short," permission should be granted "unless a substantial level of harms would result" (para 4.8). So the shortfall opens the gate (with the S5(1)(j) location), and the harms still decide. The appellant does not say the shortfall alone decides; it argues how heavily the balance tilts. Advocacy flourish: "The appeal scheme is clearly precisely the type of proposal the new NPPF was designed to" support (para 2.59).
- **A2, disagrees.** It reads Annex A paragraph 2 as cutting spatial-preference and heritage policies: "the parts of DP6 which restrict development in the countryside beyond" S5 are inconsistent, so weight drops to very limited (para 3.6); "Consequently, DP34 should attract very limited weight as this is the effect of Annex A," paragraph 2 (para 4.45, heritage).
- **TR, agrees (in part; advocacy).** It says distance is not the test: "There are no specified walking distances relating to services, facilities, and bus stops, and therefore" "these must be considered on a case-by-case basis" (Appendix A, para 1.7.2), and the Annex B 800 m figure is not a cap. It does not set route quality aside: its evidence rests on "a detailed walking audit" (para 1.3.8) and on footway, dropped-kerb and footpath-resurfacing works to "ensure there are consistent, safe, and attractive connections along the desire line routes" (para 1.3.12). It says nothing on walking in the carriageway or street lighting. Its "no upper limit" argument for distance is the appellant's gloss, and does not conflict with a route-quality test.
