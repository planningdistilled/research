## Summary

The government's explanation of the draft Framework, with 225 questions. It explains why national decision-making policies were kept non-statutory but given "day one" force by making inconsistent local policies carry very limited weight. It explains the new S3 to S5 spatial presumption: settlements identified in plans decide whether S4 (inclusive) or S5 (restrictive list) applies; the old paragraph 11(d) "tilted balance" is replaced; housing-need development outside settlements must be well related to a settlement "to guard against development being badly-located". It cites grey belt appeal outcomes (80% of major residential appeals on grey belt allowed) as evidence that national policy changes outcomes. Questions 34 to 40 ask about spatial strategy, the settlement definition and the S5 categories.

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Treat the Framework's locational principles (S4/S5) as replacing the locational approach previously found only in local plans.
- Identify settlement boundaries or criteria in plans so S4/S5 can be applied.
- Approve within settlements unless benefits are substantially outweighed; outside, approve only listed categories.
- Treat housing-need proposals outside settlements as acceptable in principle only where well related to an existing settlement.
- Give very little weight to inconsistent development plan policies unless examined and adopted against the new Framework.

## Positions against our propositions

- **SH1 — agrees.** Chapter 4, S2, p.32 (PDF): "It emphasises the importance of identifying settlement boundaries, or clear criteria for identifying settlements, to support the proposed approach to decision-making on development within and outside settlements (policies S4 and S5)." The local plan's job is to identify settlements for the S4/S5 route, not to give tiers weight of their own.
- **S5 — agrees.** S5 commentary, p.34 (PDF): "this policy restricts the types of development that are considered acceptable outside settlements, in order to prevent unsustainable patterns of growth and conserve rural character."
- **SUP — agrees.** S5 commentary, p.34 (PDF): "it adds the proviso that in such circumstances development should be well-related to an existing settlement (unless the nature of the use would make this inappropriate), to guard against development being badly-located." Supply shortfall opens the gate but location still decides.
- **A2 — silent (draft-era; checked 2026-10-02).** Transitional arrangements, p.106 (PDF): "we are proposing that development plan policies should be afforded very little weight where inconsistency arises, except where they have been examined and adopted against the new Framework." This is a consultation explanation of the draft, not the final test. It does not say whether the whole policy or only the inconsistent part loses weight ("where inconsistency arises" reads at least as naturally as limited to the inconsistent part), and it says nothing about heritage, design, access or spatial-preference policies. The final Annex A para 2 is explicit: "(or parts of those policies) which are materially inconsistent". Not a disagreement with A2; no change to our analysis.
- **METH — agrees.** "The case for non-statutory national policy", p.11 (PDF): "Since the current Framework was updated in December 2024, an unprecedented 80% of major residential appeals located on grey belt land have been approved". The government itself uses appeal outcome rates as evidence of how a Framework policy is applied in practice.
