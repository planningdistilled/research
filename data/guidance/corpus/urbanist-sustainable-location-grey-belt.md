## Summary

A consultancy blog post by a chartered town planner (Royal Town Planning Institute member) that analyses 13 grey belt appeal decisions from 2025. Despite the "[2026 Update]" title, it is written entirely against the December 2024 National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) paragraph 155(c) "sustainable location" test, the predecessor of the August 2026 GB7(1)(g)(iii) and TR3 tests; the last edit (12 March 2026) predates the August 2026 Framework. It argues the test is a proportionate judgement scaled to the size of the scheme, that settlement pattern comes first and service frequency second, and that route safety (lighting, footways, surfacing) is where weak cases fail. It reports a "working threshold" of about 500-550 metres to a bus stop via a safe route, while saying route quality matters as much as distance. It records that housing supply shortfalls (down to 0.91 years) did not rescue sites that failed the location test, and that passing the grey belt test did not guarantee approval (Burnham Green dismissed on character).

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Judge sustainable location proportionately: a single dwelling at a village edge does not need urban service levels; larger schemes need more (funded footways, bus vouchers).
- Assess walking routes from the perspective of the actual users (older people, parents with children, people walking in darkness or bad weather).
- Treat unlit lanes without footways, unpaved tracks and very infrequent buses (for example four a day) as grounds to fail the test.
- Accept modest lighting limitations where trip generation is negligible (single dwelling).
- Remember that passing the grey belt test does not settle character, design or the overall balance.
- (Aimed at applicants) Present route photographs, widths, lighting and gradients; offer small conditioned works (footway link, dropped kerb) to cure weak routes.

## Positions against our propositions

- **TR (qualifies)**: "Appeal evidence points to a working threshold of approximately 500 to 550 metres to a bus stop via a safe route, even with infrequent services and no Sunday provision. However, route quality matters as much as distance." (section "Walkability and public transport access"). Gives distance co-equal weight with route quality and frames a distance threshold; we say quality decides. Checked 2026-10-02: quote verbatim. A soft qualification only: the "threshold" is a pass range drawn from allowed cases (Hydestile bus stop about 550 m; Dormansland station about 0.5 miles), is conditioned on "a safe route", and every failure the post cites (Fobbing, Colton, Ainsworth) is a route-quality failure, not a distance one. Written under the December 2024 NPPF para 155(c), not TR3/GB7(1)(g)(iii). No change to our analysis; at most, note that a short safe route is a common feature of allowed cases.
- **TR (agrees, with a scale nuance)**: "Well-lit roads and pedestrian paths that enable residents to walk and cycle with reasonable confidence at all times of day are essential." and "modest lighting limitations were accepted where trip generation from a single dwelling was negligible, confirming that the test is applied proportionately" (section "Safety and comfort"). Checked adversarially: not a real qualification. The tolerated weakness is lighting only, and at Shipley Bridge the inspector noted "Paved footpaths nearby", so footway presence, not lighting, carried the case; the failures (Colton, Fobbing) were "unlit roads without footways". That fits our view that route quality decides and that lighting is secondary (it neither cures carriageway walking nor, on these cases, fails a footway route). The proportionality-to-scale point (single dwellings judged more leniently than 29 homes) is a nuance we could note, not a conflict. Written against the December 2024 NPPF para 155(c), not GB7/TR3.
- **TR (agrees)**: "Evidence must address the quality of routes and the actual pattern of daily trips, not simply the presence of a pavement on a map." (Ainsworth case study). Also Colton failed where "access is along an unlit country road with no demarcated footway".
- **SUP (agrees)**: "Get it wrong and even a 0.91-year housing supply won't save you." (section "Why sustainable location matters for Grey Belt developments?").
- **GB (agrees)**: "The inspector gave significant weight to housing delivery benefits given the acute shortfall, but found these did not clearly outweigh the Green Belt harm. Very special circumstances did not exist." (Fobbing case study). And "satisfying criterion 155(c) alone is insufficient. Character and appearance harm can still tip the planning balance against approval, even where Grey Belt policy tests are met and housing need is acute." (Burnham Green case study).
- **DIV (qualifies)**: "planning officers and committees have been applying an inappropriately urban lens to inherently rural places" (section "Villages don't need to perform like Canary Wharf"). On its face this says councils are stricter than inspectors on location, the opposite direction to our DIV finding. But it is a rhetorical, uncounted generalisation in applicant-side marketing, written against the December 2024 NPPF paragraph 155(c), and drawn only from appealed refusals (which by construction are cases where the council said no). It does not compare council and inspector findings on the same tests. It partly matches our own section F, where grey belt is "reversed" (members refuse grey belt status that inspectors then grant), and the article's own cases (Colton, the 0.91-year supply line, unlit lanes failing) show inspectors failing weak walking routes, which is what DIV says. Not a measured disagreement; no change to DIV, but worth citing as the applicant-side perception that councils over-refuse.
- **METH (agrees)**: "After analysing every Grey Belt appeal decision since December 2024, one truth emerges with absolute clarity" (section "Why sustainable location matters..."). Uses appeal decisions as the primary evidence of how the test is applied, as we do, though on 13 hand-picked cases with no outcome counting.
