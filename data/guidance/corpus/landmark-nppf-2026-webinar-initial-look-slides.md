## Summary

These are the 41 slides from a Landmark Chambers webinar of 3 September 2026. Rupert Warren KC (King's Counsel) covered weight, transition and inconsistency; Nick Grant covered S3-S6 and the Green Belt; Rebecca Sage covered the station "default yes"; and Edward-Arash Abedian covered press and industry reaction. Warren says the weights the Framework indicates should not be over-relied on. He says "materially inconsistent" in Annex A is a matter of judgement, best tested by asking whether there is "any practical significance" in the difference between the old policy and the NPPF. He expects many plans to be inconsistent with the S4/S5 approach to acceptability in principle, and he flags a likely difference between councils and the Planning Inspectorate (PINS) and Secretary of State over how much weight emerging plans get. Grant lists the 15 "should be refused" policies under S4 (including DP3(3) and HE6(5)). He describes S5 as a closed list of 10 "Tilt On" categories, with a tilt the other way outside them, and gives the Green Belt sequence: inappropriate means a tilt against and a need for very special circumstances (VSC); not inappropriate means a tilt in favour. Sage sets out the four rules for development near stations and the "reasonable walking distance" definition (around 800 metres, or about 10 minutes' walk where the quality of the route or physical barriers discourage walking).

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Split each analysis into (a) judgement against the adopted plan and (b) judgement against the NPPF.
- Treat the national decision-making policies (NDMPs) as relevant immediately. Do not use plan-making policies for decisions, though some may bear on the weight given to emerging plans.
- To decide "materially inconsistent" (Annex A), ask whether the old policy and the NPPF would in practice be applied differently on the same point.
- Within settlements (S4), check the 15 "should be refused" policies. Outside settlements (S5), check the 10 listed categories; anything outside them tilts towards refusal.
- In the Green Belt or Local Green Space, assess against the Green Belt and Local Green Space policies: if inappropriate, tilt against and require VSC; if not inappropriate, tilt in favour of approval.
- For the station route, apply the four development rules plus the "well-connected" station rule. Measure reasonable walking distance as about 800 metres, or about 10 minutes' walk where the route discourages walking. Apply the station sub-paragraphs only to the part of a site within that distance.
- Keep decisions consistent (same treatment of the same points unless clearly explained), especially on emerging plans and HO13(4) strategic-site allocations.

## Positions against our propositions

- **S5, agrees.** "Clear list of ‘Tilt On’ development (10 options)" and "Outside of that, tilt the other way (EC)" (slide 16, "S5: It's not (that) cold outside"). This matches our reading that the S5(1) category decides the case and that falling outside every category is normally fatal.
- **GB, agrees.** "If inappropriate, tilt against (need VSC (HC8, GB6))" (slide 16). Inappropriate development faces a tilt against approval and needs VSC.
- **GB, agrees.** "If not inappropriate then tilt in favour of approval" (slide 16). This is a one-line summary of the Framework's mechanics, not a view on outcomes: development that is not inappropriate moves into the S5 tilted balance. That fits our finding that passing GB7 is not approval. The tilt still gives way to the S5(2) "should be refused" triggers, and in our data 17 of 34 non-householder "not inappropriate" appeals were permitted. The slide does not say a pass means permission.
- **TR, not applicable.** "around 800 metres, or around 10 minutes’ walk time if topography, route availability and quality or physical barriers would prevent or discourage walking from up to 800 metres away" (slide 27, "Reasonable walking distance"). The slide reproduces the Framework's glossary definition without comment, and the definition applies only to the station sub-paragraphs (S5(1)(h), L3(2)(c), GB7(1)(h): "land around well-connected stations"). It is a restatement of policy for a different test, not a view on TR3 or GB7(1)(g)(iii) walking access, so it neither supports nor qualifies our "quality decides, not distance" reading. The slides say nothing on walking in the carriageway or street lighting.
- **A2, agrees.** "A way of dealing with it may be to ask whether there is any practical significance to the difference in the application of old policy x and the NPPF on the same point" (slide 8, "Inconsistency"). This is a point-by-point test, consistent with reducing the weight only of the clause that conflicts.
- **A2, qualifies.** "Many plans (and emerging plans, even) are likely to be inconsistent/materially inconsistent with the new NPPF: eg, the S4/S5 approach to acceptability in principle." (slide 11, "Practicalities"). This names the plan's spatial strategy (where development is acceptable in principle) as the likely area of inconsistency. It is a prediction, not a ruling on weight, and it fits our "cut only the offending clause" approach, since the offending clause would be the in-principle locational policy. But it cuts against our finding that spatial-preference policies usually keep full weight: Warren expects their in-principle limb often to be materially inconsistent with S4/S5. Our 13-cut / 17-kept split should be read as early evidence on a point counsel thinks is open.
- **DIV, agrees.** "Will LPAs give more weight than PINS or Sec State to them? The principle of consistency (ie same treatment of same points unless there is a clear explanation) will be important." (slide 11, "Practicalities"; "them" means emerging plans). Also "LPAs may take a different approach to this – if the early draft is their preferred option; compared perhaps to PINS and Sec State?" (slide 10). Both predict that councils and inspectors will diverge, here on the weight of emerging plans and strategic sites rather than on our axes (route quality, triggers, heritage method).
- **METH, silent (checked).** "Weight is for the decision maker – applying policy but explaining specific judgements, however. NB not to over-rely on the indicated weights" (slide 5, "Rules and weight"). This restates the ordinary rule that weight is a matter for the decision maker, and warns decision makers not to treat the Framework's indicated weights as automatic. It says nothing about counting appeal outcomes, the precedent value of appeal decisions, or empirical coding of decisions, so it is not a qualification of our method. Slide 11's "principle of consistency (ie same treatment of same points unless there is a clear explanation)" is raised about the weight of emerging plans (LPAs against PINS or the Secretary of State). At most it loosely supports looking at previous decisions on the same points; it is not a comment on method.
