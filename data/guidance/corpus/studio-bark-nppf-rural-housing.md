## Summary

An architect's marketing guide to the August 2026 National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) for rural homes. It explains the location-based route: S4 inside settlements, S5(1)(e) "limited infilling within groups of houses", and S5(3)/HO11 for isolated homes (the former paragraph 84 "country house clause"). It says design policy DP3(5) now gives "substantial weight" to outstanding sustainable design. It claims a government Connectivity Tool will give every site an "objective accessibility score", and that local plan policies with stricter tests than national policy will carry very little weight. It reads housing undersupply as a qualifying category under S5(1)(j) without the old "tilted balance".

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Decide first whether a site is inside a settlement, in a group of houses, or isolated, and apply S4, S5(1)(e) or S5(3)/HO11 to match.
- Treat "group of houses" and "limited infilling" as planning judgement informed by case law and appeal decisions.
- Give substantial weight to outstanding or innovative sustainable design (DP3(5)).
- Use the Connectivity Tool score as access evidence.
- Give very little weight to local plan policies that add stricter tests than national policy.

## Positions against our propositions

- SUP (agrees): "housing undersupply is now instead outlined in Policy S5.1(J) as a qualifying category, without the strength of the ‘tilted balance’ that it previously triggered." (FAQ, "How does the new NPPF approach housing land supply?")
- S5 (agrees): "The concept of ‘settlements’, and whether a site sits inside or outside one, becomes the central gateway test." (section 5, "A New ‘Development Presumption’ Framework")
- TR (qualifies; checked 2 Oct 2026, the disagrees coding did not hold): "Decision-makers will use a new government-published Connectivity Tool to produce an objective accessibility score for every site." and "The closer a site is to services and public transport, the higher its score will be under the new government tool." (section 4, "The New National Connectivity Tool" / Key Takeaways). This describes the Connectivity Tool the Framework introduces; it does not say the score decides TR3 or that route quality is irrelevant, and the same paragraph says that for rural and edge-of-settlement sites "public transport options and walking routes" will make car reliance "harder to argue away". The mild tension is the word "objective", which implies the score settles access; the decisions show the tool corroborates but does not decide (a score of 52 has both passed and failed; a bare score of 25 carried little weight in PINS-6010253). No change to our analysis.
- A2 (qualifies): "Local councils cannot invent stricter locational tests to block development. National policy now sets the definitive standard." (section 4 Key Takeaways); also "Any existing local plan policies that conflict with the new national rules will carry very little weight when planning decisions are made, effectively making the national rules the only ones that matter on those issues." (section 3, "National Frameworks Take Precedence to Contradictory Local Policies"). A loose, marketing-level restatement of Annex A paragraph 2 and the plan-making rule that plans should not reword national policy. Its example is a local countryside policy adding stricter tests than HO11 (former paragraph 84). It talks of whole "policies" rather than clauses and is broader in tone than our reading, but it limits the loss of weight to the conflict "on those issues" and never says that spatial-preference, heritage, design or access policies lose weight in general. Compatible with our clause-level reading rather than contrary to it; not a real disagreement (adversarial check, 2 Oct 2026).
- METH (agrees): "leaving the meaning to be a matter of planning judgement informed by case law and previous appeal decisions." (FAQ, "What does ‘limited infilling’ mean?")
