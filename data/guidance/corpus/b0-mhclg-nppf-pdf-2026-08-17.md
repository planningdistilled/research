## Summary

This is the full text of the August 2026 National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF), 130 pages, with policy codes in place of paragraph numbers. For decisions, the core is S3, which sends a proposal down one of two routes: S4 inside a "settlement" (approve unless the benefits are substantially outweighed) or S5 outside one (approve only listed categories; anything else only in exceptional circumstances). Which route applies depends on the Annex B definition of "settlement". That definition counts areas the development plan defines as settlements, and it excludes villages washed over by the Green Belt. Green Belt proposals go through GB6 to GB8 instead of S5. Annex A para 2 cuts the weight of plan policies "or parts of those policies" that conflict with the national decision-making policies. Annex A para 3 limits when a gap between local housing need and the plan's housing requirement counts as "unmet need" for S5(1)(j). The file is byte-identical to the asset linked after the 29 September update (see `b0-mhclg-nppf-pdf-2026-09-29`).

## What it tells officers/decision-makers to do

- Decide first whether the site is inside or outside a "settlement" under Annex B, then apply S4 or S5 (S3(1)); split mixed sites (S3(2)).
- Treat development-plan settlement boundaries "or equivalent terms" as defining settlements; exclude hamlets unless the plan names them; exclude villages that lie within and are defined as part of the Green Belt (Annex B).
- Outside settlements, find the S5(1) category first; if none applies, approve only "in exceptional circumstances" where benefits "substantially outweigh" harm, including harm to countryside character and to sustainable patterns of movement (S5(4)).
- Under S5(1)(j), a housing shortfall (no five-year supply, or a Housing Delivery Test (HDT) result below 75%) counts as unmet need. The site must still be "physically well-related to an existing settlement" and of a scale local infrastructure can take. If the land supply and HDT are both healthy, a local housing need figure above the plan requirement is not unmet need for five years after the plan is adopted (Annex A para 3).
- In the Green Belt, do not apply S5; apply GB6 to GB8. Where a proposal is not inappropriate, approve unless substantially outweighed (S5(5)). Inappropriate development needs very special circumstances (VSC), with "substantial weight" on Green Belt harm (GB6(2)).
- For grey belt (GB7(1)(g)), all four tests must be met, including a "sustainable location, with particular reference to policy TR3", and the Golden Rules (GB8) for major housing.
- Use the Connectivity Tool alongside other quantitative or qualitative evidence to judge connectivity (TR3(2)). "Reasonable walking distance" (for station-led policies) is about 800 metres, or about 10 minutes where route quality or physical barriers discourage walking (Annex B).
- Give "very limited weight" to plan policies, or parts of them, that are materially inconsistent with the national decision-making policies. Do not reduce the weight of other policies just because they predate this Framework (Annex A para 2).

## Positions against our propositions

- **SH1, agrees.** Annex B defines settlement by built-up form and by the development plan's own definitions, not by any local hierarchy tier. A local tier therefore matters only if the plan uses it to define a settlement or a boundary, which is our route (b). Quote: "This includes areas defined as a settlement in the development plan (whether using defined settlement boundaries or equivalent terms, or criteria for identifying settlement extents where boundaries have yet to be defined)." (Annex B, Settlement, PDF pp. 114-115.) S2(1)(a) likewise asks plans to identify "Settlements within the development plan area (applying the definition in the glossary at Annex B)" (Chapter 4, S2).
- **SH2, agrees.** Quote: "For the purpose of this Framework they also exclude villages which lie within and are defined as part of the Green Belt in the development plan." (Annex B, Settlement, PDF p. 115.) GB4(1)(b) adds for plan-making: "Such villages should not be identified as ‘settlements’ for the purpose of the spatial strategy in the development plan" (Chapter 13, GB4, PDF p. 62).
- **SH2, qualifies.** For a washed-over village the route is the Green Belt policies, not the S5 list. S5 does not apply at all, though its paragraph 2 balance is borrowed once a proposal is found not inappropriate. Quote: "This policy does not apply to development proposals in the Green Belt or on land designated as Local Green Space, which should instead be determined in accordance with policies HC8, GB6, GB7 and/or GB8 (as appropriate)." (Chapter 4, S5(5), PDF p. 29.)
- **S5, agrees.** Quote: "Development proposals which do not fall within one of the categories set out in this policy should only be approved in exceptional circumstances, where the benefits of the proposal would substantially outweigh the adverse effects, including to the character of the countryside and in relation to promoting sustainable patterns of movement." (Chapter 4, S5(4), PDF p. 28.) The text gives no gloss on S5(1)(j)(i) "physically well-related" beyond footnote 28 (sites beyond the edge of an unbuilt allocation), which leaves room for the competing readings we describe.
- **SUP, agrees.** Shortfall is only one limb of S5(1)(j): the site must also be "physically well-related to an existing settlement" and of a scale infrastructure can take, and the S5(1) balance still applies. Quote: "Be physically well-related to an existing settlement28 (unless the nature of the development would make this inappropriate) and be of a scale which can be accommodated taking into account the existing or proposed availability of infrastructure" (Chapter 4, S5(1)(j)(i), PDF p. 28.)
- **SUP, silent (adds a related gate rule; checked 2 Oct 2026, downgraded from "qualifies").** Annex A para 3 is policy text, not a view on our proposition, and it concerns a different trigger from a supply shortfall: it applies only where the authority *can* show a five-year supply and the Housing Delivery Test (HDT) is above 75%, and then says a standard-method local housing need figure above the adopted plan requirement is not evidence of unmet need for S5(1)(j) for five years from plan adoption. That is consistent with SUP (no shortfall, so no gate) rather than a qualification of it. patterns.md already notes there is no express appeal ruling on Annex A para 3. Quote: "this should not be considered as evidence of unmet need, for a period of five years from the date of the plan’s adoption." (Annex A para 3, PDF p. 101.)
- **GB, agrees.** S5(5) is the Framework's own route after GB7, and it matches our finding: a proposal that is not inappropriate gets the S5 "approve unless substantially outweighed" balance, but that balance is applied "applying paragraph 2 of this policy", so the S5(2) refusal triggers (heritage, flooding, design and the rest) can still defeat it. Passing GB7 brings a presumption, not approval, which is how the appeals divide (patterns.md, finding 18). Quote: "However, where development would not be inappropriate in these locations (through the application of policies HC8 and GB7), proposals should be approved unless the benefits of doing so would be substantially outweighed by any adverse effects, when assessed against the national decision-making policies in this Framework, and applying paragraph 2 of this policy." (Chapter 4, S5(5), PDF p. 29.) This is policy text, not commentary on our method.
- **TR, silent (context only; adversarial check 2 Oct 2026).** The Framework sets no distance for TR3 or GB7(1)(g)(iii); GB7(1)(g)(iii) asks only that the site be "in a sustainable location, with particular reference to policy TR3" (Chapter 13, GB7(1)(g)(iii)). The Annex B "reasonable walking distance" (about 800 metres, or about 10 minutes' walk where route quality or barriers discourage walking) is a different test: it is limited to "policies S5, L3, GB7 (relating to land around well-connected stations)", i.e. S5(1)(h), L3(2)(c) and GB7(1)(h), not TR3 or GB7(1)(g)(iii). So it does not contradict us; if anything it shows the Framework itself lets route quality displace a plain distance figure. Quote: "this should be considered to be around 800 metres, or around 10 minutes’ walk time if topography, route availability and quality or physical barriers would prevent or discourage walking from up to 800 metres away" (Annex B, Reasonable walking distance, PDF p. 113). One caveat to state in our write-up, not a disagreement: TR3(2) names a quantitative measure, "The Connectivity Tool ... should be used alongside other relevant quantitative or qualitative evidence in assessing the connectivity of particular locations" (Chapter 9, TR3(2), PDF p. 72), so route-quality evidence sits beside Connectivity Tool scores rather than replacing them. The Framework says nothing on which carries more weight; that is what the appeal evidence answers.
- **A2, agrees.** Quote: "Development plan policies (or parts of those policies) which are materially inconsistent with national decision-making policies in this Framework should be given very limited weight." and "Other development plan policies67 should not be given reduced weight simply because they were adopted prior to the publication of this Framework." (Annex A para 2, PDF p. 101.) It adds one exception we do not state: policies "examined and adopted66 or made against this Framework" keep full weight even if inconsistent.

## Leads

- Connectivity Tool (GOV.UK), now named in policy at TR1, TR3(2), L3 (densities) and Annex B. Worth checking how inspectors use its scores against route-quality evidence.
- Manual for Streets and the Design and Placemaking PPG (footnote 46 to TR4), the national standard for footways and pedestrian conflict.
- Annex E (Green Belt assessments) for how grey belt is to be assessed in plan-making.
- Annex D (Housing calculations and supply) for the five-year supply and HDT rules behind S5(1)(j) and GB7 footnote 41.
- GB4(1)(c): conservation area designation, not Green Belt, is the right tool where a village's character needs protecting "for other reasons".
