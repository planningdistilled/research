# Published guidance against our method

How 315 published sources on applying the August 2026 National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) compare with the nine propositions we drew from our decisions database. **Snapshot: 2 October 2026.** Corpus and method: [`../README.md`](../README.md). Our propositions and evidence: [`../../nppf-2026-decisions/analysis/patterns.md`](../../nppf-2026-decisions/analysis/patterns.md) (971 coded decisions; the patterns file was written on the first 794).

Every claim below cites a corpus slug in `corpus/<slug>.md`. Quotes are verbatim from the corpus files or the local copies in `data/open-sources/guidance-ogl/` (MHCLG and Planning Inspectorate pages) and the private sources repo's `guidance/` (other publishers). Counts are numbers of sources after adversarial verification (see the README for how that worked).

---

## 1. Headline

**Published guidance broadly supports our propositions. Nobody has published a reasoned, evidence-based case against any of them.** Out of about 211 claimed disagreements and qualifications that readers coded, only 21 survived an adversarial check, and only 3 of those are outright disagreements, all on one proposition (A2). Most of the rest of the guidance either restates the Framework in a way that fits our reading, or is about the December 2025 draft, or is about a different test.

Two things stand out. First, **almost no-one else is reading decisions.** The guidance is overwhelmingly a reading of the policy text; only a handful of sources (Planning Geek's appeal notes, Burges Salmon's "one month on", GapSense via The Intermediary, Urbanist Architecture's digests) look at how inspectors actually apply it. Second, **the one live area of dispute is A2**: leading planning silks (Landmark Chambers) expect Annex A paragraph 2 to bite on plans' spatial and settlement-boundary policies more widely than our early appeal evidence shows.

### SH1: local hierarchy tiers carry no standalone weight

**Agrees 17, qualifies 0, disagrees 0.** Every source that discusses the S4/S5 route says it turns on the Annex B definition of "settlement", which the development plan feeds through boundaries or criteria. None gives a local tier ("Local Service Village" and the like) weight of its own. Most do not mention tiers at all, so this is agreement by silence on the tier point.

- Annex B: "This includes areas defined as a settlement in the development plan (whether using defined settlement boundaries or equivalent terms, or criteria for identifying settlement extents" (`b0-mhclg-nppf-annex-b-glossary`).
- "The Framework introduces a national definition of a settlement for the first time, important because the Policy S4 and S5 presumptions turn on whether development is within or outside one." (`mondaq-hugh-james-new-nppf-what-you-need-to-know`)
- A council complaining about exactly this: national settlement policy "provides no consideration of the size or sustainability of settlements" (`south-oxfordshire-nppf-consultation-response`, on the draft).

Watch item: the government "will consider whether additional planning guidance would be helpful to assist the identification of settlements in plans" (`mhclg-nppf-consultation-government-response`, p.31).

### SH2: a washed-over Green Belt village is not an NPPF settlement

**Agrees 17, qualifies 1, disagrees 0.** Wide agreement, including from barristers, consultancies and practitioner tools. The one confirmed qualification is the Framework's own wording point (see section 2.9).

- Annex B: "For the purpose of this Framework they also exclude villages which lie within and are defined as part of the Green Belt in the development plan." (`b0-mhclg-nppf-pdf-2026-08-17`)
- "villages in the green belt are excluded from the definition" (Charles Banner KC, `hwgpnfy-s20e1-youtube`, auto-captions).
- "A village lying within the Green Belt is not a settlement for S4 purposes, so the S4 default yes is unavailable however built-up the frontage looks." (`livedin-nppf-2026-policy-test`)

Against the grain, but not as a reasoned view: the Local Government Association (LGA) briefing for councillors says "Villages (as opposed to hamlets) should not be considered Green Belt if they are identified as a settlement. These terms remain undefined." (`b4-lga-nppf-august-2026-changes-briefing`). That reverses GB4(1)(b) and does not engage with Annex B; it is a briefing error, coded silent (section 3). The Society of Local Council Clerks (SLCC) frames the test as inside or outside "the built-up area" without mentioning the exclusion (`slcc-new-nppf`).

### TR: walking-route quality decides, not distance

**Agrees 21, qualifies 3, disagrees 0.** Twelve more were recoded "not applicable": nearly all the claimed qualifications quoted the Annex B "reasonable walking distance" definition (around 800 metres), which governs only the station policies (S5(1)(h), L3, GB7(1)(h)), not TR3 or GB7(1)(g)(iii). Read properly, even that definition supports us, because route quality switches the test from distance to walking time.

- "Coopers Hill Road and Kings Cross Lane did not provide continuous footways or street lighting. Pedestrians and cyclists would have to share the carriageway with motor traffic." (`planninggeek-south-nutfield-grey-belt-gb7`, appeal 6009966)
- Reasonable walking distance "should not only be based on distance – e.g. within 800m but must also take account" "of the quality of such a route and its perceived level of safety and convenience." (`ciht-nppf-2026-consultation-response`, the Chartered Institution of Highways & Transportation (CIHT), on the draft)
- "A timetable and an 800-metre catchment do not prove that a site is genuinely accessible." (`urbanist-architecture-nppf-2026-in-practice`)

On the other side, softly: "Appeal evidence points to a working threshold of approximately 500 to 550 metres to a bus stop via a safe route, even with infrequent services and no Sunday provision. However, route quality matters as much as distance." (`urbanist-sustainable-location-grey-belt`, written under the December 2024 NPPF). And a land promoter: "Distance to a settlement and to everyday services is the practical test of sustainability." (`logical-planning-nppf-2026-explained`; no cases cited). Two sources (`studio-bark-nppf-rural-housing`, `wotton-donoghue-new-2026-nppf`) read TR3 through the Connectivity Tool score, which is proximity-based; neither discusses route quality.

Nobody addresses our narrower claim that **street lighting does not cure walking in the carriageway**. Sources that list lighting (CIHT, Planning Geek) list it alongside footways, not instead of them.

### S5: the S5(1) category is the whole case

**Agrees 60, qualifies 9, disagrees 0.** This is the most widely shared reading in the corpus: S4 inside settlements, a closed S5(1) list outside, and a "reverse presumption" or "default no" under S5(4). The qualifications cluster on two points: Aston Clinton as an S5(4) pass, and what "evidenced unmet need" in S5(1)(j) covers.

- "Where an out-of-settlement development does not fall within one of those categories, it should only be approved in exceptional circumstances." (`sharpe-pritchard-nppf-2026-key-planning-reforms`)
- "This is not a general green light for countryside development. ... proposals outside the listed categories require exceptional circumstances" (`planning-by-design-new-nppf-2026-major-changes`)
- Against: "applying policy S5(4), exceptional circumstances justified approval of the proposal despite it not falling within any of the S5(1) categories" (`landmark-appeal-s5-ho13-aston-clinton`); and "It can be any type of unmet need, notionally; how far this can be taken is something that will be played out in appeal decisions." (Killian Garvey KC, `propertyweek-revised-nppf-emphasis-on-national`)

The "competing readings" limb is well supported. Cornerstone Barristers list "physically well-related" and "evidenced unmet need" among five likely "battlegrounds" (`cornerstone-revised-nppf-rules-based-planning`), Livedin calls it the "main battleground" (`livedin-nppf-2026-terminology`), and Historic England warned the terms are vague and will be read inconsistently (`historic-england-response-nppf-reforms-feb26`).

### SUP: supply opens the gate but never decides

**Agrees 53, qualifies 4, disagrees 0.** Five campaign or lobby sources predicted that a shortfall would effectively decide cases (Campaign to Protect Rural England (CPRE) Hertfordshire, Dorset CPRE, County Councils Network (CCN), Community Planning Alliance, CIHT). All five were about the draft, cited no decisions, and were recoded on checking. Practitioners writing after publication say the opposite.

- "A five year supply shortfall therefore gives you a potentially powerful planning argument. It does not make landscape harm, flooding, unsafe access or a badly designed scheme disappear." (`planninggeek-build-outside-settlement-boundary`)
- "A HDT result below 75% does not make every local policy out of date or establish that a particular site is suitable." (`urbanist-architecture-nppf-2026-in-practice`; HDT is the Housing Delivery Test)
- Closest to a contrary view: on counsel's account of Aston Clinton, the benefits "(principally addressing the significant HLS shortfall) would \"substantially outweigh\" the adverse effects" (`landmark-appeal-s5-ho13-aston-clinton`). Recoded "qualifies": the decision also rested on 25% affordable housing and an emerging allocation.

Several sources agree that **supply is not the only gate**: affordable-housing need opened S5(1)(j) at Kingston Bagpuize with a five-year supply (`landmark-placemaking-slides`, `simonicity-evidenced-unmet-need`, `planoraks-nppf2026-welcome-to-the-future`). Our full finding already says this (PINS-6005809 ¶57), but our one-line SUP summary does not.

### GB: grey belt usually won at a village edge; no VSC from supply; passing GB7 is not approval

**Agrees 54, qualifies 2, disagrees 0.** "Grey belt is not approval" is close to universal, including in the government's own guidance. Several readers coded the S5(5) "tilt in favour" after GB7 as cutting against us; every one of those was recoded "agrees", because a tilt is not a grant and our pattern 18 already applies the S5(5) balance.

- "Where a development is not inappropriate in the Green Belt, this does not itself remove the land from the Green Belt nor require development proposals to be approved." (`mhclg-ppg-green-belt`, paragraph 010)
- "Among appeals where land was found to be grey belt, schemes that passed the sustainable location test succeeded 72% of the time. Those that failed it managed just 5%." (`intermediary-grey-belt-changing-green-belt-appeals`, on the December 2024 Framework)
- Qualifying: at West Kingsdown "The Inspector therefore applied some flexibility because this was a traveller proposal rather than general housing." (`planninggeek-sevenoaks-grey-belt-appeal`). And Freeths' shorthand "planning permission should be granted without the need to demonstrate very special circumstances" (`freeths-consultation-to-publication-what-changed`), recoded "qualifies" as loose wording rather than a contrary reading.

Annex E and the Green Belt Planning Practice Guidance (PPG) both say villages are not "large built-up areas", which underpins the village-edge finding (`mhclg-nppf-annex-e-green-belt-assessments`, `mhclg-ppg-green-belt`). Nobody has published a case where housing supply produced very special circumstances (VSC) once a scheme was inappropriate; the South Nutfield note reports the opposite (`planninggeek-south-nutfield-grey-belt-gb7`).

### A2: Annex A paragraph 2 cuts only the offending clause

**Agrees 28, qualifies 10, disagrees 3.** This is the only proposition with confirmed disagreement, and it is about spatial and settlement-boundary policies, not heritage or design. On the clause-level mechanism, published guidance agrees with us; on whether spatial-restraint policies usually keep their weight, informed opinion leans the other way.

- Annex A: "Development plan policies (or parts of those policies) which are materially inconsistent with national decision-making policies in this Framework should be given very limited weight" (`b0-mhclg-nppf-annex-a-implementation`)
- "Age alone is not enough. ... The key question will be whether there is a material inconsistency, assessed policy by policy and in the circumstances of the individual proposal." (`planning-by-design-new-nppf-2026-major-changes`)
- Against: "this provision will be likely to override all other settlement-based constraint policies in existing LPs: because of Annex A and fact that be inconsistent." (Reed KC, `landmark-placemaking-slides`); "Many plans (and emerging plans, even) are likely to be inconsistent/materially inconsistent with the new NPPF: eg, the S4/S5 approach to acceptability in principle." (Rupert Warren KC, `landmark-nppf-2026-webinar-initial-look-slides`); "Most restrictive rural housing policies adopted under earlier editions sit here." (`livedin-nppf-2026-terminology`)

More than a dozen sources that paraphrase Annex A loosely ("local plans which conflict", "that plan now carries only very limited weight") were checked and recoded silent: shorthand, not a reasoned whole-policy reading. Most draft-era sources quote the draft wording ("in any way inconsistent"), which the final text narrowed.

### DIV: councils pass what inspectors fail

**Agrees 4, qualifies 2, disagrees 0.** Almost no-one has tested this. The sources that touch it either predict inconsistent application or run in the "reverse" direction we already record for grey belt.

- S5 "will be difficult for applicants and decision-makers to interpret, and therefore for it to be applied consistently and effectively." (`historic-england-response-nppf-reforms-feb26`)
- "Will LPAs give more weight than PINS or Sec State to them? The principle of consistency ... will be important." (`landmark-nppf-2026-webinar-initial-look-slides`; LPA is local planning authority, PINS the Planning Inspectorate)
- Against the direction of DIV: "planning officers and committees have been applying an inappropriately urban lens to inherently rural places" and grey belt refusals "have subsequently been allowed by the Planning Inspectorate" (`urbanist-sustainable-location-grey-belt`, `urbanist-architecture-nppf-2026-in-practice`). Both fit our "reversed" grey belt row; neither compares findings on the same test.

Rural lobby groups expect the opposite of DIV (councils too restrictive), but as predictions with no evidence (`waypoint-planning-nppf-rural-landowners`, `nfu-nppf-refresh-food-production`, `fwi-new-planning-framework-farmers-rural-businesses`), all recoded silent.

### METH: decision letters as evidence; coded findings; outcome rates

**Agrees 33, qualifies 4, disagrees 0.** Nobody argues against reading decisions as evidence of how the Framework is applied; the government itself cited appeal outcome rates (80% of major residential grey belt appeals allowed) as evidence its policy works (`mhclg-nppf-consultation-december-2025`). The confirmed qualifications are all about **reading outcome rates**: selection, procedure, allocation and small numbers.

- "Planning policy has to be understood at the level of individual decisions, not ministerial announcements." (`intermediary-grey-belt-changing-green-belt-appeals`)
- "why headline rates of allowed appeals don’t tell the full story" (`pins-blog-appeal-decision-written-in-the-stars`)
- "This may produce apparent patterns of outcome that are in fact artefacts of the way work is allocated and do not provide evidence as to an Inspector’s fitness for or approach to a particular case" (`pins-casework-database`)

The "not binding precedent" caveat appears often (`planninggeek-aston-clinton-appeal-settlement-test`, `urbanist-architecture-nppf-2026-in-practice`, `planninggeek-croxley-green-appeal`), always alongside "useful indication" language; it fits our method, which never claims precedent. Separately, PINS now requires a declaration where artificial intelligence (AI) was used to produce "a summary or analysis" in material sent to an inspector (`pins-ai-in-casework-evidence`). That is a compliance step for anything we submit, not a challenge to the method.

## 2. Verified disagreements and qualifications, ranked

Only the 21 codings that survived adversarial checking are treated as real. They reduce to nine issues.

### 2.1 Medium: spatial and settlement-boundary policies under Annex A paragraph 2 (A2)

> **Resolved 2 Oct 2026: we changed the finding.** A recount of the 96 appeal letters that weigh a plan policy against the Framework (`../../nppf-2026-decisions/analysis/appeals-review/issue-3-a2-recode.tsv`) found spatial restrictions cut in 30 of 45 letters (16 for a named S4/S5 conflict, 13 for supply), against 38 of 39 heritage, design, amenity and access policies kept. The silks are right about settlement-boundary policies; we were right about heritage and design. A2 now reads as `patterns.md` item 9. The recommendation below is kept as the record of what the review proposed.

**Who:** Reed KC (`landmark-placemaking-slides`, disagrees); Rupert Warren KC (`landmark-nppf-2026-webinar-initial-look-slides`, qualifies); Livedin (`livedin-nppf-2026-terminology`, disagrees; `livedin-nppf-2026-policy-test`, qualifies); Miller Homes' planning witness at Colwell Farm (`mid-sussex-colwell-farm-appellant-nppf-2026-id32`, disagrees); Cornerstone (`cornerstone-revised-nppf-rules-based-planning-web`, qualifies).

**What they say.** Settlement-based constraint and countryside policies in older plans are, or are likely to be, "materially inconsistent" with the S4/S5 approach to acceptability in principle, so they get very limited weight. Reed predicts S5(1)(j) will "override all other settlement-based constraint policies". The Colwell Farm witness goes further and cuts heritage policies: "Consequently, DP34 should attract very limited weight as this is the effect of Annex A," (para 4.45). Cornerstone adds that "very limited weight" is itself a default the decision-maker can depart from.

**What we say.** Annex A paragraph 2 cuts only the offending clause; spatial-preference, heritage, design and access policies keep full weight. Our evidence is about 13 cut against about 17 kept, and only 6 of the 13 cuts led to an allowed appeal (patterns.md item 9).

**Recommendation: change our wording and add a caveat; do not change the finding.** Reasons:
- Warren KC and Reed KC advised on or teach the Framework, and our evidence is six weeks of mostly written-representations appeals. Our split (13 to 17) is not lopsided enough to say "full weight" flatly for spatial policies.
- Reword A2 to: "Annex A ¶2 cuts only the offending clause. A blanket bar on development outside settlement boundaries that would refuse what S5(1) or GB7 permits is the typical offending clause. Heritage, design and access policies keep full weight; spatial-preference policies usually keep weight in our sample."
- Re-code the 13 "cut" cases by basis: (a) cut for under-delivery (2024-style "out of date" reasoning, as at Aston Clinton ¶38 to 39) versus (b) cut because the in-principle limb conflicts with S4/S5. Only (b) tests the silks' prediction.
- On heritage, rebut: no source other than a partisan inquiry witness argues heritage policies lose weight, and our kept cases include heritage (PINS-6007541). Watch the Colwell Farm decision (appeal 6005786) for whether a whole-policy heritage cut is accepted.

### 2.2 Low: S5(4) is "almost always fatal", but the count is stale (S5, and SUP)

> **Resolved 2 Oct 2026.** Recounted at 37 fail / 3 pass at appeal (Cople 6011253, Honeysuckle Bottom 6011694, Aston Clinton 6008253). `patterns.md` items 1 and 2, `principle-and-balance.md` P1 and `DISTILLATION-GUIDE.md` updated; SUP now reads "rarely decides the case on its own".

**Who:** `landmark-appeal-s5-ho13-aston-clinton`, `planninggeek-aston-clinton-appeal-settlement-test` (both qualify S5).

**What they say.** At Aston Clinton (PINS-6008253, 66 homes, allowed 24 September 2026) the site failed S5(1)(j) on character and setting harm but was allowed under S5(4): "proximity and good access do not automatically make a site physically well related, and failure under S5(1)(j) does not necessarily mean refusal." (`planninggeek-aston-clinton-appeal-settlement-test`)

**What we say.** S5(4) is almost always fatal: "n 27 fail / 2 pass", and "Both S5(4) passes turned on the *absence* of transport harm".

**Recommendation: change our analysis (statistics only).** Our own case file already codes PINS-6008253 as an S5(4) pass, but patterns.md item 2 was not updated. Recount (probably 3 passes), add Aston Clinton as a counter-example, and reword the "both turned on absence of transport harm" line: Aston Clinton turned on a substantial shortfall, 25% affordable housing and the council's own emerging allocation. "Almost always" still holds at about 27 to 3. Also soften SUP's one-line form from "never decides" to "rarely decides on its own", citing Aston Clinton, where supply was the main benefit tipping an S5(4) balance.

### 2.3 Low: what counts as "evidenced unmet need" in S5(1)(j) (S5, SUP)

**Who:** Cornerstone (`cornerstone-revised-nppf-rules-based-planning`, `...-web`); Garvey KC (`propertyweek-revised-nppf-emphasis-on-national`).

**What they say.** Unlike GB7, S5(1)(j) "is expressly not limited to these circumstances. Does that mean that an evidenced unmet need for, say, affordable housing or older/disabled persons housing would be sufficient to bring a residential proposal which included such housing into the countryside category?" (Cornerstone). Garvey says it can be "any type of unmet need, notionally".

**What we say.** Our S5 proposition puts the competing readings on "physically well-related"; our SUP and policy-codes notes treat a shortfall or HDT below 75% as making need automatic, and say nothing on other needs.

**Recommendation: add a caveat.** Record a second contested element inside S5(1)(j): whether need for one housing type (affordable, older persons) can open the category where five-year supply is met, and for how much of a scheme. Note Kingston Bagpuize (PINS-6005809) took the wider reading and Baroness Taylor's Lords answer of 15 September pointed to the narrow one (`simonicity-evidenced-unmet-need`). Search the 971 decisions for S5(1)(j) passes on non-supply need.

### 2.4 Low: the permissive reading of "physically well-related" (S5)

**Who:** `mid-sussex-colwell-farm-appellant-nppf-2026-id32`; `fwi-new-planning-framework-farmers-rural-businesses`.

**What they say.** Adjoining the boundary is enough: "S5(1)(j) does not include any express requirements itself in respect to accessibility, beyond" being physically well-related (Colwell Farm, para 4.9). A rural consultant suggests "“Within” can also mean immediately adjacent to a settlement" (Farmers Weekly, on the draft).

**What we say.** There are four competing readings; inspectors mostly read settlement form and severance, and some read accessibility in.

**Recommendation: no change; cite as the promoter reading.** This is advocacy, and it is the same reading councils use (13 of 13 S5(1)(j) passes) and inspectors mostly reject (patterns.md section F). Add Colwell Farm to item 3 as the permissive reading argued at inquiry and watch its outcome.

### 2.5 Low: outcome rates mix procedure, case mix and allocation (METH)

**Who:** `pins-blog-appeal-decision-written-in-the-stars`, `pins-casework-database`, `planoraks-basics-19-how-many-appeals-win`, `intermediary-grey-belt-changing-green-belt-appeals` (two codings).

**What they say.** Allowed rates for 2025/26 section 78 appeals were 30% by written representations, 53% at hearings and 72% at inquiries, because complex cases go to inquiry. "That isn’t because choosing an inquiry wins appeals; stronger cases reach inquiry in the first place." (GapSense). "A headline claiming three-quarters of major grey belt schemes succeed can be perfectly accurate and still badly mislead". Small numbers per inspector or area give misleading 100% rates (PINS casework database). Inspectors "are, in the end, human beings ... Not algorithms." (Planoraks)

**What we say.** We derive outcome rates and decision routes; patterns.md already says raw permit rates "measure selection, not leniency".

**Recommendation: add a caveat and stratify.** Carry the selection caveat down to route-level and subgroup rates, including the Navigator. Where a sample allows, split by procedure (written representations, hearing, inquiry) and scale (major, minor); always show the denominator; flag per-authority figures from small samples (for example Waverley 4 of 4); and describe route rates as associations, not forecasts for a single appeal.

### 2.6 Low: traveller sites get locational flexibility in GB7 (GB)

**Who:** `planninggeek-sevenoaks-grey-belt-appeal` (enforcement appeal 3378336).

**What they say.** Ordinary housing at a remote West Kingsdown site would fail GB7's sustainable-location limb, but HO12 let the inspector apply flexibility to a traveller pitch (temporary, personal permission).

**Recommendation: add a caveat** to patterns.md items 15 and 18: traveller sites under HO12 get locational flexibility in GB7, so "village edge" and the location limb apply with less force to them. Add 3378336 to the decisions database.

### 2.7 Low: distance treated as co-equal with route quality (TR)

**Who:** `urbanist-sustainable-location-grey-belt`.

**What they say.** A "working threshold" of about 500 to 550 metres to a bus stop "via a safe route", and "route quality matters as much as distance".

**Recommendation: rebut, with a small addition.** The threshold is a pass range taken from allowed cases, it is conditional on a safe route, and every failure the source cites (Fobbing, Colton, Ainsworth) is a route-quality failure. It is also written under the December 2024 Framework. Add one line to TR: in allowed cases a short, safe walk to a bus stop is common, and inspectors scale expectations to scheme size for lighting-only deficits.

### 2.8 Low: S5(5) wording for Green Belt sites (SH2)

**Who:** the Framework itself (`b0-mhclg-nppf-pdf-2026-08-17`, S5(5)): S5 "does not apply to development proposals in the Green Belt or on land designated as Local Green Space, which should instead be determined in accordance with policies HC8, GB6, GB7 and/or GB8 (as appropriate)."

**Recommendation: change our label.** "S5/Green Belt, not S4" is loose: the S5(1) list and S5(4) never apply to Green Belt land. Say "the Green Belt route (GB6 to GB8, then the S5(5) balance only once a proposal is found not inappropriate), not S4". `settlement-hierarchy-and-service-centres.md` already uses the right wording; the proposition label and any shared notes need tightening. An AI planning skill made exactly this compression error and produced a false finding (`github-uk-planning-skills-issue-62`).

### 2.9 Low: other S5 qualifications

`ihbc-ccn-responds-new-nppf` and `lgl-default-yes-homes-near-stations` carry the County Councils Network's prediction that councils will be "unable to refuse development in locations that are not connected to existing settlements". Recoded "qualifies (low weight)": a publication-day forecast that names no policy. **Rebut:** our appeal data shows the opposite outside the S5(1) categories; keep it as a forecast to test.

### Reader errors (not real disagreements)

About 190 claimed disagreements or qualifications were recoded on checking. The recurring errors, worth knowing because other readers of the Framework make them too:

- **Station distance mistaken for TR3.** The Annex B "around 800 metres" definition applies only to S5(1)(h), L3 and GB7(1)(h). Readers repeatedly took it as distance governing all walking access (Lichfields, Pegasus, Savills, Strutt & Parker, Burges Salmon, Freeths, Simonicity, Cornerstone, the Home Builders Federation (HBF) and others).
- **Draft text treated as final.** The draft Annex A said "in any way inconsistent"; the draft settlement definition had no Green Belt village exclusion; the draft S5(1)(j) said "well related" without "physically". Gedling, East Hampshire, King's Lynn, New Forest, Battle Town Council, Turley, Irwin Mitchell and the December 2025 MHCLG documents were all recoded on this ground.
- **Policy text coded as a stance.** The Framework and MHCLG pages restate policy; they cannot disagree with an empirical finding about appeals.
- **"Tilt in favour" read as approval.** S5(5) after GB7 is a presumption that S5(2) triggers can still defeat (Landmark, Simonicity, MHCLG chapter 4, Freeths).
- **Predictions read as findings.** Campaign and lobby forecasts (CPRE branches, CCN, Community Planning Alliance, Just Space, Civic Voice, NFU, Waypoint) cite no decisions.

## 3. How councils are training officers and members

### What exists

The corpus holds 37 training or briefing sources. Of the 12 council documents, 10 are member briefings or committee reports, 1 is an inquiry note to an inspector (`three-rivers-lpa-response-nppf-2026-oxhey-lane`) and 1 was not retrieved (`broads-authority-committee-nppf-consultation-bng`). **Only two council documents found were written after 17 August 2026**, and only one of them (Three Rivers) applies the final tests.

**Official training is thin.**
- The Chief Planner's August newsletter, the official briefing to every Chief Planning Officer, says only: "I would encourage authorities to familiarise themselves with the new Framework" (`mhclg-chief-planner-newsletter-aug-2026`). Nothing on Annex B, S4/S5, TR3, grey belt or supply.
- The PPG had not been updated for the 2026 codes by 2 October. The Green Belt PPG still cites 2024 paragraph numbers (`mhclg-ppg-collection`, `mhclg-ppg-green-belt`). Officers are applying new policy codes with guidance keyed to the old ones.
- The Planning Advisory Service (PAS) ran roadshows and deep dives on the **draft** in January and February 2026 (`b4-pas-2026-events`). Autumn events on the final text, including a November deep dive on development inside and outside settlement boundaries, appear only in snippets and are unverified (`b6-pas-welcome-planning-advisory-service`).
- The Local Government Association (LGA) briefing is the main written guide for councillors on the final text (`b4-lga-nppf-august-2026-changes-briefing`). It misstates GB4 (above) and drops "or parts of those policies" from Annex A paragraph 2.
- Inspectors' training: FOI releases show the Inspector Training Manual (ITM) was updated about eight weeks after the December 2024 Framework ("Consolidated Inspector Training Manual on 10 February 2025", `b4-wdtk-inspector-training-manual-5`). No 2026 version is public.

**Commercial and legal training fills the gap.** Free chambers webinars aimed partly at council lawyers and officers (Landmark, Kings Chambers, Cornerstone) and paid continuing professional development (CPD) courses (`mbl-nppf-2026-complete-overhaul-webinar`, which still uses consultation-stage wording). Parish councils get one-hour sessions resold through county associations (`worcscalc-new-nppf-what-councils-need-to-know`) and SLCC webinars (`slcc-new-nppf`). Planning Jungle's officer service is built around collections of appeal decisions (`planning-jungle-nppf-august-2026-update`), which suggests officers learn application from appeals much as we do.

### What the training tells officers and members, test by test

**Settlement definition (SH1, SH2).** No council training document explains Annex B or the washed-over village exclusion. St Albans, a Green Belt authority, briefed members on the draft without mentioning settlements, washed-over villages or grey belt, and said the changes "will take a considerable period of time to comprehend them all" (`st-albans-ppc-briefing-draft-nppf-2026-01`). The LGA briefing gets GB4 backwards; SLCC teaches parish councils to ask whether a site is "inside or outside the built-up area" (`slcc-new-nppf`); Battle Town Council's handout says development "within settlement boundaries permitted in most circumstances" (`battle-tc-how-planning-system-is-changing-handout`). Read literally, each would treat a built-up washed-over village as "inside". **Divergence from inspectors:** our database has an inspector applying the exclusion squarely (PINS-6009919 ¶49, Fobbing) and none disagreeing with it.

**S4/S5 (S5).** Lancaster's report told members the draft gives "a more consistent presumption in favour of sustainable development within settlements, and outside settlements on sites that are well-located" (`lancaster-cabinet-report-nppf-feb-2026`). New Forest's report worried that draft S5 would allow remote development because harm is judged only against national policy (`new-forest-npa-pc-507-26-nppf-consultation`). None explains S5(4) or the S5(1)(j) "physically well-related" test. **Divergence:** councils passed S5(1)(j) 13 times out of 13 in our sample, treating adjacency as enough; inspectors passed 26 and failed 30 (patterns.md section F). The training does nothing to close that gap.

**TR3 and walking routes (TR).** No council training document covers TR3 route quality. The one council applying the final text, Three Rivers, does what inspectors do: it treats topography and residents' evidence, not the 800 metre line, as the reason a site is not within a reasonable walking distance, runs accessibility through TR3(1)(a), and argues that even if GB7 is passed, S5(5) with S5(2) and DP3 still means refusal (`three-rivers-lpa-response-nppf-2026-oxhey-lane`). **Divergence:** elsewhere, councils in our database accept carriageway walking ("existing residents do it") and short car trips; inspectors reject both (Stratford-on-Avon District Council (SDC) cases, patterns.md section F). The Country Land and Business Association (CLA) "continues to lobby for improved training of planning officers on rural issues" (`fwi-new-planning-framework-farmers-rural-businesses`).

**Grey belt (GB).** Gedling told members of the grey belt definition change and routine Green Belt reviews (`gedling-portfolio-holder-report-nppf-2026`); nothing explains the GB7 limbs. **Divergence (reversed):** in our database members, not officers, reject grey belt by regrading purpose (a), and lose at a village edge (PINS-6011103). Planning Geek's Croxley Green preview shows the same officer-member split at Three Rivers (`planninggeek-croxley-green-appeal`).

**Supply (SUP).** North East Lincolnshire, briefing full council in September 2026, still framed the shortfall in 2024 terms: "Not meeting the five-year supply means national planning policy takes precedence over local policy" while noting "the Council has been successful in a recent appeal" (`nelincs-cabinet-local-plan-sep-2026`). East Hampshire approves housing "taking account of the tilted balance" (`ehdc-cabinet-local-plan-update-may-2026`). Battle's handout says five-year supply still matters for speculative development outside boundaries. **Divergence:** the tilted balance no longer exists; inspectors treat any shortfall as opening S5(1)(j) or GB7 limb (ii) but give it the same outcome rate across supply bands. Councils give substantial housing weight at any scale; inspectors split 67 to 69 on 1 to 9 homes.

**Annex A (A2).** This is where council training most clearly diverges from inspectors. Every council briefing on Annex A taught the **draft** whole-policy wording:
- Gedling: inconsistent policies, "however recently adopted, will be considered out of date and be accorded little weight" (`gedling-portfolio-holder-report-nppf-2026`).
- King's Lynn: policies "which are in any way inconsistent ... should be given very limited weight" (`kings-lynn-nppf-update-presentation-2026`).
- East Hampshire: even "positively framed local policies, could attract very limited weight" (`ehdc-cabinet-local-plan-update-may-2026`).
- Battle Town Council: national policies "will take priority over (replace) existing policies in a local plan" (`battle-tc-how-planning-system-is-changing-handout`).

The final text narrowed this to "policies (or parts of those policies) which are materially inconsistent". **Divergence:** in our database SDC cut whole heritage (CS.8) and access (CS.26) policies, while inspectors cut clauses and kept heritage, design and access policies at full weight (PINS-6007541; PINS-6006637 ¶40). The draft-era training is a plausible cause of that council practice.
