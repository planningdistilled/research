# 01 Approach to Decision Making: Annex 5 - Sensitive personal information in decisions

## Annex 5 - Sensitive personal information in decisions

1. The Data Protection Act 2018 (DPA18) makes provision about the processing of personal data, and that most processing of personal data is subject to the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR). In other words, the DPA18 brings the UK GDPR into UK law.

2. “Personal data” is defined under s3(2) of the DPA18 as meaning ‘any information relating to an identified or identifiable living individual’. S3(3) continues that an “identifiable living individual” means ‘a living individual who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to:

(a) an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data or an online identifier, or

(b) one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural or social identity of the individual’.

3. S3(4) of the DPA18 defines “processing”, in relation to information, as meaning ‘an operation or set of operations which is performed on information, or on sets of information, such as (a) collection, recording, organisation, structuring or storage, (b) adaption or alteration, (c) retrieval, consultation or use, (d) disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, (e) alignment or combination, or (f) restriction, erasure or destruction’.

4. Some personal data is likely to be more sensitive based on the potential harm or impact on the individual(s). For instance, information relating to children, including their name, age, address or school is likely to be seen to be more intrusive than that relating to an adult.

5. Article 9(1) of the UK GDPR prohibits the processing of “special categories of personal data”, meaning: personal data that reveals the racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs or trade union membership [of an identifiable living individual]; genetic or biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person; or data concerning a natural person’s sex life or sexual orientation50. Personal data relating to health should be treated as subject to Article 9(1) even if it is not strictly ‘genetic or biometric’ in nature.

6. Exceptions to that prohibition are set out in Article 9(2); 9(2)(g) is most likely to be applicable to casework: ‘processing is necessary for reasons of substantial public interest, on the basis of domestic law which shall be proportionate to the

[^50]: Article 10 of the UK GDPR does not prohibit but sets out strict limitations to the processing of personal data relating to criminal convictions or offences, or related security measures.

aim pursued and provide for suitable and specific measures to safeguard the fundamental rights and the interests of the data subject’.

7. S10(3) of the DPA18 provides that any processing of “special categories of personal data” will comply with Article 9(2)(g) only if it meets a condition in Part 2 of Schedule 1 of the DPA18. The “substantial public interest conditions” include 6 which is, in short: the processing is necessary for the exercise of a function conferred on a person by an enactment or rule of law, or the exercise of a function of a Minister of the Crown or government department, and it is necessary for reasons of substantial public interest.

8. In other words, an Inspector may only lawfully collect, record, store, consult, use, disseminate or otherwise make available special categories of personal data where that is necessary in the exercise of their duties and there is a substantial public interest in doing so.

9. It is necessary for Inspectors (and/or the Planning Inspectorate), in the exercise of their functions, to ensure that evidence is properly copied to the main parties. For example, Rule 14(1) of the planning inquiry rules (2000/1625) requires the Secretary of State to send a copy of each proof of evidence to the local planning authority, the appellant and any Rule 6 party.

10. However, the Rules do not compel the sharing of other evidence even with the main parties and, no matter what the procedure, it may be unnecessary to share (in full) any particular evidence with interested parties. When considering whether to disseminate and/or refer to unredacted evidence which reveals special categories of or otherwise sensitive personal data, the question must be whether there is a substantial public interest in doing so.

11. That will be a matter for the decision-maker depending on the facts and circumstances. In general, however, any personal data or sensitive information should be redacted from documents, and evidence which is labelled as ‘confidential' should not be accepted. In most instances, the Casework Teams will turn away ‘confidential’ documents and redact personal data or other sensitive information from the evidence we can accept. However, the onus is still on the Inspector to ensure that this is done as appropriate and necessary.

12. If it is necessary to share or refer to personal data, consideration must be given to the extent to which that is in the public interest. For example, even if it is essential for the LPA or a Rule 6 party to know that the appellant is relying on the health needs of their child as a factor in favour of the development, it will still normally be possible to redact the name and date of birth of the child from the relevant evidence.

13. In the decision letter, it will normally be possible to omit not only details of the child, but also any specifics of their health needs or related evidence, such as their NHS number or where they are treated. It should suffice, for example, to

<!-- Part1 p.117 -->

refer to “Child A, who is below school age, has a serious medical condition which requires regular hospital outpatient appointments; this has been confirmed in a letter from Child A’s consultant”. The Inspector may then attribute weight to the issue in the usual way.

14. The same approach should be taken when considering the educational needs of children. It would rarely be necessary to name the school that a child attends or reveal what school year they are in. Inspectors must always be very careful not to refer to information which may imply the nature of the particular need; reference, for example, to special needs co-ordinators would probably give too much information about the personal circumstances of the child.

15. As a rule, publishing personal data on the internet is likely to be particularly intrusive of an individual’s right to privacy. Since PINS publishes casework decisions, ideally without redaction, it is vital that Inspectors write decisions and reports in a manner which can be published without contravening the DPA18 and UK GDPR. Even if personal data provided by a party concerns a crucial or determining consideration, it must be described in the most general terms and not in any detail in the decision letter or report.

16. It is not always possible to anonymise identities but that would normally be more reason, not less, to avoid sharing unnecessary personal data. The Inspector must check that their decision does not contain any special category of personal data, information relating to criminal convictions, or other information that is sensitive in nature. If it is considered essential to share or publish personal data, the Inspector should demonstrate that there is a substantial public interest in doing so and set out the information in one place in the decision letter or report for ease of redaction.

If in doubt as to what comprises personal data, or if it seems essential to refer to such information in your decision, seek advice from your line manager, Senior Data Protection Manager, Professional Lead and/or Knowledge Centre as appropriate.

18. The consequences of a breach of the UK GDPR or DPA18 can be severe for both the identifiable living individual affected, and the organisation responsible for the breach. Even before the DPA18 came into force, in May 2017, the Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) considered a case where Basildon BC had published a planning application statement that contained sensitive personal data, including the names, ages and health and disability issues of family members. It was possible to identify each person and their homes.

19. The ICO concluded that the publication of this sensitive personal data on the internet was in breach of the DPA98, in breach of the Council’s own policy in relation to disclosure and was likely to cause substantial damage and/or

<!-- Part1 p.118 -->

substantial distress to the persons affected. The ICO further found that the publication of sensitive personal data involving ethnic communities could lead them to legitimately fear how that might be used by hostile parties. Basildon BC was thus issued with a penalty, reduced on appeal to £75,000.

20. The Local Government Ombudsman (LGO) similarly found that a breach of the DPA98 (and of the Human Rights Act 1998) where Council publicised sensitive personal information, including details of the names, ages, schools and medical conditions of children on a site, in relation to a planning application.

21. The LGO accepted that it was necessary for the Council to obtain sensitive and personal information about the site occupiers’ circumstances, so to reach an informed view on the development. But it was not necessary or proportionate to publish that information and put it in the public domain. The information could have been considered without being widely circulated, so as to reduce the interference with the occupiers’ right to privacy
