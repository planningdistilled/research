- have all the parties had a fair opportunity to comment on a matter which might be a determining issue - “fair crack of the whip”? (see Poole, R (on the application of) v SSCLG t & Anor [2008] EWHC 676 (Admin) (14 March 2008))

- would the parties reasonably expect you to place significant weight on the matter? If this is necessary and if you are minded to rely on what you have seen, you should ensure that the main parties have had the chance to comment first on their relevance to the case.

In this context the Wainhomes case12 identifies that the decision as to whether submitted material “out of time” should be seen and taken into account by the Inspector lies with the Inspector or an appropriate person to whom s/he has delegated that responsibility. Case officers and their managers will have considered any such material and will have advised you of anything that has been rejected but it is essential that you, as decision maker, apply the “natural justice” principle if you consider that there is a risk that the rejected document could contain / represent a relevant material consideration.

### Consistency

92. If Inspectors reach significantly different conclusions about obviously similar cases with no explanation or acknowledgement then this can undermine confidence in the appeal process. Consistency in the planning system is therefore important for appellants, local planning authorities and the public. The courts have established that like cases should be decided in a like manner. However, that does not mean that like cases must be decided alike13.

93. A previous appeal decision is capable of being a material consideration where the previous decision is sufficiently closely related14 to the issues that regard should be had to it. This is not limited to proposals for the same or for the same type of development on the same site. Whether it is sufficiently closely related should be broadly defined and may include the interpretation or application of an individual policy (which may include spatial objectives15), whether a particular policy was out-of-date or not or similar types of development in the locality where similar issues and/or policies apply16 17. In general, where previous appeal decisions are provided in evidence these should not be summarily rejected as not material unless they are clearly irrelevant. Those that are treated as material considerations should be addressed in the decision.

94. There is, however, unlikely to be any need to take other appeal decisions expressly into account if they are only referred to by the winning party.

95. In ‘grasping the nettle’ 18of dealing with other relevant appeal decisions it may be that they can be distinguished from the appeal for good reason. This might be, for example, because the scheme has been changed since an earlier decision on the same site, the physical relationship to neighbouring properties or the surrounding area is not the same, the proximity to services and facilities is different, the policy background has changed or there has been a material change in circumstances such as the granting of another permission in the interim or a change in the housing land supply position.

96. The reasons for differentiating between previous decisions should be spelt out. These should be as specific and detailed as possible based on the evidence available and may need to explain how concerns identified by a previous Inspector on the same site have been overcome by changes to the scheme. The reasoning does not necessarily have to be lengthy and where there are multiple decisions submitted in respect of sites elsewhere, they can often be dealt with as a group rather than individually. If full details of the other appeals have not been provided, it is preferable to indicate that there is no evidence to indicate that they are directly comparable to the current appeal rather than to say that you have insufficient information.

97. You are nevertheless entitled to disagree with an earlier decision (whether on the same site or elsewhere) if there are sound reasons for so doing. The previous decision does not have to be accepted as ‘correct’. However, you should only do so where you have demonstrably had regard to it and given substantiated reasons for departing from it, having regard to the importance of consistency. This matter must be fully addressed and clear and specific reasons given for not following a critical element of the reasoning or the findings or the final outcome. It will not be sufficient to simply say that you have dealt with the appeal on its own merits.

98. The reasons for doing so will be case specific but might include matters such as the relative importance given to certain considerations, that a previous Inspector did not consider a particular matter and whether more or better evidence has been provided. However, you should be careful not to imply that this was due to the format of the appeal such as one following a hearing when the previous decision was made on the basis of written representations. Also, whilst the planning regime may have changed over time general development management policies may not have done. On the other hand, if there has been a clear shift in the known policy position this may be the rationale for reaching a different decision to the previous appeal.

99. In Gladman Developments Ltd v SSHCLG & Central Bedfordshire Council [2019] EWHC 127 (Admin) the Inspector was presented with nine appeal decisions relating to the weight to be attached to a particular policy. These had decided the point in different ways. Despite the difficulties in doing so, the Court ruled that simply because there were a significant number of previous decisions falling on both sides of the line was not a reason for not applying the principle in North Wiltshire. There was a failure in law to properly grapple with and provide reasons for departing from the earlier conclusions of Inspectors addressing the same issues in respect of the policy. By contrast, another Inspector in an appeal referred to had, as required, risen to the task of examining the earlier decisions, explaining why conflicting conclusions had been reached and providing reasons for departing from those conclusions which differed.

<!-- Part1 p.52 -->

100. To help ensure consistency, where possible, case officers will link similar appeals (for example, if on the same site) or chart them to the same Inspector so they ‘travel together’ (if in the same area). However, if this is not possible and you become aware that a similar appeal on the site or in the area is being dealt with by a different Inspector, you will need to decide what action to take. Consider the following:

- discuss the matter with your case officer – is there any scope for both appeals to be dealt with by the same Inspector?

- if not, the case officer should be asked to copy whichever decision is made first to the parties in the 2nd appeal in order to provide them with an opportunity to comment on whether it has a bearing on their cases

- whatever action you take, you should not discuss your case with the other Inspector. This could be seen as improper influence by someone who is not the appointed Inspector. Any such liaison should be via the PfL.

101. You should only refer to another appeal decision if the parties are aware of it. If not, you should give them the chance to comment.

### Seeking advice

102. When you are appointed to determine an appeal, you are solely responsible for what is decided. Whilst pre-issue quality assurance by colleagues is endorsed by the Courts, your reasoning, judgment, and conclusions on an appeal must not result from a discussion or consultation with another Inspector manager or anyone else within PINS19.

103. However, if a novel matter arises which is not covered in the ITM, it may be appropriate to seek:

- legal advice – for example, in respect of opposing legal views on complex legal matters or where interpretation of the planning acts, related legislation and case law is required;

- best practice advice on a particular point, procedural matter or on the application of planning policy.

104. When seeking advice:

The key to this is in ensuring that the Inspector takes the decision and the reader (or mentor as referred to by the Judge) does not interfere in his or her judgment:

- it is your responsibility to decide how the appeal should be dealt with and what decision should be reached.

- in respect of legal advice, the purpose should be to add to your knowledge of the law.

- advice between a lawyer and client is privileged and so will not be disclosed to the parties. However, it is important that any such advice is properly recorded (i.e. in writing).

105. Salaried Inspectors – any requests for legal advice must be made via a Professional Lead. They may know if the issue has arisen before and so be able to answer your question. Policy advice may be sought direct from the Knowledge Centre. Where a matter is novel, the advice given will then be assimilated into the relevant section of the Training Manual.

106. PADS – you must initially contact the Contract Management Unit (CMU).

107. If a decision on the planning merits cannot properly be decided without a complicated or difficult legal issue being decided upon first, then jurisdiction might need to be recovered by the Secretary of State. If this possibility arises consult with a Professional Lead.

108. Finally, the ITM refers to multiple approaches to our work, case law and perspectives. Inspectors will need to reach a rational and reasoned decision rather than relying solely on any one piece of advice within it without reference to other elements. In light of this the ITM should NOT be referenced or quoted from in decisions.

### National planning policy

109. The National Planning Policy Framework was first published in March 2012, and then updated in July 2018, February 2019, July 2021, September 2023, December 2024 and most recently, August 2026. It is often referred to as ‘NPPF’ or ‘the Framework’.

The Planning Practice Guidance was published by DCLG on 6 March 2014 as a web-based resource.

110. Written Ministerial Statements can also provide clarification on national policy and could be a relevant material consideration.

111. If draft national policy emerges it may be cited by the parties. If relevant, it may be a material consideration. However, the weight given to it will depend on whether it seeks to significantly change existing relevant policy and the certainty that it will be published in the same form given that it could change as a result of consultation. In general draft policy is likely to be of limited weight and the further views of the parties on it should be sought sparingly.

### The development plan

112. Section 38 of the 2004 Act (as amended by the Localism Act 2011) defines the development plan (in England) as follows:

- Outside Greater London – a) the regional spatial strategy for the region (if there is one), b) adopted development plan documents (taken as a whole) and c) neighbourhood development plans.

- In Greater London – a) the spatial development strategy (currently the London Plan), b) adopted development plan documents (taken as a whole) and c) neighbourhood development plans.

113. However, all regional strategies have now been revoked in full or in part.

114. Policies in old style Local Plans and UDPs will form part of the development plan if they have been “saved” by a Direction of the Secretary of State and provided they have not been superseded by a subsequent development plan.

### The NPPF and the development plan

115. Neither the NPPF nor any other national policy guidance can of itself provide that provisions of a development plan are no longer applicable20 and you must apply, address and conclude on development plan policies and s.38(6) in your decisions. Furthermore, the NPPF does not prescribe the weight which should be given to development plan policies21.

- The NPPF (in section 2 on Plan-making policies and section 3 on Decision-making policies) provides detail about dealing with its policies in decision making including the weight to be given to existing policies. In dealing with the NPPF the following might need to be considered: The Planning Practice Guidance states that the NPPF “must be taken into account where it is relevant to a planning application or appeal.”22 Nevertheless it is a material consideration and not statute. You will generally not need to expressly conclude against the NPPF if relevant development plan policies are consistent with it, even if it is mentioned in the reasons for refusal. However, you should do so if dealing with

### Planning Practice Guidance

116. In Mead Realisations Ltd v Secretary of State for Housing, Communities and Local Government [2025] EWCA Civ 3, the Court of Appeal provided guidance about the role and relationship of the NPPF and the Planning Practice Guidance (PPG).

117. In particular, it explained that the legal status of the NPPF and the PPG is essentially the same. No legal distinction exists between them. Both are statements of national policy issued by the Secretary of State exercising their general power to do so as minister with overall responsibility for the planning system. They have somewhat different purposes. In general, the NPPF is a comprehensive framework of national planning policy, in which the Government

sets out its general policies for planning decision-making and plan preparation, whereas the PPG explains how those policy objectives and purposes are to be achieved, and the principles put into practice. Where the NPPF policy is in relatively broad terms, the need for elucidation or explanation in the PPG may be greater. Where the policy is more prescriptive, it may be less.

118. It is therefore not right to describe the PPG as being, in a legal sense, wholly subservient or subordinate to the NPPF in a hierarchy of national planning policy. The exact relationship between a particular policy in the NPPF and corresponding or relevant guidance in the PPG will vary according to the content and terms of the policy and guidance in question. Policies in the NPPF and guidance in the PPG may be used as an aid to interpretation of each other. The publication of the PPG does not need to be contemporaneous with the NPPF in order to explain its intention. Together the NPPF and PPG form a mature body of planning policy and guidance.

### Interpreting and applying the development plan

119. The interpretation of development plan policies is a matter of law but the application of those policies to the particular circumstances of the case is a matter for the decision-maker. This principle should always be borne in mind. Furthermore, you must base your decision on the development plan policies in force at the time of your decision, irrespective of the policies used to determine the planning application. In addition:

- Have you demonstrated through your reasoning that you have understood and correctly interpreted the relevant policies? See Tesco Stores Ltd v Dundee City Council [2012] UKSC 13:

“… policy statements should be interpreted objectively in accordance with the language used, read as always in its proper context.” (paragraph 18)

120. “As has often been observed, development plans are full of broad statements of policy, many of which may be mutually irreconcilable, so that in a particular case one must give way to another. In addition, many of the provisions of development plans are framed in language whose application to a given set of facts requires the exercise of judgment. Such matters fall within the jurisdiction of planning authorities, and their exercise of their judgment can only be challenged on the ground that it is irrational or perverse. Nevertheless, planning authorities do not live in

<!-- Part1 p.57 -->

the world of Humpty Dumpty: they cannot make the development plan mean whatever they would like it to mean.” (paragraph 19)24

- In interpreting policy the supporting text does not have the force of a policy and the correct focus should be on the detailed policies themselves as confirmed by the Court of Appeal in Cherkley Campaign Ltd, R (on the application of) v Mole Valley DC & Anor [2014] EWCA Civ 567 (para 16):

121. “The supporting text consists of descriptive and explanatory matter in respect of the policies and/or a reasoned justification of the policies. That text is plainly relevant to the interpretation of a policy to which it relates but it is not itself a policy or part of a policy, it does not have the force of policy and it cannot trump the policy. I do not think that a development that accorded with the policies in the local plan could be said not to conform with the plan because it failed to satisfy an additional criterion referred to only in the supporting text. That applies even where, as here, the local plan states that the supporting text indicates how the polices will be implemented.”

- Does the decision turn or depend on the application of a particular policy. If so, then it is likely to be clearer and more logical to summarise this first so that the subsequent reasoning can relate to it in a comprehensible way.

- When reaching your conclusion, you should do so by reference to the specific wording of the policy itself rather than referring to perceived compliance with the objectives of a development plan policy.

- Are your references to policy as brief as they can be? Have you avoided setting out long free-standing summaries of policies or quoting from them verbatim? You may need to go into more detail if the interpretation, application or relevance of the policy is disputed.

- You do not need to state that the development plan has been adopted. However, if there is a dispute about its status, you would need to make the position clear.

122. Concluding on the development plan:

- Your attention will often be drawn to a large number of policies. Have you been selective about which you need to refer to? You need only assess the proposal against policies which are relevant to the main issues25. However, in doing so you should deal with any relevant policies which
