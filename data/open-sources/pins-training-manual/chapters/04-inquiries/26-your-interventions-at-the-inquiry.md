# 04 Inquiries: Your interventions at the Inquiry

<!-- Part1 p.210 -->

### Your interventions at the Inquiry

130. As part of the management of the inquiry, it is important that you as Inspector play an active role. You should intervene during the event to:

- stop discourteous/disruptive, aggressive or bullying behaviour. Do this as soon as it first occurs.

- stop advocates trying to score points which are not directly relevant to your consideration of the planning merits of the case.

- ensure the witnesses answer the questions put to them.

- prevent questions which are repetitious, unhelpful, irrelevant, clearly designed to annoy or are beyond the witness’s area of expertise.

- stop cross-examination on legal matters unless this is clearly in the expertise of the witness. Such matters are normally dealt with in submissions rather than through the cross-examination of a non-lawyer by an advocate.

- prevent leading questions during evidence-in-chief and especially during re-examination.

- remind interested persons asking questions of a witness that it is not an opportunity for them to make a statement.

- move questioning along or ask for evidence to be focussed on specific areas, if that is most helpful to you.

131. You should issue a warning if anyone makes a potentially slanderous or discriminatory remark. See the Inspector Training Manual chapters on Human Rights and Equality and The Approach to Decision Making for more

<!-- Part1 p.211 -->

information. The advice in Annex K ‘Managing Disruptive Parties’ may also be relevant in this context.

132. Inquiries may be attended by large numbers of people who have strong feelings about the proposal. They are likely to be unfamiliar with the planning system and with Inquiry procedures. They may be frustrated by having to wait to present their case. You may need:

- act quickly to stop disruption, including audible whispering, general conversation, gasps, applauding, booing or unsolicited comments. It will usually be enough to stress the need for politeness and that you need quiet so that you can hear all the participants and that the procedures are designed to allow everyone to have their say.

- amend the programme (if it is not already set up appropriately) so that interested persons are allowed to speak as early as possible in the proceedings. This can help to defuse tension.

- call a short adjournment if things become heated, which can sometimes help restore calm.

133. If the approaches outlined above are not successful you have the power to:

- Refuse to permit irrelevant or repetitious evidence or cross-examination (Rule 16(6)). Be aware, however, that the Rules state that if you refuse to permit oral evidence, the person must be allowed to submit the evidence in writing before the close of the Inquiry.

- Require a person behaving disruptively to leave, refuse to permit the person to return or permit them to return subject to conditions. Even so you must allow any such person to submit any evidence in writing before the close of the Inquiry (Rule 16(9)).

- Proceed with an Inquiry in the absence of a person entitled to appear (Rule 16(11)). This should only be done in exceptional circumstances.

134. Only rarely will you find it necessary to give a formal warning or ask someone to leave. If you do, make a careful written note for future reference in the event of a complaint. If you have asked someone to leave and they refuse, or if disorderly behaviour is disrupting proceedings despite your best attempts to maintain control, you should ask that building security is contacted in the first instance.

135. Your final option is for the police4 to be called, preferably by building security, and/or to adjourn to another day.
