# 04 Inquiries: General Approach

<!-- Part1 p.201 -->

### General Approach

65. Inquiries are more formal than Hearings. Witnesses are introduced by their advocates3. and there is a set procedure for giving evidence in chief, cross-examination and re-examination which is led by the advocates.

66. It is important that you demonstrate that you are in charge of proceedings. Avoid being tentative, passive or quiet. Clearly direct the transition between different stages of the Inquiry. For example:

“Mr A, you may now cross-examine Mrs B”.

“Mrs C, would you now call your 2nd witness”.

“We will now move on to the third main issue, which we are going to deal with in round-table format”.

67. Be prepared to intervene during the formal presentation of evidence rather than sitting inscrutably. Questions and interventions, notably to move cross-examination along or to ensure you understand a point, will reduce Inquiry time and assist the advocates. Interventions will also be necessary where there is unacceptable behaviour from advocates or witnesses.

68. Although you must retain an appropriate degree of formality, you are human and should appear so. You can smile and inject a degree of humour. Indeed, for many attendees this will be their first, and perhaps only, experience of a planning Inquiry. These can be daunting events and so you need to be able to put them at their ease. But do so carefully and avoid referring to controversial subjects or making light of the issues at the Inquiry.

69. Expert witnesses should sit at the witness table (but see the advice below about interested persons).
