# 04 Inquiries: What are the key evidential documents that you should receive?

<!-- Part1 p.189 -->

### What are the key evidential documents that you should receive?

14. A Statement of Case sets out the parties’ cases, identifying what they intend to argue. Section 12 of the Procedural Guide: Planning appeals – England provides more detail about what should be included (see also Rule 2(1) on “full statement of case”).

15. In cases where the appeal is against the LPA’s failure to issue a decision, there can often be delays with an LPA indicating its putative reasons for refusal. For example, this can be due to this needing to be agreed at committee. It may lead to delays because the appellant states that they do not know what case they have to answer. The template at Annex A can be adapted to request the putative reasons for refusal as soon as a failure case is allocated. This may help overall timeliness.

16. Rule 6(8) gives the Inspector/SoS the power to request further information about the matters contained in the Statement of Case if the submitted version is inadequate.

17. Proofs of evidence are the documents that contain the evidence of specific witnesses. A summary of the proof is required unless the proof is less than 1,500 words (Rule 14(2)).

18. The cases for the Appellant, LPA and any Rule 6 party should already have been set out in full in their Statements of Case. Consequently, the main purpose of a proof of evidence is to allow expert witnesses to:

- bring together evidence necessary for the presentation of their case at the Inquiry.

- give their opinion on the evidence provided by other parties in their Statements of Case.

19. Rebuttal proofs are not a formal part of the process and are accepted at your discretion. They can narrow areas of disagreement which may save inquiry time. But that may often be an unhelpful recitation of the parties’ cases, and this type of rebuttal should be firmly resisted. They are not an opportunity to introduce new evidence.

<!-- Part1 p.190 -->

20. The Planning appeals: procedural guide provides more advice about the contents of proofs and rebuttals, their length and the need for summaries. Chapter 15 sets out specific requirements for Expert Evidence.

21. Annex C: Content and Format of Proofs and Appendices gives further suggestions on the style and content of proofs. You may wish to include a version of this, tailored to your specific requirements, with your CMC note. Annex C: Content And Format of Proofs and Appendice

22. Statement(s) of Common Ground (SoCG) should set out the range of details on which the parties agree and, much more importantly, disagree. PINS has published guidance on SoCG for appeals. The guidance was prepared with the Planning & Environmental Bar Association (PEBA) and is intended to assist all professional representatives and expert witnesses in compliance with their duty to assist the Inspector.

23. In addition to the main SoCG, topic based SoCGs are a very useful document which will be of assistance to you. These will most likely be in relation to complex matters where the differences between the parties are unclear or where there is a vague reason for refusal such as “Insufficient information has been provided to enable a determination on highway safety”. Getting the parties to spell out the key areas of dispute may well save you a lot of questioning. You should flag up the possibility of topic based SOCGs at the CMC and set a deadline for their submission, usually alongside the proof of evidence. They can also be very useful instead of a rebuttal proof.

24. Advice on the content, form and purpose of a SoCG is also provided in Section 13 to the Procedural Guide
