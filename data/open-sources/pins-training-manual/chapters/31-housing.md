## Introduction

1. Inspectors make their decisions on the basis of the evidence before them. Consequently, they may, where justified by the evidence, depart from the advice given in this chapter.

2. Housing casework is likely to be encountered in various guises throughout an Inspector’s career. This training material is based on practical experience and is intended to cover the range of issues that you will encounter both in early cases and also in more demanding work as your allocation level increases. It is primarily directed at appeals casework but will also be relevant in the conduct of development plan examinations.

3. The general advice in the ITM chapter Approach to decision making applies to housing appeals as much as to any other type of appeal. The advice below should be read alongside the general advice in that chapter.

4. This training material applies to casework in England only and incorporates key points from caselaw.

## Legislation, national policy and guidance

5. At the outset it is important to remember that the statutory provisions in 1 and section 38(6) of the 2004 Act 2 apply to all

s70(1)(a) of the 1990 Act planning appeals, including housing appeals. ## The implications of paragraph 11 of the NPPF for housing appeal decisions

### Decision-taking (paragraphs 11 c and d) and the presumption in favour of sustainable development

9. Paragraph 11 of the NPPF states that plans and decisions should apply a presumption in favour of sustainable development. This section provides an overview and there is more detail about the steps to take in decision-making in the subsequent section on structure.

10. Paragraph 11 goes on to say, in its “decision-taking” section:

For decision-taking this means:

12. If the development proposal accords with an up-to-date development plan, paragraph 11 c) indicates that it should be approved without delay3. ### Appeal procedure

43. Where the existence of a 5YHLS or the extent of any shortfall is disputed, you may be presented with a considerable amount of evidence regarding the deliverability of particular sites. There may also be disagreement over what the 5YHLS requirement is.

44. In any such cases you will need to consider:

- Are issues relating to 5YHLS likely to be material to your decision?

- If so, does the evidence need to be tested by questioning because it is unclear, contradictory or inconsistent?

45. If the answer to both these questions is ‘yes’, you are likely to judge that the appeal should be dealt with by means of a hearing or inquiry. You should discuss this with your Case Officer who will notify the parties. The same is likely to apply if the parties have not addressed the issue of 5YHLS in any detail, but you consider that it is material to your decision and that you need to hear evidence on it. Inspectors and case officers should be pro-active in identifying and discussing such cases well before the event date. The appeal may need to be re-allocated to another Inspector if you are not yet trained to deal with hearings or inquiries.

103. If the housing requirement figure in the plan policies is set out as a range, the lower end of the range should be taken as the basis for calculating the 5YHLS requirement figure.45

Calculating the 5YHLS based on the local housing need assessment

115. If the strategic plan policies which set out the housing requirement for the plan period are more than five years old, and a review has not found that they do not need updating, the 5YHLS requirement will be based on the local housing need assessment for the plan area. The local housing need assessment uses a standard method set out in the PPG chapter Housing need assessment based on a formula that incorporates a baseline of local housing stock which is then adjusted upwards to reflect local affordability pressures to identify the minimum number of homes expected to be planned for.

116. The standard method produces an annual figure, which then needs to be multiplied by 5 to give the 5YHLS requirement in relevant appeals (subject to the addition of a buffer, as described in the next sub-section). As it is based on known data from specific sources and an exact formula there should be limited scope for disagreement about the final figure arising from the standard method. However, where the strategic policies are less than 5 years old then the housing requirement should be taken from the local plan in accordance with paragraph 78 of the NPPF.

117. Very often an LPA will use the monitoring year as the basis for the calculation of the 5YHLS. ### Which sites can be included in the 5YHLS?

118. In order for housing sites to be included in the 5YHLS, paragraph 72 of the NPPF requires them to be deliverable. The NPPF’s Glossary defines “deliverable” as follows:

To be considered deliverable, sites for housing should be available now, offer a suitable location for development now, and be achievable with a realistic

[^48]: PPG ID: 68-004-20240205

<!-- Part2 p.366 -->

prospect that housing will be delivered on the site within five years. In particular:

a) sites which do not involve major development and have planning permission, and all sites with detailed planning permission, should be considered deliverable until permission expires, unless there is clear evidence that homes will not be delivered within five years (for example because they are no longer viable, there is no longer a demand for the type of units or sites have long term phasing plans).

b) where a site has outline planning permission for major development, has been allocated in a development plan, has a grant of permission in principle, or is identified on a brownfield register, it should only be considered deliverable where there is clear evidence that housing completions will begin on site within five years.

119. This provides a clear division between sites considered in be deliverable in principle under a) and others. In one category sites are assumed to be deliverable unless there is “clear evidence” to the contrary and under b) “clear evidence” of their deliverability is required. The PPG chapter on Housing supply and delivery gives advice on what might constitute the “clear evidence” referred to in the NPPF.49

120. The words “in particular” shows that categories a) and b) do not set out the only types of site covered by the definition. Therefore, it does not contain a closed list. This has been accepted by the Secretary of State in submitting to judgment following a legal challenge (East Northamptonshire Council v Secretary of State for Housing, Communities and Local Government case number CO/917/2020 – Consent Order sealed 12 May 2020). The Order says: “The proper interpretation of the definition is that any site which can be shown to be ‘available now, offer a suitable location for development now, and be achievable with a realistic prospect that housing will be delivered on the site within five years’ will meet the definition; and that the examples given in categories (a) and (b) are not exhaustive of all the categories of site which are capable of meeting that definition. Whether a site does or does not meet the definition is a matter of planning judgment on the evidence available.”. The Order does not have the same status as a judgment made by the courts, but it nevertheless provides clarity.

121. This means that provided there is “clear evidence” about deliverability and a “realistic prospect” that completions will occur within 5 years, there is no reason to exclude sites that are not specifically mentioned in categories a) and b) as a matter of course. Furthermore, as noted above, the PPG refers to the use of the “latest available evidence” and so there is no barrier in principle to consider information about sites after any base date for assessment.

123. If the deliverability of one or more sites is disputed, then any findings you make will need to be set out and justified. The amount of detail required will depend on the evidence presented and the level of any agreement or disagreement between the parties. However, it may not be necessary to go through each and every site canvassed individually. Whilst you may have received evidence about multiple sites there is no need to address them all one-by-one if this would not assist in reaching your overall finding. 139. When affordable housing arises in casework consider the following:

- Should affordable housing be a “main issue” or an “other matter”? It is likely to be a main issue where the LPA contends that affordable housing should be provided but it is not – or where the LPA considers the provision being made is not sufficient or is not of the right mix – that is to say if it is a contested issue. - Should the provision of affordable housing be a factor that is weighed in favour of the proposal? ### Planning obligations and conditions

143. In order for affordable housing to be provided effectively, arrangements must be made to transfer it to an affordable housing provider, to ensure that appropriate occupancy criteria are defined and enforced, and to ensure that it remains affordable to first and subsequent occupiers. The legal certainty provided by a planning obligation (either a section 106 agreement or unilateral undertaking) makes it the best means of ensuring that these arrangements are effective. However, there is nothing in national policy or advice that requires an obligation to be entered into in order to assure the delivery of affordable housing.

144. If the evidence in a given case indicates that affordable housing should be provided you should, therefore, normally expect that a completed planning obligation providing the affordable housing is submitted with the appeal, or at the hearing or inquiry. However, where the parties have been genuinely unable to complete the planning obligation before a hearing or inquiry closes, you may allow limited time after the close (a maximum of one or at most two weeks) for the obligation to be submitted so that you may take it into account in your decision.

145. There is a detailed checklist for planning obligations in PINS’ Planning obligations: good practice advice.

146. In the absence of a planning obligation, it may be possible in limited circumstances to use a planning condition to secure affordable housing. However, you should be aware of the advice in the PPG that a positively worded condition that requires the applicant to enter into a planning obligation is unlikely to be enforceable. The PPG chapter Use of Planning Conditions further advises that:

“A negatively worded condition limiting the development that can take place until a planning obligation or other agreement has been entered into is unlikely

<!-- Part2 p.373 -->

to be appropriate in the majority of cases. […] However, in exceptional circumstances a negatively worded condition requiring a planning obligation or other agreement to be entered into before certain development can commence may be appropriate where there is clear evidence that the delivery of the development would otherwise be at serious risk (this may apply in the case of particularly complex development schemes). In such cases the 6 tests must also be met.”

“Where consideration is given to using a negatively worded condition of this sort, it is important that the local planning authority discusses with the applicant before planning permission is granted the need for a planning obligation or other agreement and the appropriateness of using a condition. The heads of terms or principal terms need to be agreed prior to planning permission being granted to ensure that the test of necessity is met and in the interests of transparency.”57

147. It is a matter of judgement for the decision-maker as whether all these tests in the PPG are met, so that the use of a condition to secure affordable housing is appropriate. They are quite specific and only occur in exceptional circumstances and so the reasoning to support the use of a condition should address the relevant tests directly.

148. Even if a proposed condition does not explicitly require a legal agreement, but leaves the method of securing the affordable housing vague, it will be reasonable to conclude that a legal agreement will be required and that the PPG tests regarding the use of conditions to secure obligations should still be applied. This is because the judgment in R (on the application of Skelmersdale Ltd Partnership) v West Lancashire BC [2016] EWCA Civ 1260 confirmed that the interpretation of a condition is based on "what a reasonable reader would understand the words to mean when reading the condition in the context of the other conditions and of the consent as a whole".

149. In particular, in Skelmersdale, the phrase "submits a scheme which commits to retaining their presence as a retailer" was interpreted as requiring a legally-binding obligation. Consequently, a condition such as that at Annex 4 to this chapter requiring a scheme to “ensure” that dwellings remain as affordable housing (or other similar wording) could also be reasonably interpreted as requiring a legal agreement, and so engage the PPG tests. In order for it to meet those tests, therefore, you would need to be satisfied, before imposing the condition, that there are exceptional circumstances to justify this and that the tests set out at para 010 of the PPG are met.

Occasionally you may encounter proposals for domestic staff accommodation.

257. Where it is proposed to construct an extension to a dwellinghouse or a separate building in its curtilage, the use of the structure will normally be to provide additional ordinary living space or for purposes incidental to the use of

- To provide additional ordinary living space – this is referred to in the Technical Guidance: Permitted Development for Householders as ‘primary living accommodation, such as a bedroom, bathroom, or kitchen’. Particularly in the case of a proposed outbuilding that would provide primary living accommodation, the parties might suggest that such a use is ‘ancillary’ to the main dwellinghouse use. Uttlesford informs that incidental ‘means something occurring together with something else and being subordinate to it …incidental purposes are regarded as being those connected with the running of the dwelling-house or with the domestic or leisure activities of the persons living in it, rather than with the use as ordinary living accommodation’. Examples of incidental uses are parking/garaging, garden storage buildings, home gyms etc. In such cases, it is more appropriate to use the term ‘incidental’, rather than ‘ancillary’. This is largely because relevant legislation, including section 55(2)(d)89 of the 1990 Act and Class E90, of Part 1, Article 3, Schedule 2 of the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (England) Order 2015, uses this term.

258. Where it is proposed to construct an extension or outbuilding within the curtilage of a dwellinghouse to provide living space for a relative or other person (often described as an “annexe”), these tend to fall into one of two categories:

- Additions to dwellings which are simply extensions in the usual sense of the word, or an outbuilding within the curtilage of a dwellinghouse. Such proposals would provide limited facilities, such that the occupier would wholly rely on most of the facilities within the main house. The facilities within the accommodation, the physical layout, and the functional use of the addition would be such that the unit would not provide for independent living. An example of this might include an extension or outbuilding that contains a bedroom and ensuite bathroom; or

- Annexes (either by means of an extension or an outbuilding) which would provide for independent living.

259. Concerns from LPAs and others tend to fall into two categories:

- Where it is accepted that the accommodation would be part and parcel of the existing dwellinghouse – but there are concerns about the local effect on character/appearance, living conditions or other matters; or

- There are concerns that the accommodation would be unlikely to be part and parcel of the existing dwellinghouse and so would, in reality, be used as an independent/separate dwelling – this might give rise to concerns of principle (for instance, if countryside policies seek to prevent new dwellings) or that use as a separate dwelling might cause other problems (e.g. through additional traffic, noise and disturbance or an unsatisfactory relationship with the main dwelling). In such cases there may also be objections relating to the effect of the development on character/appearance, living conditions, etc.

260. Gravesham BC v SSE & O’Brien [1983] JPL 306 is relevant to proposals that provide for independent living. The case identified the distinctive characteristic of a dwellinghouse was its ability to afford to those who used it the facilities required for day-to-day private domestic existence. However, the judge in Uttlesford considered that, even if the accommodation provided facilities for independent day-to-day living, it would not necessarily become a separate planning unit from the main dwelling. In each case it would be a matter of fact and degree. In Uttlesford the accommodation gave the occupant the facilities of a self-contained unit although it was intended to function as an annex with the occupant sharing her living activity in company with the family in the main dwelling. There was no reason in law why such accommodation should consequently become a separate planning unit from the main dwelling.

261. If it is argued that the accommodation would be used as an independent or separate dwelling, or you are concerned that what is before you is a proposal that might not be used in a way that is part and parcel of the existing dwellinghouse use, you should consider both the physical and functional connection between the proposed accommodation and the existing dwellinghouse. The following questions might help you decide:

Physical connection:

- Is the layout such that the annex would share any facilities or space with the main house – e.g. garden space, garage, parking, garden store, access for drivers and pedestrians?

- Would there be an unhindered internal (if extension) or external (if outbuilding) connection between the main house and the proposal?

- How close would the proposal be to the main house (near or far)?

<!-- Part2 p.402 -->

- How would the proposal compare in size (footprint and/or floorspace) to the main house (smaller or not)?

- What internal facilities would it contain and how do these compare with those in the main house? - e.g., kitchen (large or small kitchenette?), bathroom (family bathroom size or en suite?), living space (modest or substantial?), bedrooms (more than 1?).

Functional connection:

- would there be a familial relationship between the occupiers?

- Would occupants live as part of the household in the main house, or would they live independently? For example, would the occupiers of the annex take meals together with the main household. Would there be comings and goings between the two?

- Would care be provided for the occupiers of the annex by the occupiers of the main household?

- Would there be single services and utility provision?

- How would the shared facilities and spaces be used by all occupants?

- Having regard to the above, would all occupiers still be regarded as a single household, or would the proposal result in there being a separate household within the new accommodation?

262. With the above in mind, it is important to remember that, if not a separate dwellinghouse, Uttlesford tells us that a residential annex would be a ‘use as ordinary living accommodation’ that is ‘normally to be expected as part and parcel of any dwelling's normal facilities’, and that it is an ‘integral part of the ordinary residential use as a dwelling-house’. Its use would be part and parcel of the dwellinghouse, rather than it being ‘incidental’ or ‘ancillary’, as some parties may suggest.

263. Notwithstanding the above, it is important to carefully consider the description of the proposed development given in the application form. The provisions of section 75(2) and (3) of the 1990 Planning Act say:

(2) Where planning permission is granted for the erection of a building, the grant of permission may specify the purposes for which the building may be used.

(3) If no purpose is so specified, the permission shall be construed as including permission to use the building for the purpose for which it is designed.

Accordingly, if you are minded to grant permission, you should be satisfied that the description of the development is consistent with (i.e. could not be interpreted as anything other than) a proposed extension or building to be used in a way that is part and parcel of the existing dwellinghouse use (see

<!-- Part2 p.403 -->

the advice in the Enforcement Chapter on ‘Interpretation of Planning Permissions’). Otherwise, having regard to section 75(3) of the 1990 Planning Act, there is a risk that permission might inadvertently be granted for a separate dwelling.

264. The starting point is to consider the proposal as applied for. Even if the development could be used as a separate dwelling, and a party has raised sound planning objections for such use, if you are satisfied that the description of the development is precise in describing development that would be used in a way that is part and parcel of the existing dwellinghouse use, it should suffice to point out that there is no proposal for a separate dwelling before you. If the structure is not built or used as proposed, or if there is a material change of use in the future to create a separate dwelling, then a separate grant of planning permission would be required, and the building would be at risk of enforcement action if such permission is not granted. For this reason, and provided that you are satisfied that the description of development is precise in describing the development for which you will grant permission, a condition limiting the use to that proposed would not normally be necessary, although this is often suggested by the LPA. Furthermore, a condition limiting the use to an ‘ancillary’ or ‘incidental’ residential use would not be correct, for the reasons given above.

265. In some cases, the description of development might not be sufficiently precise, yet you might be satisfied that, having regard to the evidence before you, its use would be part and parcel of the existing dwellinghouse use. In these circumstances there is advice in the ‘Extensions, Outbuildings and Annexes’ section of the Conditions Chapter on the use of an appropriate condition. A condition linking the use of the development to the existing dwellinghouse use might also be appropriate in certain circumstances, bearing in mind that the development will remain in place long after the need which gave rise to the application has gone.

266. There will, however, be circumstances where you cannot be satisfied that the development would be additional accommodation to the main dwellinghouse and that its use would not be part and parcel of the main dwellinghouse. In such cases you should provide robust reasons, explaining why you have reached this conclusion, despite the description of the development before you.
