# 37h Local Plan Examinations (NPPF 2024): Economic Development: Assessing economic and business need

<!-- Part3 p.85 -->

## Assessing economic and business need

### The evidence base

13. Strategic level local plans should be supported by a robust and up to date evidence base to understand existing business needs. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment”, paragraph 025)

14. The steps involved in building up this evidence base (PPG “Plan making”, paragraph 040) include:

- working together with local authorities and combined authorities to define the most appropriate geography to prepare policies (note that PPG “Plan making” refers to local enterprise partnerships, but their functions have been transferred to local authorities and combined authorities).

- preparing and maintaining a robust evidence base to understand both existing business needs and likely changes in the market, with reference to local industrial strategies where relevant; and

- engaging with the business community to understand their changing needs and identify and address barriers to investment, including a lack of housing, infrastructure or viability.

15. Both the NPPF and PPG state that plan-making should be informed by proportionate evidence. Having regard to PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment”, paragraph 026, the work to compile the evidence base is likely to include an assessment of:

- the existing land stock

- the recent pattern of supply and loss

- evidence of market demand and wider market signals

- labour demand and supply

- land and property take-up

- future property market requirements

- consultation with relevant organisations

- the likely achievability of economic development (PPG “Plan making”, paragraph 041) and

- any evidence of market failure that prevents land from being used effectively.

### Establishing needs

16. Paragraph 11b of the NPPF states that strategic policies should as a minimum provide for objectively assessed needs, which include economic and business development, as well as any needs that cannot be met within neighbouring areas (as established through statements of common ground), unless the exceptions listed in NPPF 11b apply. Lower provision than that identified in the needs assessment will require robust justification.

17. That said, the amount of business and economic development land provision can take into account the role and character of the specific local authority. For example, a substantial city with a large business and economic base is likely to be a major employment draw for an adjoining more rural district. The business and employment provision in the local plans of both authorities will need to take this into account. And there may not be a direct relationship between housing and employment growth – an example is where the more rural authority is taking some of the urban authority’s unmet housing need, but may be able to rely on spare business and economic development land capacity in the urban authority.

### Functional economic market areas

18. Needs are generally established at the geography of a functional economic market area (FEMA).

19. A FEMA is a geographic region defined by economic activity, with (broadly) a unified labour market, travel-to-work patterns, integrated business activity, shared services and infrastructure and similar consumer behaviour patterns.

20. The FEMA may overlap several administrative areas and the assessment may need to be carried out on a cross boundary basis. (PPG “Housing and economic development needs assessment” paragraphs 25-26). The figures may then need to be broken down into figures for each authority unless, for example, a joint plan is being prepared and needs are to be met across the whole plan area.

21. There are some other factors that the plan-making authority might need to take into account in establishing needs.

- FEMAs can overlap;

- some activities may have their own specialised market areas;

- local authority areas may not be contiguous with the FEMA; and

- the FEMA can mask substantial variations within an area such as spatial inequalities.

### Translating forecasts into floorspace and land requirements

22. Forecasts of job numbers. space needs and operational requirements per sector may be translated (thorough anticipated employment densities and site area to floorspace ratios) into site area and floorspace requirements for sectors, use classes and type of property. (PPG “housing and economic needs assessment paragraph 030). The Royal Institute of Chartered Surveyors’ publication entitled “Land measurement for planning and development purposes” (April 2023) may assist local authorities with the calculation.

23. In the process of calculating space and land requirements, regard should be had to matters that may not be covered adequately by conventional approaches to projection and forecasting, such as:

- specific local needs and opportunities

- strengths and deficiencies

- skills and knowledge clusters

- growth initiatives.

24. For example, an overall figure derived from conventional forecasting might not adequately recognise:

- emerging sectors (for example the need for datacentres)

- important local needs

- the needs of rural industry

- the needs of specific clusters

- the needs of particular businesses in the area

- the need to re-balance an economy that is overdependent on particular industries

- the need to address the oversupply or undersupply of a particular land type.

25. Local companies, agents and representors may have more detailed information on the needs of these activities.

26. In addition, the broader range of activities and freedom of operation allowed for by Use Class E should be recognised.

### Calculating the needs of strategic distribution facilities

27. Locational factors are critical in meeting the need for strategic distribution. Conventional employment land “needs” assessments based on local sectoral forecasts and past build rates may not be sufficient for this purpose. The scale of this kind of development means that any calculation of a requirement figure is often carried out separately from that for general economic development or employment land.

28. Very high levels of demand for new or extended facilities are likely to be experienced in locations with very high accessibility to the strategic road network, a concentration of existing strategic distribution facilities and substantial catchment populations. Other areas may attract little interest. The amount of provision in any particular plan is not therefore likely to be a function of employment projections. Rather, it will be strongly influenced by market demand, the availability of suitable land and the provision of adequate infrastructure.

29. Strategic distribution provision may require the provision of new or modified infrastructure, including power and highways, which may give rise to issues of phasing.

30. Calculating land and floorspace needs for strategic distribution will require collaboration with other authorities, infrastructure providers, logistics developers and occupiers and other interests to identify the scale of need across the relevant market areas.

### Data centres and AI facilities

31. The UK’s AI Opportunities Action Plan was introduced by the Secretary of State for Science, Innovation and Technology in 2025. It is based on three broad goals, one of which is building sufficient secure and sustainable AI infrastructure (including the establishment of AI Growth Zones). This was followed, also in 2025, by the UK Compute Roadmap, which sets out a plan for establishing the UK at the forefront of AI.

32. Demand for data centres and AI is significant and growing rapidly, The AI Opportunities Action Plan states that the UK will need at least 6GW of AI-capable data centre capacity by 2030, a threefold increase on the available capacity in 2025. Should the capabilities and adoption of AI accelerate, demand could exceed this baseline significantly. The NPPF identifies datacentres, along with certain other facilities as important to meet the needs of a modern economy.

33. Large data centres are now designated as Nationally Significant Infrastructure Projects (NSIPs) and developers of such projects can request that their schemes are opted in to the NSIP regime. They require significant amounts of power and water. Adequate infrastructure provision is critical. Smaller facilities and AI projects are likely to come under the normal planning permission regime.

<!-- Part3 p.89 -->

34. Whilst datacentres are likely to have relatively low levels of direct employment, they create construction jobs, highly skilled permanent jobs and support a wide range of other jobs in the local and national economy. They may also become a catalyst for a cluster of other economic and business activities in the locality and encourage inward investment.

35. The need to identify land for datacentres and other high tech industry is specifically mentioned in paragraphs 86 and 87 of the 2024 NPPF. Local planning authorities should be fully aware of the potential demand for AI facilities, demand for datacentres and a range of related technologies and where appropriate this need should be quantified and suitable locations identified.

### The relationship between business and economic land needs and the housing requirement and other demographic factors

36. The plan’s housing requirement is likely to be closely related to the level of business and economic land provision. The needs of additional planned economic growth in an area may require the plan’s housing requirement to be raised above the level of local housing need. Conversely, where the housing requirement is raised above local housing need to, for example, meet the unmet housing needs of a neighbouring area, this may stimulate business growth and in turn generate the need for more employment provision.

37. Other demographic factors might also be relevant, including the implications of an ageing population or out-commuting.

### Timescales and economic cycles

38. Strategic policies are expected to look ahead at least 15 years from adoption, so the employment land needs assessment and requirement should normally cover that period, although circumstances occasionally arise in which a plan can be found sound even if its strategic policies do not look ahead at least 15 years from adoption.

39. Needs assessments must be sufficiently up to date. The Planning Inspectorate’s Procedure Guide to Local Plans advises that evidence base documents that are two or more years old when the plan is submitted may be at risk of having been overtaken by events.

40. That said, care should be taken over changes in recent data. Recent data alone may be unrepresentative of longer term conditions and trends. Authorities will need to take account of longer term economic cycles in assessing this data and consider and plan for the implications of alternative economic scenarios. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” paragraph 027)

41. The need to take a longer term view when faced with short term economic volatility is reflected in the letter from the Minister for State for Housing and

<!-- Part3 p.90 -->

Planning to the Interim Chief Executive of the Planning Inspectorate, dated 15 July 2026:

“It is important that local plan deliverability and viability challenges be considered in a broad context, especially when economic conditions are volatile and are causing fluctuations to market conditions and heightening development risk and associated costs. As such, allocations should generally be assessed against their prospects of delivery over the plan period as a whole, rather than solely against current market conditions. For these reasons, where a robust evidence base exists and there is a reasonable basis for concluding that allocations are capable of delivery over the plan period as a whole, I expect concerns regarding deliverability and viability to be approached pragmatically.”

42. Thus it is necessary to maintain the long term view rather than react to short term volatility. However, evidence updates may be required in relation to specific sectors. Convincing evidence might be provided to the examination about the needs of a business sector that was not foreseen by the local planning authority The emerging AI / tech sector could be an example; or it might be a need for more rural business space. In such circumstances it may be possible to rectify this at examination by means of an updated topic paper or plan modification. A pragmatic approach should be taken that avoids delaying the examination where possible.

### Establishing the plan’s need figure

43. The evidence described above may be used to establish quantitative and qualitative needs for all foreseeable kinds of economic activity over the plan period. There is no nationally prescribed methodology for this analysis. If there are arguments about employment land or floorspace needs during the course of plan preparation, they should be resolved as far as possible before the examination by the local planning authority in consultation with developers and other stakeholders.

44. The outputs from needs assessments may include separate hectare or floorspace figures for different kinds of employment uses, warehouses and offices. Once existing permissions and allocations are taken into account, these figures can be translated into a minimum residual requirement, to be met through site allocations.

45. Plans may sometimes include an estimated contribution from existing designated industrial and business areas towards the plan’s requirement for industrial and business floorspace. In the London Plan, additional floorspace for industrial and warehouse uses is assumed to come through intensification of existing sites. If this approach is taken in a plan, it is essentially relying on windfalls and should be critically examined for deliverability having regard to viability, market demand, land ownership and other practicalities.

<!-- Part3 p.91 -->

46. Sometimes local planning authorities will state that they do not need to allocate land for business and employment purposes in the local plan because of the inherited supply from previous local plans and planning permissions. The evidence for this will be tested at examination – for example, there might be evidence of need in particular sectors which, for locational or qualitative reasons, is not being met by the existing supply.

47. Whilst there is no national policy that requires a quantitative surplus of supply, it may be prudent for the plan to contain a surplus in recognition of the length of time allocated sites take to be completed, to allow for unforeseen business land needs, or to ensure that different types of site in different locations are available for various kinds of activity.

48. The plan-making authority should be able to demonstrate, by reference to its evidence base and its process of analysis, preferably through a topic paper, that it has taken a sound evidence-based approach to the identification of business needs and land and floorspace requirements.
