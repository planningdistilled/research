## Introduction

1. The Government’s approach to flooding is to assess and then avoid risk. The National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) sets out strict tests to protect people and property from flooding. These are designed to ensure that development is steered to lower risk sites and can be made safe throughout its lifetime without increasing flood risk elsewhere. The Planning Policy Guidance (PPG - Flood risk and coastal change) states that where these tests are not met, new development should not be allowed.

2. The term ‘flood risk’ is key to the application of the presumption in favour of sustainable development at Chapter 4, S1 of the NPPF. ‘Areas at risk of flooding’ are included in footnote 24. This means that the application of policies in the Framework relating to areas at risk of flooding could provide a strong reason for refusing the development under paragraph S4, 2(b). If you conclude that it is a strong reason for refusal, you must explain why this is the case. This finding can be brief but should be explicit rather than inferred.

3. Be aware that publication of the August 2026 NPPF means that the September 2025 PPG will need updating in regard to paragraph numbers, and possibly some minor content/wording.

### What is flooding?

4. “Flood risk” is a combination of the probability and the potential consequences of flooding. Areas at risk of flooding are those at risk of flooding from any source, now or in the future.

5. There are numerous sources of flooding, including from rivers and the sea, rainfall on the ground and rising groundwater, overwhelmed sewers and drainage systems, and from reservoirs, canals and lakes and other artificial sources. The main types of flooding are described in the table below.

6. A ‘design flood’ is a flood event of a given annual probability plus an appropriate allowance for climate change. As an example, for river flooding, this is the likely flood to occur with a 1% annual probability (a 1 in 100 chance each year).

Rivers and the sea Low-lying areas that are close to rivers or the sea are more likely to flood when water levels rise.

River floods are also known as ‘fluvial floods’.

<!-- Part1 p.1069 -->

Land is classified as being in Flood Zones 1 to 3b for risk from rivers and the sea, where 1 is at the lowest risk and 3b is the functional flood plain.

Typical design floods are 1% annual probability for rivers and a 0.5% annual probability for tidal flooding.

Surface water | Surface water flooding is sometimes known as flash or pluvial flooding. It happens when rainwater cannot drain away through normal drainage systems.

Maps are available from the Environment Agency (EA) that show where surface water flooding is likely to occur with an annual likelihood by extent and depth, but this is not in zones.

Lead local flood authorities (LLFAs) are responsible for managing flood risk from surface water and may have more information.

Typical design flood is a 1% annual probability.

Groundwater | Groundwater is the water that is usually held in rocks and soil underground. Groundwater flooding happens when this water rises and flows above the surface. Flooding from rivers is more likely when groundwater levels are high.

There are no flood zones available for groundwater.

LLFAs are responsible for managing flood risk from groundwater. They may hold more detailed information, including previous groundwater flooding details for an area.

Reservoirs | A reservoir is a large natural or artificial lake that is designed to collect and store water.

They are usually formed by building a dam across a river, or by building a large tank or surrounding embankment. If one of these dams or embankments fails, then water could escape from the reservoir. This would result in land or properties being flooded.

Mapping and modelling of flooding from reservoir failure is available.

### Outline of the approach to appeals

7. The following steps must always be taken where flood risk has been raised as an issue:

<!-- Part1 p.1070 -->

- assess the risk of flooding

- conclude against policies in the local development plan, as per section 38(6) of the Planning and Compulsory Purchase Act 2004.

- conclude against the policies in the NPPF as a material consideration

- undertake the planning balance. This is the point at which other considerations, including housing land supply, may be relevant, alongside the outcome of the NPPF flood risk tests.

8. When undertaking a planning balance, keep in mind that flooding is a serious issue. Building in an area at risk, even if the tests are passed, can result in poor quality development that is potentially uninsurable and unliveable if regularly flooded, even if it can be made safe. The weight to be given to the quality of development and any benefits arising from it is a matter for your planning judgement.

## Assessment of flood risk

### Establish if the site is at risk of flooding

9. According to section F4 of the NPPF, an application must be accompanied by a site-specific Flood Risk Assessment (FRA) if the development is:

- in Flood Zones 2 and 3a and 3b

- in Flood Zone 1 where the site is of 1 hectare or more

- land identified by the EA as having critical drainage problems

- land identified in an SFRA as being at increased flood risk in the future

- land that may be subject to other sources of flooding, where the development would introduce a more vulnerable use

10. Even if the site is not at risk of flooding, section F8 of the NPPF requires that all development that could affect drainage on or around a site should incorporate SuDS to reduce wider flood risk. Proposals for major development should take into account advice from the LLFA, and have minimum standards and maintenance arrangements.

### Site-specific Flood Risk Assessment

11. In most cases, an application will be accompanied by an FRA carried out by an appropriately qualified specialist. An exception to this may be if it is for a simple, low risk development, such as a house extension.

<!-- Part1 p.1071 -->

12. Para 020 of the PPG states that the objectives of a site-specific FRA are to establish:

- whether a proposed development is likely to be affected by current or future flooding from any source;

- whether it will increase flood risk elsewhere;

- whether the measures proposed to deal with these effects and risks are appropriate;

- the evidence for the local planning authority to apply (if necessary) the Sequential Test, and;

- whether the development will be safe and pass the Exception Test, if applicable.

13. The level of detail needs to be credible and fit for purpose. The FRA should be proportionate to the anticipated degree of flood risk and the scale, nature and location of the development. It should make use of the SFRA, the Flood Map for Flooding, and the surface water and groundwater flood risk information on the ‘long term flood risk’ site.

14. As a minimum, an FRA should contain the information set out in the flood risk assessment checklist in para 080 of the PPG.

15. The following sections describe the most common sources of information and how these should be used.

16. You will need to be satisfied that you have sufficient information before you to understand the risk from flooding. Where it is missing, you may conclude that the proposal fails to demonstrate that it avoids flood risk, in which case a Sequential Test may be needed.

17. The absence of an FRA, or the fact that a submitted FRA does not contain sufficient or adequate evidence on which to base a decision can sometimes be a reason for refusal.

### Risk management authorities

18. Responsibility for managing flooding and responding to planning applications falls to several bodies, with different remits, which can sometimes appear to overlap. These include the EA, LLFAs, District Councils, Internal Drainage Boards, Highways Authorities, and Water and Sewerage Companies. The main bodies are described below to help understand how they relate to each other, their responsibilities and what you should expect to receive from them.

<!-- Part1 p.1072 -->

EA | The EA supervises and works with other organisations to manage the risk of flooding and coastal erosion in England. It also directly manages flood risk from main rivers, the sea and reservoirs.

There is a statutory requirement for local planning authorities to consult the EA for developments in certain areas of flood risk (as defined in Schedule 4 of the Town and Country Planning (Development Management Procedure) (England) Order 2015 before granting planning permission. For lower risk sites the EA may refer to standing advice.

The EA provides flood risk guidance for local planning authorities and developers on what to include in a flood risk assessment, when to consult the Environment Agency and how to address flood risk using standing advice.

The EA produces the Flood Map for Planning and the ‘Long term flood risk’ data and mapping.

Applicants can also buy additional data from the EA to help complete the flood risk assessment. This includes information about risk management structures, climate change and residual flood risk, in addition to data for additional modelling. These are referred to as ‘Product 1’ to ’Product 8’ information.

All local planning authorities should notify the Environment Agency of the decision on any planning application where the Agency has objected on flood risk grounds.

For any planning application for major development where the local planning authority is minded to grant permission and the proposal:

1. Is within Flood Zones 2 or 3, or on land within Flood Zone 1 which has been notified to the local planning authority as having critical drainage problems; and

2. Is the subject of a sustained objection by the Environment Agency on flood-risk grounds,

the local planning authority must follow the procedure provided for in the Town and Country Planning (Consultation) (England) Direction 2021. This can result in a referral to the Secretary of State for possible call-in.

LLFAs | LLFAs have the lead operational role in managing the risk of flooding from surface water and groundwater. In areas with no district or borough council, they also have the lead role in managing flood risk from ‘ordinary watercourses’, for example any watercourse that isn’t a main river.

They can be either unitary authorities or county councils.

They prepare and maintain a preliminary flood risk assessment, flood hazard maps, flood risk maps and flood risk management plans

<!-- Part1 p.1073 -->

When considering proposals for major development the local planning authority will need to consult the lead local flood authority on surface water drainage.

District | Manage flood risk from ordinary watercourses, operate and maintain Councils | existing sea defences and carry out other work to manage flood risk from the sea.

They take flood risk into account when making decisions on development in their area

Internal | These are independent public bodies responsible for water level Drainage | management in low lying areas. They work in partnership with Boards | other authorities to actively manage and reduce the risk of flooding.

### Strategic Flood Risk Assessments

19. A Strategic Flood Risk Assessment (SFRA) is a study carried out by one or more local planning authorities to assess the risk to an area from flooding from all sources. An SFRA takes into account the impacts of climate change and assesses the cumulative impact that land use changes and development in the area will have on flood risk. It is developed in consultation with the EA, the LLFA and local stakeholders.

20. The SFRA is used to inform strategic policies including, among other things, the allocation of land in the local plan. The policies should consider cumulative impacts in, or affecting, local areas susceptible to flooding. The SFRA and policies should take account of advice from the EA and other relevant flood RMAs, such as lead local flood authorities and internal drainage boards.

21. At paragraph F5.3, the NPPF explicitly states that it is the SFRA that will provide the basis for applying the Sequential Test for both the plan and individual developments.

22. There are two levels of SFRA, which reflect the likely risk of flooding from all sources and development pressures. They are:

- Level 1 SFRAs represent the first stage in assessing strategic flood risk and identify where more detailed assessment may be necessary.

- Level 2 SFRA, where development cannot be allocated outside areas at risk of flooding, or it anticipated that applications may come forward in areas at risk of flooding.

<!-- Part1 p.1074 -->

23. More guidance about strategic FRAs can be found in the EA’s ‘How to prepare a strategic flood risk assessment’.

### EA’s Flood Map for Planning and the long-term flood risk service

24. The data shown on the flood map for planning should be used alongside the SFRA.

### Flood map for planning: ‘flood zones plus climate change’

25. There are two datasets shown on the flood map for planning.

- The first shows the risk of flooding from rivers and the sea in Flood Zones, both for the current day and with climate change, represented by ‘2070 to 2125’.

- A second set of maps shows the extent of surface water flooding according to an annual likelihood. *Note: This was updated on 28 May 2026 to add climate change extents and banded depth information. ‘Banded depths’ are the depth of water during different severities of flash flooding. This is intended to give greater focus to surface water flooding and on resilience and recovery measures rather than relying on traditional flood control structures.

26. Flood defences are ignored because they can fail and water could spill over the top if the water is deep enough.

27. Flood Zone 1 sites that are within the ‘Flood Zones plus climate change’ extent should be treated as if they were in Flood Zone 2 according to the EA’s guidance. These are shown in orange on the Flood Map for Planning.

### Long-term flood risk service

28. The PPG states that the service is a source of information to inform a site-specific FRA. However, it is clarified in the EA’s ‘Flood risk assessments: applying for planning permission’ that it should only be used to check the high level risk from groundwater or reservoirs. This mapping should not be used in development planning to assess the risk from rivers, the sea or surface water (from May 2026).

29. This mapping also ignores flood defences.

### Climate change

30. All assessments of flood risk should include an allowance for climate change. Further information about how climate change allowances should be applied can be found here: Flood risk assessments: climate change allowances

<!-- Part1 p.1075 -->

31. This explains how predictions of changes to peak river flow, rainfall intensity, sea level rise and offshore wind speed and extreme wave height should be applied for each river catchment.

### Minor, major and non-major development in relation to flood risk

32. Para 051 of the PPG provides the following definitions for the purpose of flood risk assessment:

Minor development | minor non-residential extensions (industrial/commercial/leisure etc): extensions with a floorspace not in excess of 250 square metres.

alterations: development that does not increase the size of buildings, e.g. alterations to external appearance.

householder development: for example, sheds, garages, games rooms etc. within the curtilage of the existing dwelling, in addition to physical extensions to the existing dwelling itself.

This definition excludes any proposed development that would create a separate dwelling within the curtilage of the existing dwelling (e.g. subdivision of houses into flats) or any other development with a purpose not incidental to the enjoyment of the dwelling.

Major development | ‘Major development’ is as defined by the Town and Country Planning (Development Management Procedure) (England) Order 2015 (Article 2).

Non-major development ‘Non major development’ is any development falling below the above thresholds but excluding minor development. For example, a planning application for 8 dwellings an office building creating 750 square metres of floor space, or a development with a site area of 0.4 hectares.

### Changes of use

33. Para 052 also explains that changes of use can increase the vulnerability of the development or result in occupation or use by people who are more vulnerable than the previous occupants/users to risks from flooding.

34. Older existing properties may not previously have been subject to a flood risk assessment and appropriate mitigation measures, or the nature or severity of the flood risk may have changed over time, requiring more appropriate mitigation. Even if a development’s vulnerability is not increasing, change of use

<!-- Part1 p.1076 -->

can often present an opportunity to improve the flood resilience of existing development.

### Permitted development rights and prior approval

35. A number of permitted development rights are subject to prior approval by the local planning authority in respect of flooding. Such applications for prior approval in areas of flood risk must be accompanied by a site-specific FRA.

36. As per section F4 of the NPPF, flooding prior approvals require consultation with the EA where the development is in an area within Flood Zone 2 or Flood Zone 3, or in an area within Flood Zone 1 which has critical drainage problems.

37. Rights that provide for hard surfaces, for both householders or business, require that provision is made for a permeable or porous surface to ensure that such works do not increase levels of surface water runoff.

### Assessment of vulnerability

38. There are five classes of flood risk vulnerability set out in Annex F, Table 2 of the NPPF and this should be referred to for a full list. In summary, these are:

- Essential infrastructure, including some transport and energy projects

- Highly vulnerable, including emergency infrastructure required during flooding, basement dwellings and caravans for permanent use

- More vulnerable, including dwelling houses, hospitals, care homes, caravans for temporary use and some non-residential uses

- Less vulnerable, including works that do not need to remain operational during flooding, shops, car parks

- Water compatible development, including pumping stations, sand and gravel workings, water-based activities, open space and recreation.

### Lifetime of development

39. Para 006 of the PPG states that residential development can be assumed to have a lifetime of at least 100 years, unless there is a specific justification for considering a different period. For example, this could be a time-limited planning condition.

40. The lifetime of a non-residential development depends on the characteristics of that development, but a period of at least 75 years is likely to form a starting point for assessment.

<!-- Part1 p.1077 -->

41. Where development has an anticipated lifetime significantly beyond 100 years such as some major infrastructure projects, or where it would create significant land-use change such as a new settlement or substantial urban extension, it may be appropriate to consider a longer period for the lifetime of development

42. Para 005 of the PPG sets out the following considerations when determining whether a proposed development will be safe for its lifetime:

- the characteristics of a possible flood event, including residual risks. For example, the type and source of flooding and frequency, depth, velocity, speed of onset and duration.

- the safety of people within a building if it floods. The safety of the people around the building. Including people who are less mobile. This includes the ability of users to safely access and exit a building during a design flood and to evacuate before an extreme flood (0.1% annual probability with allowance for climate change).

- structural safety of the buildings

- the impact of a flood on the essential services provided to or from a development.

43. Further advice is provided in para 050 where development must be time-limited and potentially relocated, for example because of coastal erosion or increased risk because of climate change.

### Reliance on flood risk management infrastructure

44. Where proposals are located in areas of flood risk which are protected by existing flood defences that seek to protect the site and/or the wider area, para 005 of the PPG states that FRAs should, where appropriate:

- identify how the infrastructure will be operated, funded and maintained

- ensure there is space for maintenance. This means that the FRA should demonstrate that the development would not impede or curtail the operation of the flood defence and ensure adequate space for maintenance of this.

- consider the consequences of it failing or the design standard being exceeded

- consider the likelihood of defences keeping pace with climate change

### Common issues with assessment of flood risk

### FRA is out of date

<!-- Part1 p.1078 -->

45. The FRA can become out of date because new information emerges, included updated EA flood risk maps. Where this has been raised and where flooding is a key determining issue in the appeal, it is recommended that the views of the parties (including the EA) on the implications of this are sought.’

### FRA uses the wrong modelling data

46. If one of the parties raises concerns that the wrong modelling data have been used, then it will be necessary to consult all parties to gather views on the significance of this.

47. If the consequences of erroneous modelling are likely to cause the outcome of the risk assessment to be worse and/or unacceptable then this would be a matter of considerable adverse weight, and it may be appropriate to dismiss the case on the basis that an adequate FRA must be submitted in areas at risk of flooding.

### FRA doesn’t include surface water risk

48. If the surface water risk has not been included and the omission not explained, then it may be appropriate to contact the parties to gather their views on this.

49. If such an assessment should have been provided then the lack of it would be a matter of considerable adverse weight, and it may be appropriate to dismiss the case on the basis that an adequate FRA must be submitted in areas at risk of flooding.

### The water level would be really low

50. The risk is a consequence of both the height and velocity of flood water. According to the Government’s guidance on flash flooding, shallow flood water (around 15cm or 6 inches) that does not look dangerous can knock a person over or conceal hazards. It only takes around 60cm (2 feet) of water to carry away a vehicle of any size.

51. There is nothing in the policy or guidance that absolves applicants from undertaking an FRA or sequential test because the water level would be low. However, the characteristics of a flood are relevant when assessing mitigation measures, including escape routes, under paragraph F7.1 and 2 of the NPPF.

### There would be an escape route

52. There is nothing in the policy or guidance that absolves applicants from undertaking an FRA or sequential test because there is an escape route.

53. However, the efficacy of an escape route is taken into account when assessing the safety of the site over its lifetime.

<!-- Part1 p.1079 -->

### Only the road floods

54. A road is built development. An FRA and sequential test are still necessary in these circumstances according to paragraph F5.2 of the NPPF.

55. The degree to which the site floods and the use of the road as an escape route are matters when assessing the safety of the site over its lifetime.

### The building is already there

56. Changes of use are not subject to the sequential test.

57. However, changes of use can increase the vulnerability of the development or result in occupation or use by people who are more vulnerable than the previous occupants/users to risks from flooding.

58. Older existing properties may not previously have been subject to a flood risk assessment and appropriate mitigation measures, or the nature or severity of the flood risk may have changed over time, requiring more appropriate mitigation.

59. Even if a development’s vulnerability is not increasing, change of use can often present an opportunity to improve the flood resilience of existing development.

### Extensions

60. Small extensions (less than 250 sqm) are not subject to the sequential test.

61. However, an FRA is still required, proportional to the degree of risk and sufficient to demonstrate that the requirements of paragraph F7.1 and 2 of the NPPF have been satisfied.

## National policy

62. The outline of the approach for individual developments in the NPPF is illustrated in the flowchart in Annex 1.

63. The general approach to decision-taking to flood risk is to:

- Assess flood risk

- Avoid flood risk by applying the Sequential and Exception Tests as appropriate, then

- Manage and mitigate any unavoidable risk, including by ensuring development is flood resilient and resistant, safe and will not increase flood risk overall

64. The PPG states that ‘even where a flood risk assessment shows the

### development can be made safe throughout its lifetime without increasing

risk elsewhere, the Sequential Test still needs to be satisfied’. No matter how much detail an appellant may provide to prove that a proposed development would be safe from flooding for its lifetime, if it is at flood risk you must apply the Sequential Test first.

### Outline of the Sequential Test

65. Paragraph F5.1 of the NPPF states that the aim of the Sequential Test is to steer new development to areas with the lowest risk of flooding from any source.

66. Para 024 of the PPG explains that the Sequential Test ensures that a sequential, risk-based approach is followed to steer new development to areas with the lowest risk of flooding, taking all sources of flood risk and climate change into account. Where it is not possible to locate development in low-risk areas, the Sequential Test should go on to compare reasonably available sites:

- within medium risk areas; and

- then, only where there are no reasonably available sites in low and medium risk areas, within high-risk areas.

67. Initially, the presence of existing flood risk management infrastructure should be ignored, as the long-term funding, maintenance and renewal of this infrastructure is uncertain. Climate change will also impact upon the level of protection infrastructure will offer throughout the lifetime of development.

68. The Sequential Test should then consider the spatial variation of risk within medium and then high flood risk areas to identify the lowest risk sites in these areas, ignoring the presence of flood risk management infrastructure.

<!-- Part1 p.1081 -->

69. It may then be appropriate to consider the role of flood risk management infrastructure in the variation of risk within high and medium flood risk areas. In doing so, information such as flood depth, velocity, hazard and speed-of-onset in the event of flood risk management infrastructure exceedance and/or failure, should be considered as appropriate. Information on the probability of flood defence failure is unsuitable for planning purposes given the substantial uncertainties involved in such long-term predictions.

70. Paragraph F5.3 of the NPPF states that the strategic FRA will provide the basis for applying this test. The guidance does not suggest augmenting this with more up-to-date datasets. In these circumstances, para 180 of the NPPF says that the exception test may need to be reapplied for allocated sites.

71. Para 174 of the NPPF also states that development should not be permitted if there are reasonably available sites appropriate for the proposed development in areas with a lower risk of flooding.

### Why is a Sequential Test required?

72. PPG (para 023) states that this means ‘avoiding, so far as possible, development in current and future medium and high risk areas considering all sources of flooding including areas at risk of surface water flooding’.

73. The reason given for this is that it is the most effective way of addressing flood risk because it places the least reliance on measures like flood defences, flood warnings and property level resilience features.

74. In addition, application of the sequential approach in the plan-making and decision-making process will help to ensure that development is steered to the lowest risk areas, where it is compatible with sustainable development objectives to do so, and developers do not waste resources promoting proposals which would fail to satisfy the test (PPG para 023).

75. The PPG (paras 023 and 029) explain that application of this approach means that developers do not waste resources promoting proposals which would fail to satisfy the test. It emphasises that early engagement on the Sequential Test should avoid other work being undertaken unnecessarily.

76. This is not merely an administrative exercise. The costs and consequences of flooding are significant. This policy recognises the uncertainty associated with flood risk modelling and climate change, including over long-periods, and concludes that, if possible, such risks should be avoided. The Chapter 18 objective of the NPPF states that such development is ‘inappropriate’. It is only when it has been demonstrated that avoidance is not possible that flood management and mitigation are considered.

<!-- Part1 p.1082 -->

### When is a Sequential Test required?

77. Paragraph F5.2 of the NPPF states that a sequential risk-based approach should be taken to individual applications in areas known to be at risk now or in the future from any form of flooding. PPG para 027 states that the Sequential Test should be applied to ‘Major’ and ‘Non-major’ development proposed in areas at risk of flooding. Paragraph F5.2 of the NPPF sets out exemptions from the Sequential Test, which includes most minor development.

78. Paragraph F5.2(b) of the NPPF states that a Sequential Test is not required where a site-specific FRA demonstrates that no built development within the site boundary, including access or escape routes, land raising or other potentially vulnerable elements, would be located on an area that would be at risk of flooding from any source, now and in the future.

79. PPG paragraph 027 states that a ‘proportional approach’ should be taken to application of paragraph F5.2(b) when considering surface water flood risk. Where a site-specific FRA demonstrates clearly that the proposed layout, design, and mitigation measures would ensure that occupiers and users would remain safe from current and future surface water flood risk for the lifetime of the development, without increasing flood risk elsewhere, then the Sequential Test need not be applied. Note that this applies to surface water flood risk only; it does not apply to other forms of flooding, including rivers and the sea.

80. Under paragraph F5.2(c) of the NPPF, certain types of development that are listed are also exempt from a Sequential Test (and an Exception Test). Don’t forget that these sites must still meet the requirements for site-specific FRAs though. These are:

- Householder development

- Small, non-residential extensions (with a footprint of less than 250 m2)

- Changes of use, except to a caravan, camping or chalet site, or to a mobile home or park. There is further information on this category in para 014 of the PPG.

81. Paragraph F5.2(a) of the NPPF also states that where sites have already been allocated in the development plan through a Sequential Test, applicants need not apply the test again. However, the Exception Test may need to be re-applied if more recent information about flood risk has come to light.

<!-- Part1 p.1083 -->

82. Developers may refer to earlier judgments, including ‘Wathen-Faye v SoS’1 in which the judge accepted that a Sequential Test was not necessary for an area at risk of groundwater flooding. However, these cases rest on previous versions of the guidance and the findings no longer apply. The guidance is now explicit that the Sequential Test applies to all sources of flood risk.

83. Although it is not explicitly stated in the PPG, the ST should be applied even when only part of a site is identified at risk of flooding, except where a site-specific FRA demonstrates that no built development within the site would be located on an area.

### Area of the Sequential Test

84. According to the PPG (para 027a), the area of the test will be governed by local circumstances relating to the catchment area for the type of development and the needs it is proposing to address. For example, for a non-major housing development it would not usually be appropriate for the area of search to extend beyond the specific area of a town or city in which the proposal is located, or beyond an individual village and its immediate neighbouring settlements. Overall, the sequential test should be applied proportionately, focusing on realistic alternatives in areas of lower flood risk that could meet the same development need.

85. If there is disagreement about the area of the test the guidance in the PPG will be highly relevant. This should not be constrained by land ownership and should be proportionate to the scale of development proposed. For major housing development this may extend across a town, housing market area or District. Arguments that development is needed to sustain the existing community should be carefully scrutinised before accepting that sites outside them would not provide reasonable alternatives

86. Para 029 of the PPG states that the planning authority will need to determine an appropriate area of search, based on the development type and relevant spatial policies. The applicant will then need to identify whether there are any other ‘reasonably available’ sites within the area of search, that have not already been identified by the planning authority in site allocations or relevant housing and/or economic land availability assessments, such as sites currently available on the open market. The applicant may also need to check on the current status of relevant sites to determine if they can be considered ‘reasonably available’.

[^1]: Wathen-Fayed v Secretary of State for Levelling Up, Housing and Communities Case No: CO/3847/2021 & Substation Action Save East Suffolk Ltd v Secretary of State for Energy Security and Net Zero: CA-2023- 000087

<!-- Part1 p.1084 -->

87. A pragmatic approach should be taken to the availability of alternatives. The examples given in the PPG are:

- a small extension to existing premises where it may be impractical accommodate the additional space in an alternative location.

- where there are large areas in Flood Zones 2 and 3 (e.g. coastal towns and settlements on major rivers) and development is needed in those areas to sustain the existing community, sites outside them are unlikely to provide reasonable alternatives

- It may also, in some cases, be relevant to consider whether large scale development could be split across a number of alternative sites at lower risk of flooding, but only where those alternative sites would be capable of accommodating the development in a way which would still serve its intended market(s) as effectively.

88. Sites should be considered ‘reasonably available’ for the purposes of the Sequential Test if their location is suitable for the type of development proposed, they are able to meet the same development needs and they have a reasonable prospect of being developed at the same time as the proposal.

89. The Mead Realisations Ltd judgment [2024] EWHC 279 (Admin) (paragraph 100) rejected a highly specific approach whereby alternative sites would need to accommodate the proposed development in its various particulars, commenting that such an approach would render the Sequential Test ineffective.

90. Mead (paragraph 110) also clarified that a ‘series of smaller sites’ connotes a relationship between sites appropriate for accommodating the type of development which the decision-maker judges should form the basis for the sequential assessment. This addresses the concern that a proposal should not automatically fail the sequential test because of the availability of multiple, disconnected sites across a local authority’s area. The issue is whether they have a relationship which makes them suitable in combination to accommodate any need or demand to which the decision-maker decides to attach weight.

91. ‘Point in time’ was also covered in Mead. The Court commented that comparison of availability between two sites involves matters of degree. It does not require precise alignment (paragraph 121 of the judgment).

92. An appellant may apply a capacity threshold to a long list of candidate sites – for example rejecting sites that would be more than 25% smaller than the appeal site. A ‘series of smaller sites’ is considered in Mead, which says that this wording connotes a relationship between sites appropriate for accommodating the type of development which the decision-maker judges should form the basis for the sequential assessment.

<!-- Part1 p.1085 -->

93. In considering whether alternative lower-risk sites would be capable of accommodating the proposed development, such alternative sites do not need to be owned by the applicant to be considered ‘reasonably available’.

94. The EA provides guidance on searching for alternative sites, and the provision of information about potential alternative sites, but not on the extent of the search area.

95. For the schedule of sites, a good tip is to ask for a summary table with the appellant’s comments and the Council’s comments in adjoining columns, much as you might do for HLS sites.

### Lack of Sequential Test and paragraph S1 and S3 of the NPPF

96. The PPG (para 004) states that ‘where the Sequential and the Exception Tests have been applied as necessary and not met, development should not be allowed’. In this eventuality, those findings should be balanced against other considerations in your planning balance.

97. Paragraph F5.1 of the NPPF states that development should not be permitted if there are reasonably available sites in areas with a lower risk of flooding. If there is no Sequential Test, it is highly unlikely that it can be concluded that there would be no alternative sites. It follows from this that the lack of a Sequential Test is potentially a reason for refusal.

98. The alternative could be perverse; a developer not undertaking the test could be at an advantage. In addition, the lack of Sequential Test could lead to avoidance of the Exception Test, which is predicated on the Sequential Test. If the Exception Test is failed, then the PPG is clear that development should be refused (para 063), which must be weighed against the other considerations in the planning balance.

99. The PPG states that ‘flood risk’ is key to the application of the presumption in favour of sustainable development in paragraph S1 and footnote 24 of the National Planning Policy Framework.

100.Para 027 of the PPG specifically states that the absence of a 5-year housing land supply is not a relevant consideration in applying the Sequential Test for individual applications. However, housing considerations, including housing land supply, may be relevant in the overall planning balance, alongside the outcome of the Sequential Test.

### Application of the Exception Test

108.The Exception Test only applies to fluvial and tidal flooding.

109.There are three parts to the Exception Test, as set out in paragraph F6.1(b) of the NPPF, all of which must be satisfied for the development to be permitted:

- Provide wider sustainability benefits to the community that outweigh the flood risk;

- Be safe for its anticipated lifetime in accordance with policy F7; and

- Not increase flood risk elsewhere and, where possible, reduce flood risk overall.

110.For further information on how to demonstrate a reduction in the causes and impacts of flooding, see paras 062 to 067 of the PPG.

111.Para 037 states that to satisfy the Exception Test without an overall reduction in flood risk, it would need to be demonstrated that such measures cannot be identified or are unfeasible.

112.For essential infrastructure in Flood Zone 3a, Table 2 of the PPG additionally requires that it is designed and constructed to remain operational and safe in times of flood.

113.For essential infrastructure in Flood Zone 3b, Table 2 of the PPG additionally requires that it is designed and constructed to remain operational and safe in times of flood, does not result in a net loss of floodplain storage, and does not impede water flows and increase flood risk elsewhere.

### Wider sustainability objectives

114.Para 031 of the PPG says that it may be possible to move onto the Exception Test when the Sequential Test has failed (so there are sites that would be at lower risk) where, accounting for wider sustainable development objectives, application of relevant local and national policies would provide a clear reason for refusing development in any alternative locations identified.

115.The PPG para 036 advises that Local Planning Authorities should set their own criteria for assessing wider sustainability objectives, based on the sustainability appraisal in the Local Plan. Such appraisals are typically wide ranging and may not be up to date. Furthermore, the examples given in the PPG do not form a closed list. Accordingly, you will need to reach a judgement on whether the stated sustainability benefits are relevant in this case and the weight to be attributed to them. The PPG advises that the impacts of flood risk on social, economic and environmental factors should be considered. Therefore, arguments based solely on housing numbers without wider benefits should be carefully scrutinised, and are unlikely, on their own, to outweigh the flood risk.

116.The examples given include re-use of brownfield land as part of a regeneration scheme, an overall reduction in flood risk to the wider community, or the provision of SUDS that significantly exceed the NPPF policy requirements.

117.The benefits still need to be balanced against the flood risks, informed by the site-specific risk assessment.

118.If benefits are absent or outweighed by the flood risk, the Exception Test will not have been satisfied, and the development is contrary to national policy. The NPPF is clear that this is a strong reason for refusal which should be weighed in the planning balance.

### Not increasing the flood risk elsewhere

119. The requirements of paragraph F7.1 and F7.2 of the NPPF must be applied to all sites, including those exempt from undertaking the Sequential Test.

120. Paragraph F7.2 of the NPPF requires that developers demonstrate that:

a) Within the site, the most vulnerable development is located in areas of lowest flood risk, unless there are overriding reasons which justify a different arrangement;

b) The development will be safe throughout its lifetime taking account of the vulnerability of its users;

c) Any residual risk can be safely managed, and safe access and escape routes are included where appropriate, as part of an agreed emergency plan;

<!-- Part1 p.1089 -->

d) The development is appropriately flood resistant and resilient such that, in the event of a flood, it could be quickly brought back into use without significant refurbishment; and

e) It can be demonstrated that flood risk will not be increased elsewhere.

121. Para 004 specifically states that ‘tensions between flood risk mitigation measures and other planning matters, do not justify unsafe development’.

### Resistant and resilient construction

122. The PPG references the British Standard as describing suitable measures that can be taken to avoid flood risk. However, this was withdrawn in 2025 and the PPG has not been updated. You may get reference to the 2015 standard or its replacement, which only references resilience. Inspectors should be alert to this when assessing the measures proposed’

123. Inspectors should consider whether proposals for flood resistant or resilient development meets the test of being able to quickly bring the development back into use without significant refurbishment (Paragraph F7.2(d) of the NPPF).

124. The PPG (para 068) says that passive measures should be prioritised over active measures as they are likely to be more effective and more reliable.

### Residual risk

125. Residual risk comes in two main forms:

- from flood risk management infrastructure. Examples given in the PPG include breach of a flood defence, failure of pumped drainage, failure of a reservoir or a flood event that exceeds the design standard

- risk to a development once site-specific flood mitigation measures are taken into account, including safe access and escape routes. Examples given are the depth of internal flooding predicted after raising of floor levels, the hazard to which people would be exposed on escape routes after they have been raised, and or failure in flood warning.

126. Para 041 of the PPG states that where flood risk management infrastructure is likely to be improved to keep pace with climate change, the potential consequences of flooding resulting from breach or failure of that improved infrastructure is likely to be the main driver for mitigation. Para 005 of the PPG suggests that, if relying on flood defences, the likelihood of them keeping pace with climate change will also inform the nature of the residual risk. Where infrastructure is unlikely to be improved, the potential consequences of flooding resulting from overtopping or a breach will also be an important consideration.

<!-- Part1 p.1090 -->

127. Para 042 says that is not appropriate to rely solely on emergency plans to mitigate residual risk. More information on this is provided in para 048.

### Emergency plans

128. Para 043 of the PPG states that emergency plans are needed wherever emergency flood response is an important component of making a development safe. Emergency plans will be essential for sites at risk of flooding used for holiday or short-let caravans and camping and for any site with transient occupancy (e.g. hostels and hotels). Further details of the requirements for emergency plans can be found in Para 044 to 047 of the PPG, including for reservoir failure.

129. Paragraph F7.2(c) of the NPPF specifically requires that safe access and escape routes are included as part of ‘an agreed emergency plan’. It is not clear from the wording in the NPPF whether this means such plans should be provided as part of the application, rather than something that can be left to a condition, as has previously been done. In absence of further detail in the PPG, Inspectors should consider any submissions on this point and should assess whether in principle a plan could be agreed.

### Flood resistance and flood resilience

130. Flood resilience measures (also called recoverability measures, or wet-proofing), accept that water will enter the building, but through careful design and changes to the construction will minimise damage and allow faster cleaning, drying, repairing and re-occupancy of the building after a flood.

131. Flood resilience measures can use a combination of flood resistance and recovery measures. The industry-developed CIRIA Property Flood Resilience Code of Practice provides advice for both new-build and retrofit.

132. Flood resistance measures, or dry-proofing, stop water entering a building up to a safe structural limit. Resistance measures can be passive, such as flood doors which are normally closed; or active, such as air brick covers or removable flood barriers.

133. Further information on flood resistance and resilience can be found in PPG paras 068 to 069.

### Sustainable drainage systems

134. Sustainable drainage systems (SuDS) are an explicit requirement for major development and development in flood risk areas, unless there is clear evidence that this would be inappropriate (Paragraph F8 of the NPPF).

<!-- Part1 p.1091 -->

135. SuDS are designed to control surface water run off close to where it falls, combining a mixture of built and nature-based techniques to mimic natural drainage as closely as possible, and accounting for the predicted impacts of climate change. Many types of sustainable drainage systems are possible, contributing to reducing the causes and impacts of flooding.

136. The layout and function of drainage systems needs to be considered at the start of the design process for new development, as integration with road networks and other infrastructure can maximise the availability of developable land.

137. You should be satisfied that the minimum standards of operation for the proposed sustainable drainage system are appropriate, and that there are clear maintenance and adoption arrangements in place for the lifetime of the development.

138. Further information on SuDS, including the information required, can be found in PPG para 055 to 061.

## Conditions

139. The Sequential and Exception Tests are intended to establish whether the principle of development is acceptable in terms of flood risk. It is therefore unlikely that there will be circumstances where it would be appropriate to attach a condition requiring these tests to be carried out.

140. Case files often contain conditions recommended by the Environment Agency. Sometimes these are reflected in the conditions suggested by the LPA, and sometimes not. If you intend to allow the appeal you should consider all suggested conditions. However, in doing so apply the tests set out in the NPPF.
