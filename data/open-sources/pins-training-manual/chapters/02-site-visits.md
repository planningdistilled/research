## Introduction

1. This advice relates to site visits for appeals carried out by the Written Representations procedure. Separate advice is provided in ‘Hearings’ and in ‘Inquiries’ as there are some slight differences – although most of the principles set out here apply.

2. You should be aware of what the Procedural Guide – Planning appeals – England1 and the Guide to taking part in planning, listed building and conservation area consent appeals proceeding by written representations say about site visits. The parties may read these and will have a legitimate expectation that you will follow what is said. However, this chapter has taken into account the current versions of these documents.

3. The Procedural Guide(s) explains that the purpose of the site visit in written representations casework is solely to enable the site and its surroundings to be viewed. The advice is intended for Inspectors, their representative or anyone else representing PINS at a site visit but in the interests of brevity, references will generally only be to Inspectors.

## Site Visits

4. For most people the site visit will be the first, and possibly last, time they encounter an Inspector or their representative. Therefore, the way you conduct the site visit is extremely important.

5. Your site visit must be carried out in accordance with the Franks’ Principles (Openness, Fairness, and Impartiality) and the Conflicts of Interest policy. The advice in this guide will help you do this and further information about the Code and the Franks’ Principles can be found in the ITM Chapter ‘Role of the Inspector’.

6. You should always take your PINS ID card and have a supply of “Calling cards”. Make sure your car does not have any badges or stickers which might cause people to doubt your impartiality. For the same reason you should not wear or display anything that identify an organisation or society.

7. A site visit is not an opportunity for anyone present to discuss the details of the appeal or the written evidence they may have previously provided. The Inspector, or any PINS representative, should not allow any discussion about the case with anyone at a site visit. The only exception would be where the Inspector, or PINS representative, has asked any invited parties to point out physical features that they have referred to in their written evidence or to clarify other factual matters.

[^1]: See the Planning Inspectorate’s homepage on GOV.UK for more information.

<!-- Part1 p.123 -->

8. Regardless of the type of visit you are undertaking; you should not leave the site until you have seen everything you need to allow you to write a well-reasoned decision.

9. There are 3 types of site visit unaccompanied (USV), access required (ARSV) and accompanied (ASV). The majority are ARSV or USV. This chapter, therefore, first refers to these types of visit and the steps to be taken in advance of those visits. Information regarding ASVs is included at the end of the chapter.

Unaccompanied (USV)

10. The parties to the appeal will not attend and you will not normally be able to enter the appeal site because you will not have the appellant’s agreement to do so. You would normally only view the appeal site and its surroundings from the road, a public right of way or some other public vantage point and would not normally go onto neighbouring sites. It is important that as part of the visit you DO NOT go onto private land unless expressly authorised to do so.

11. If you are unable to see everything you need from public land in order to reach a sound decision you may need to abandon the site visit. You should inform the Case Officer straightaway and if it is possible for you to keep the case you should do so, otherwise the case will be re-allocated to another Inspector.

12. If you are approached by the appellant or neighbours during the USV, briefly and politely explain the purpose of the visit, note that you cannot listen to any comments or representations and that it is necessary for you carry out the visit unaccompanied. Do not get drawn into conversation about the appeal.

13. If, when carrying out a USV, you decide that it is essential to go on the appeal site, the Procedural Guide – Planning Appeals – England indicates that you can “approach the occupants to gain permission/access”. If you follow this approach you will need to explain very clearly the purpose of your visit and that you cannot enter into any dialogue.

14. PINS provides a ‘calling card’ for Inspectors to use where they were expecting access to be provided but the owner/occupier did not answer. The card is not meant to be used as a replacement for calling. Neither does the calling card replace any of the processes that should be undertaken after an Inspector informs the Case Officer that s/he was unable to complete the site visit.

Access Required (ARSV)

15. An ARSV will be arranged where you need to go onto the appeal site.

16. The principles are as follows:

- The Procedural Guide states that “If the appellant’s or agent’s presence is required at the appeal site it will be required solely to provide access to the site” and that “The local planning authority will not attend the site visit”.

<!-- Part1 p.124 -->

- The appellant will be told the day of the site visit and whether the Inspector or his/her representative will call in the morning or in the afternoon with the relevant 2 hour window slot. Wherever possible, you should arrange your visits so that you arrive in the first 30 minutes of the time slot.

- The appellant will be asked in advance to make arrangements for you to access the site. They may be present themselves; they may arrange for someone else to be present to allow access or they may provide a written agreement that you can go on the site (preferably beforehand in writing via the Case Officer or, exceptionally, by leaving a note pinned to the door).

- If the site is not land associated with a building or property it will be sensible to establish where the appellant or their representative will wait for you.

- In areas with limited parking facilities it may also be necessary to check in advance whether you can also park on the appellant’s land.

- When you arrive at the site you should always ring the doorbell2/knock on the door even if you think it would be possible to do the visit unaccompanied. This is because the appellant will be expecting you and may have waited in. Similarly, if the appellant is waiting somewhere in order to allow you access to the site.

- You should make it clear that the appellant’s attendance is only required to allow you to access the site. Politely discourage any attempt to engage in discussion about the appeal. However, you may wish to indicate that you have read all the representations made and are familiar with the case.

- If it has been arranged that you will also view from a neighbouring site – explain to the appellant that this will take place without them being present, that it is merely to allow you to view the relationship between the two sites and that there will be no opportunity for the neighbour to engage you in conversation or discussion.

- The appellant can be asked to wait inside while you carry out the site visit. However, to prevent accusations of theft, damage or interference with personal possessions, it is best to be accompanied by the appellant/their representative if you intend to enter any rooms inside the property.

17. On completion of the visit thank the appellant/their representative for providing access. If asked how long it will be until the decision is issued do not give a

[^2]: It is possible that an individual may rely upon a doorbell as an adaptive measure due to a sensory impairment (the doorbell may make lights flash or a device vibrate)

<!-- Part1 p.125 -->

timescale. If necessary, refer to the average time taken after the event or that it will be forthcoming as soon as possible.

18. If the appellant or their representative is not present:

- Has the appellant confirmed in writing that you can go on the site - either by leaving a note on site or, preferably, via the Case Officer?

- Are there are safeguarding concerns such as unattended minors on site? If yes, you should rearrange the visit for when an adult is present.

- If not, could you carry out the site visit unaccompanied without going on the appeal site? If so, you must inform the Case Officer.

- If you need to go on site, are you able to contact the appellant via the Case Officer (if you have time without delaying your programme)?

- If the appellant is not present and you need to go on the appeal site but you have no clear permission to do so - you will need to abandon the site visit and it will need to be rearranged. You should not go on to private land without express permission to do so. You should inform the Case Officer by email explaining the circumstances in order that they can confirm to the parties what happened.

- Where the site visit is abandoned and arrangements have been made to view the appeal site from a neighbouring property – you should visit the interested party and explain that the ARSV has been abandoned, and why, and that they will be advised of the new arrangements

- Alternatively, if you carry out the site visit unaccompanied and view the appeal site from the agreed neighbouring property you must inform the Case Officer.

- You should use the Calling Card if there is no answer

19. Viewing the appeal site from a neighbouring property

- The questionnaire asks the LPA if the Inspector will need to enter a neighbour’s land or property and asks for details of the address of that land or property. It is your decision as to whether it is necessary to do so. The question to ask yourself is whether it will help in reaching my decision and whether it would be prudent to do so given the cases made by either the LPA or the neighbour. In particular, if the LPA has requested a visit, if there are representations from the affected party and if the reasons for refusal concern the direct impact on that property, then it is advisable to view from the neighbouring property.

- Although usually picked up by Case Officers, it is also best practice to check the appeal questionnaire to see if the LPA has made such a request and that it has been actioned.

<!-- Part1 p.126 -->

- If you do need to visit a neighbouring property, it will be undertaken as ARSV and the Case Officer will notify the neighbour of the date and time (am or pm) of your visit.

- If an interested party has been asked to provide access you must ring the doorbell3/knock on their door even if you think it would be possible to do the visit unaccompanied. This is because the neighbour will be expecting you and may have waited in.

20. When you visit:

- briefly explain the purpose of your visit.

- politely discourage any attempt to engage you in conversation or discussion about the appeal.

- you then can ask the neighbour to wait inside while you carry out the site visit. However, to prevent accusations of theft, damage or interference with personal possessions, it is best to be accompanied if you intend to enter any rooms inside the neighbour’s property

21. If the neighbour is not present at the notified time:

- Complete your inspection from the appeal site and public land, along with any other visits arranged for the appeal.

- If you have enough information to make your decision inform the Case Officer.

- If you consider that it is essential to visit the neighbouring site provide the Case Officer with a clear explanation as to why so that they can to write to the parties to inform them the site visit will be re-arranged. If you have seen everything else at the site visit then you need only rearrange the visit to the neighbouring property.

22. If you consider it is essential to view from a neighbouring site in order to arrive at a sound decision and this has not been arranged beforehand you should inform your Case Officer and explain the reasons for requiring the site visit. However, before pursuing this option you should very carefully consider whether it really is necessary that you view from the neighbouring site. You should only do this if it is essential to allow you to make your decision. If you are satisfied that you can properly judge the effect of the proposal on neighbours from within the appeal site then there is no need to visit their properties (see Hallinan v SSE and Barnet LBC [1993] JPL 584).

[^3]: It is possible that an individual may rely upon a doorbell as an adaptive measure due to a sensory impairment (the doorbell may make lights flash or a device vibrate).

<!-- Part1 p.127 -->

23. If it is essential to do so a further visit will be scheduled in your programme where an appointment will be made to gain the necessary access to the relevant site(s).

## Taking photographs or video clips

24. It is up to you to decide whether you want to take photos/video to help you remember the site. However, make sure that this does not distract you from looking carefully at what you need to see when you are on site. It should not be a substitute for your own observations and on-site assessment.

25. If you intend to take photos/video you should ask the parties first and make sure they have no objections. Tell the parties that it is only to help you picture the site as an aide-mémoire.

26. If you do take any photos/video, they should be kept with your own notes as they could be the subject of a Freedom of Information request – they can be securely destroyed in line with PINS protocol after the High Court Challenge period has passed (see ‘Retention of Notes’ in the ATDM for further details)

27. In line with good safeguarding practise, you must avoid photographs/video where children or potentially vulnerable people are in the picture, or a washing line is up with underwear on the line, for example.

28. You must only use your PINS issued device for any photo/video taking.

## Health and safety when carrying out site visits

29. The PINS Policy statement on health and safety is as follows:

‘The Planning Inspectorate is committed to the protection of the health safety and welfare of all our employees, our customers, the public and all persons working under the control of the organisation. Securing this commitment is an important management objective that contributes to business performance.’

30. For salaried Inspectors information and advice is provided on the Intranet about ‘Health and Wellbeing’. In particular, see the ‘Health and Safety Training Guides’. This provides links to training modules and risk assessments relating to the conduct of site visits, driving safely for work and working remotely in safety.

31. The Inspector guidance explains that you should carry out a ‘dynamic risk assessment’ when undertaking site visits. This is because you have a responsibility to take reasonable care for your own health, safety and welfare as well as those around you who may be affected by your acts or omissions.

32. For PADS: their companies or, in the case of PADS who are sole traders, the PADS, themselves, have responsibility for managing their own health and safety. In deciding what measures are necessary PADS may wish to consider the guidance for salaried inspectors set out in the paragraphs that follow. Further information for PADS is provided in the General Terms and Conditions and in the PADS Notes.

<!-- Part1 p.128 -->

33. Some key points to consider are set out below. When travelling to and from site visits:

- Don’t increase the risks from the normal hazards of driving by working or driving for excessive periods of time. A working period of 10 hours in a day is a reasonable maximum for Inspectors travelling to and from site visits by car. If you cannot carry out your site visits in one 10 hour day then book an overnight stay in a hotel and travel down the day before or split your site visits over two days.

- Don’t rush to get to site visits if you are late. Contact the Case Officer to let them know how late you may be so they can inform the parties. You should always drive safely.

- Always consider postponing a journey when the weather is bad. If so, contact the Case Officer so that they can inform the parties.

- If you feel it would be unsafe to use public transport or walk (perhaps because of an inner city or remote location or due to the time of day), it is reasonable to use a taxi and to ask the driver to wait until you have completed the visit. Remember to get a receipt.

- If you use a hire car, take time to familiarise yourself with the controls and to adjust the driving position.

34. When carrying out the site visit:

- Be aware of any advance warning of potential risks which have been placed on file for the appeal, such as animals/chemicals on site. Might you need any protective clothing/equipment (PPE)?

- If you visit a school, construction site, factory/warehouse, quarry, waste operations site, nursing home, hospital or similar, always report to the site office/reception and follow any safeguarding and/or health and safety instructions, including in respect of PPE.

- Consider any risks and how you might deal with them. For example, are there any hazardous buildings/structures? Is there any moving machinery or vehicles? Will you be checking visibility splays at a junction or working on a busy highway or one without pavements? Is there a possibility of animal attack? What are the ground conditions? Are there any issues relating to bio-security (for example, when visiting farms)4?

- Is any protective clothing necessary? Do you need a hard hat, high visibility jacket or safety shoes/boots. Salaried Inspectors can order these from PINS here.

[^4]: See the DEFRA publication on ‘Biosecurity Guidance to Prevent the Spread of Animal Diseases’

<!-- Part1 p.129 -->

- If you feel uncomfortable about the situation that you are entering into, do not carry on with the visit or that part of it. This might involve circumstances where you are being asked to climb scaffolding, stepladders or go onto unprotected roofs. Only carry out a site visit if you think it is safe to do so.

- Take shelter if the weather is bad.

35. When conducting site visits you will be working alone:

- Salaried Inspectors are provided with a lone worker protection system (Orbis) via a mobile handset. Guidance on its use can be found on the Orbis Red Alert - Lone Worker Protection System section on the Intranet.

- Failure to use Orbis is a disciplinary matter.

- It is good practise to tell someone at home where you are going and what time you expect to be back. If this is not possible consider asking someone else in PINS to fulfil this role. In addition, make sure you have phone numbers for your Case Officer/Team Leader and your line manager.

36. All Inspectors, whether salaried or non-salaried, should always report accidents, dangerous occurrences or near misses to PINS. This can allow lessons to be learnt and may help prevent such problems arising in future. To report an accident or potential incident, salaried Inspectors should follow the guidance on the intranet and inform your IM or SIT. PADS should inform CMU.

## Before the site visit

37. Your forthcoming programmes of written representation cases will be available to view in your worklist on Manage Appeals.

38. When you first receive the case assignment – check the following and take up any problems with the Case Officer straightaway:

- Do you have any potential conflicts of interest? (see ‘'Conflicts of Interest’)

- Is the case suitable for the Written Representations procedure? (see ‘Role of the Inspector’)

- Are there any safeguarding issues? If you have any safeguarding concerns, advice is contained in the Approach to Decision Making chapter

39. Contact the Case Officer immediately if you are unable to carry out a site visit because of a conflict of interest or you feel a change in procedure is required. Email the Case Officer explaining the circumstances.

40. If the cases are suitable, you should email the Case Officer (via their Team email address) with the timings of your site visits as soon as possible and, preferably, not later than three weeks before the date of the site visit. For ARSVs you should

<!-- Part1 p.130 -->

provide a one or two hour time slot. For ASVs you should provide a precise time. For health and safety reasons, you should also note the dates you intend to carry out USVs. This will ensure the information is pulled through to your decision template.

41. When timing site visit programmes:

- Make sure you leave enough time to conduct the site visit without being rushed and to travel safely to the next site visit. As a rough guide, a straightforward site visit relating to a smaller case (for example, a house extension) might usually take around 15-20 minutes.

- If the site is very large, set out where you will meet the parties.

- Check for any information provided by the Case Officer which indicates that you might need to allow more time for any site visits (for example if the LPA has requested that you view from a large number of neighbouring properties)

- Tools such as Google Maps and Bing Maps can help you work out how long it will take to travel between sites, and where car parking might be available in the area. However, allow enough time to cope with potential traffic delays, your unfamiliarity with an area and finding somewhere to park.

- Allow for short winter days and longer journeys in rush hours and school traffic.

- Try to work out where you will park if the site is in a city/town centre or any area where parking is likely to be restricted. Public transport can be the best solution in such areas.

- Check to make sure that the programme of visits is practical.

- Think carefully about how many site visits you can reasonably do in one day. If you have a programme of several visits, do you need to consider an overnight stay either by travelling the day before or splitting the visits across two days? On health and safety grounds a working day of visits and travel of more than 10 hours should be avoided.

42. The parties may ask you to view the site at a particular time or day of the week but it is for you to decide if this is necessary. Is the case one where you could reasonably use your experience and judgement to assess the effects of a proposal even if you do not visit at the suggested time? If so, you should provide the Case Officer with a written explanation as to the reasons why. However, if the request can be easily accommodated into your programme, then it is good practice to do so.

43. The site visit is your opportunity to see the site and its surroundings and to assess the significance of what has been set out in the written representations.

<!-- Part1 p.131 -->

44. Before you carry out the site visit – have you:

- Checked that relevant parties have been informed of the date and time?

- Worked out where you will meet the parties and where you might park?

- Considered where toilet and other breaks might be taken?

- Made sure you understand the proposal and the main issues and have identified the relevant plans?

- Made a list of everything you want to see on the site visit, including in the surrounding area – and anything you want to check with the parties (for example, in relation to physical features)?

- Made a note of any interested parties who you have been asked to call upon?

- Identified any missing documents (policies, conservation area plans, interested party representations etc.) and asked the Case Officer to secure them?

- Got your note taking device, plans, a contact number for the Case Officer, ID card, calling cards, sat nav and maps?

## Transport

45. You are responsible for organising your own transport when undertaking site visits. This can be public transport modes or via a car. The Travel and Subsistence Policy and the Intranet has more information about booking through Enterprise or through CTM.

46. Wherever possible, it is best to use your own transport to travel to any other sites that you have been requested to view. However, in ASV cases sometimes it may be more practical to accept a lift - for example if there are a number of sites and there are good reasons why the parties should accompany you. If you accept a lift, you should ensure that you are accompanied by someone representing the LPA and the appellant.

47. There may also be occasions where the appellant will need to arrange transport

- for example, where the site is very large or if it is a long distance away from any roads and specialist 4x4 transport may be required. Where possible, it is best to arrange this in advance.

## Representations and late evidence

48. You should firmly resist accepting any evidence or revised plans which you may be offered at the site visit. This is to avoid any accusations of unfairness. At the site visit, depending on the circumstances, you might advise that:

<!-- Part1 p.132 -->

- evidence should be submitted on time unless there are exceptional circumstances

- you cannot accept any evidence on site

- if someone wishes to submit additional evidence they should be told to contact the Case Officer in writing, as soon as possible, to explain why late evidence is being submitted but you should not give any indication that it will be accepted.

49. You can find further information on late evidence in ‘The Approach to Decision Making’ ITM.

## Requests to view other sites in the area

50. Sometimes you will be asked by the main parties to view other sites in the area, for example where it is argued that similar developments have been carried out. The extent to which you comply with such requests is for you to decide. However, it is good practise to visit sites that are reasonably close to the appeal site, if locational details have been provided which allow you to find them without undue searching and if they appear likely to have a direct bearing on the outcome of the appeal.

51. Any other sites should only be viewed from public places. The ‘Approach to Decision Making’ ITM also provides further advice about dealing with other developments and decisions as material considerations in your reasoning.

### Accompanied (ASV)

52. The Procedural Guide – Planning appeals – England states that:

“In some circumstances we may deem it necessary for the Inspector or his/her representative to be accompanied by both the appellant (or agent) and a representative of the local planning authority and, where appropriate, interested people.”

53. This may apply to contentious cases; where there are known H&S issues, individuals who are known to be difficult or vexatious, or to ensure fairness and transparency of process.

54. A site visit is not an opportunity for anyone present to discuss the merits of the appeal or the written evidence they may have previously provided. The Inspector, or any PINS representative, should not allow any discussion about the case with anyone at a site visit. The only exception would be where the Inspector, or PINS representative, asks the invited parties to point out physical features that they have referred to in their written evidence or to clarify other factual matters.

55. You should always aim to arrive on time. However, if you are delayed:

- Are you able to contact the Case Officer so they can attempt to let the parties know your estimated arrival time?

<!-- Part1 p.133 -->

- Will you still have time to see what you need at the site visit and to get to any subsequent sites safely and on time? If not, could you visit the site later in the day if the parties are willing to do so? Alternatively, do you need to cancel the site visit? When it is safe to do so, contact the Case Officer who will attempt to contact the parties.

56. If you arrive early:

- Wherever possible avoid waiting outside the site. If you have travelled by car, park around a corner or further down the street - unless parking on the appeal site is unavoidable – but, if so, seek the appellant’s permission.

- Take the opportunity to look at the wider area and to visit any sites and developments which have been referred to by the parties.

57. When arriving for the site visit:

- Arrive at the arranged time or just 1 or 2 minutes early.

- Arrive on your own. Inspectors and LPA officers seen arriving together has been identified by appellants as a perceived indication of unfairness and lack of impartiality.

- If the LPA representative is waiting alone outside the site, consider asking them to go on ahead to check if the appellant is on site.

- If a main party is late, do not wait with the party who has arrived. Wait at a safe distance and do not enter into conversation, beyond explaining that you will return when everyone is present.

58. At the start of the site visit:

- Introduce yourself.

- Check who is present – attempt to locate any missing parties you are expecting. It is good practise to make a note of the names of those present and who they are representing.

- If hands are shaken – make sure you shake hands with everyone (so you are seen to be fair and impartial).

- Explain that the purpose is for you to see the site and surroundings and that you cannot listen to any representations/discussion/arguments - but that the parties can point out physical features. If necessary, remind the parties of this during the site visit.

- Confirm that you have seen and read the statements made by the parties and, if necessary, the representations made by interested parties.

- Explain how you will deal with any requests from interested parties to attend the site visit or view from their property.

<!-- Part1 p.134 -->

- Explain the order of your site visit (for example, when you will view from neighbouring properties, if you intend to carry out any part of the visit unaccompanied or if you have already visited other sites or locations unaccompanied).

- If you have already met the LPA representative or the appellant’s agent at a previous site visit that day – make this clear to the other parties and explain that you have no other connection with that person.

59. If interested parties have requested that you view from their properties then consider:

- If they are not present at the start of the visit go and ring their doorbell5/knock at their door.

- Explain that interested parties can only go on the appeal site if the appellant agrees. This is because the site will usually be private property with no general right of access. In some cases there may also be health and safety or insurance reasons why it would not be appropriate for interested parties to go on to the site. If the appellant denies access, you may need to explain to the interested party that you have no power to compel access. You can also reiterate that the purpose of your site visit is to see the site and surroundings, that you cannot listen to any representations and that you will be accompanied by the LPA as well as the appellant.

- If the neighbour refuses to allow the appellant or their agent onto their land would they allow you to go on their land unaccompanied? Would the other parties be agreeable to this? Would the parties be able to have a clear view of you from the appeal site or the road?

- Explain when you will visit neighbouring properties although this should be after you have inspected the appeal site. If necessary, you can then suggest that the neighbour returns to their property while you visit the appeal site itself.

- You should not enter neighbouring land if the site owner/occupier or their representative is not present unless you have received advanced written authority to do so. Therefore, could you see everything you need to from the appeal site? If you cannot see what you need to, the site visit will have to be re-arranged (through your Case Officer). Explain this to the main parties. In practise, this is likely to be a rare occurrence.

60. During the site visit:

<!-- Part1 p.135 -->

- Be polite – but make sure you are also firm and authoritative.

- Never allow yourself to be left alone with any of the parties.

- The parties do not need to follow you around. It can often be best to ask them to wait at a particular point while you see what you need to.

- Turn down all offers of hospitality. You should not use any facilities, including a construction site Portaloo, unless it is an emergency.

- Politely avoid getting drawn into any conversations about the case or other matters - remarks that may seem harmless could be misrepresented (for example, avoid commenting on how lovely the site is or the view, or how difficult it was to park, especially if there are highway matters in dispute).

- You can ask the parties to confirm particular features which have been referred to in written statements (for example a particular property or tree or the location of a Conservation Area or Green Belt boundary) – but frame any questions neutrally.

- If it is necessary to check any measurements – ask the parties to do this and to agree the figure.

- Make sure you take into account any mobility difficulties of those attending.

- If the weather is poor, check that the parties are content that you continue. In extreme circumstances you may need to delay or abort the visit.

61. At the end of the site visit:

- It can be helpful to ask the parties if they are content that you have seen everything and if there is anything else they wish to point out.

- If asked how long it will be until the decision is issued do not give a timescale. If necessary, refer to the average time taken after the event or that it will be forthcoming as soon as possible.

- Thank everyone and make sure you are the first to leave. Do not leave the site with anyone else.

62. If one of the main parties fails to attend an ASV:

- Wait for about 5 minutes to see if they arrive.

- If they don’t arrive, try to contact them to find out if they are on the way (via the Case Officer) or you can ask the main party who is present to try to contact them direct).

<!-- Part1 p.136 -->

- Explain how long you can wait. You need to leave enough time to be able to arrive at your next site visit on time having travelled safely.

- Wait separately from any parties who are present. Make any necessary conversations as brief as possible and do not get drawn into any discussions.

63. If the missing party cannot be contacted or cannot attend or would not be able to arrive in time – consider the following options:

- Could you carry out the visit unaccompanied – can you see everything you need to from public land? If so, explain this to those present and ask them to leave so you can carry out an unaccompanied visit

- If the appellant is present - you can go on to the appeal site provided they give their permission. However, you will need to carry out the visit without their presence with you so will need to ask the appellant to wait inside or leave the site. This will convert the visit to become an ARSV. You will also need to ask any interested parties to leave

- If the appellant is not present - and you need to go onto the appeal site it is likely that you will need to abandon the site visit.6 If so, inform the Case Officer with an email explaining the circumstances. However, in some cases, the appellant may give oral consent for you to go on the site over the phone (via the Case Officer or the LPA officer) – so allowing you to go onto the site unaccompanied. However, you should only exercise this option if you are certain that permission has been given and that it would be safe to go on the site unaccompanied - ask the Case Officer to get confirmation from the appellant in writing after the visit. You will then need to ask the LPA and any other parties to leave.

- Where the site visit is abandoned and requests have been made to

view the appeal site from a neighbouring property - you should explain to the interested party (visiting any interested parties if they are not present) that the site visit has been abandoned, and why, and that they will be advised of the new arrangements.

- Post-event actions - If you carry out the visit unaccompanied (ASV/ARSV to USV) or because there was a change in procedure from ASV to ARSV you must inform the Case Officer.

64. If none of the parties attend:

## Appeal dismissed for undue delay

65. Section 79(6A) of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 gives the Secretary of State the authority, after issuing a warning notice, to dismiss an appeal if any of the parties is responsible for undue delay in its progress. “Undue delay”, in terms of the site visit, is most likely to occur due to the appellant or their representative’s failure to attend the arranged site visits without providing a satisfactory reason.

66. If at least 2 previous site visits have been abandoned (whether they were due to be conducted by you or a previous Inspector) due to the behaviour of the parties, you should consider the reasons put forward for the delay. If you believe, on balance, that the delay is being caused unduly, wilfully or deliberately, you should contact your Case Office to enact the s79(6A) process.

67. The Case Officer, via their Desk Instructions, has access to the suite of letters needed to complete the process before the appeal can be finally dismissed. You may need to provide some wording for the letters on a case-by-case basis.
