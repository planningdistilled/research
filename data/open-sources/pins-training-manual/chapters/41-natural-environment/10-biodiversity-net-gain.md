## Biodiversity Net Gain

216. The content of this section is provisional and subject to change as we accumulate experience of dealing with the consequences of mandatory BNG. As such, you should ensure that you regularly review this guidance until matters become more settled. There is also a suite of external guidance that sets the broader context and is therefore able to inform your decision-making. The following now applies to all major development submitted after the 4 February 2024 and all minor development

217. The relevant primary legislation for the statutory framework for biodiversity net gain is principally set out under Schedule 7A (Biodiversity Gain in England) of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990. This legislation was inserted into the 1990 Act by Schedule 14 of the Environment Act 2021 and was amended by the Levelling Up and Regeneration Act 2023. The Biodiversity Gain (Town and Country Planning) (Consequential Amendments) Regulations 2024 made consequential amendments to other parts of the 1990 Act. There are separate provisions governing the Biodiversity Gain Plan (BGP). To ensure applicants are clear about this distinction, the LPA must not include the BNG condition in the list of conditions imposed in the written notice when planning permission is granted. However, if an appeal is allowed it is good practice to alert the appellant to the deemed condition as part of a closing paragraph.

Due to the provisions of Schedule 7 of the 1990 Act and as none of the statutory exemptions apply in this case, the development may not be begun unless a biodiversity gain plan has been submitted to and approved by the local planning authority.

OR

Based on the information available this permission is considered to be one which will not require the approval of a biodiversity gain plan before development is begun because a statutory exemption/transitional arrangement applies.

218. Local authorities may request that additional conditions are attached in relation to the biodiversity gain plan, including those relating to implementation and/or a completion report. However, the local planning authority may only approve the plan if it is satisfied, amongst other things, that the biodiversity gain objective is met (paragraph 15 (2) (e) of Schedule 7A). Furthermore, although not expressly stated, there is no reason why the local planning authority could not, if necessary, attach conditions when approving the plan. Such matters may also be incorporated into an accompanying legal agreement. Given that the approval of the biodiversity gain plan is outside the scope of a regular appeal, imposing conditions of this type should be avoided as unnecessary.

219. The refusal or non-determination of such plans are now subject to appeals under s78(1)(b) of the TCPA. Appeals are also possible under s73, although the scope of these are constrained by Regulation 7 of the Biodiversity Gain (Town and Country Planning) (Consequential Amendments) Regulations 2024. We have yet to receive any appeal through either route. This section will be updated and training will be provided, if required, after the initial cases have been undertaken. Other provisions relating to s77 and s62A of the TCPA also apply. A number of s62A applications have

220. Under the statutory framework for biodiversity net gain, subject to some exceptions, every grant of planning permission is deemed to have been granted subject to the condition that the biodiversity gain objective is met. This objective requires development to deliver at least a 10% increase in biodiversity value relative to the pre-development biodiversity value of the onsite habitat. This increase can be achieved through onsite biodiversity gains, registered offsite biodiversity gains or statutory biodiversity credits. As these provisions only relate to habitats and you will still need to consider whether additional conditions are necessary for any species of principal importance or protected species that may be present. See the relevant section of this chapter for further details.

221. The biodiversity gain condition does not apply to the following types of development:

- Development which is subject of a householder application as defined within Article 2(1) of the Town and Country Planning (Development Management Procedure) (England) Order 2015;

- Development granted planning permission by a development order under section

59. This includes permitted development rights;

- Development subject to the de minimis exemption which does not impact a priority habitat, less than 25 square metres (e.g. 5m by 5m) of onsite habitat and 5 metres of linear habitats such as hedgerows;

- Self-build and custom build development116 which: o consists of no more than 9 dwellings, and o is carried out on a site which has an area no larger than 0.5 hectares, and o consists exclusively of dwellings which are self-build or custom housebuilding as defined in section 1(A1) of the Self-build and Custom Housebuilding Act 2015.

- Urgent Crown development granted permission under section 293A of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990;

- Development of a biodiversity gain site which is undertaken solely or mainly for the purpose of fulfilling, in whole or in part, the biodiversity gain condition which applies in relation to another development; and

- Development forming part of, or ancillary to, the high speed railway transport network comprising connections between all or any of the places or parts of the transport network specified in section 1(2) of the High Speed Rail (Preparation) Act 2013.

222. Biodiversity net gain also does not apply to:

- Retrospective planning permissions made under section 73A; and

- Section 73 permissions where the original permission which the section 73 relates to was either granted before 12 February 2024 or the application for the original permission was made before 12 February 2024.

223. Biodiversity net gain has not been commenced yet for planning permissions which have been granted through other routes. These include:

- Local development orders;

- Simplified Planning Zones;

- Neighbourhood development orders;

- Successful enforcement appeals; and

- Deemed planning permission.

224. The grant of permission in principle is not within the scope of biodiversity net gain because it is not a grant of planning permission. However, the subsequent technical details consent, as a grant of planning permission, are subject to the biodiversity gain condition. The approval of reserved matters for outline planning permissions is also not subject to the biodiversity gain condition because it is not a grant of planning permission.

225. Where applicants consider that the development would not be subject to the biodiversity gain condition, Article 7 of The Town and Country Planning (Development Management Procedure) (England) Order 2015 provides that the applicant must provide a statement as part of the planning application setting out the reasons why they believe this is the case.

One of the commonly claimed exemptions is for custom and self-build housing, although the Government will remove this on 6 August 2026. In the meantime, a s106 obligation is likely to be the most appropriate method of ensuring that the development is self or custom build housing rather than market housing. 227. If there is no valid exemption, then the development will be subject to the biodiversity gain condition and the minimum information requirements. In such circumstances, you would dismiss the appeal because the biodiversity gain objective would not be met and there is insufficient information. This is not the same as an invalid appeal where the appellant has simply failed to meet the minimum information requirements. In these instances, there will have been a validation failure at both the application and appeal stages. Such cases will be very infrequent.

228. If the exemption statement is missing and the minimum information has not been provided, the appeal will be invalid (as was the original application). Although the case team should have dealt with this matter, you may find that some cases still get through. If this is the case then you should go back to the parties stating why you think it is invalid, (what information is missing, for example). This can prompt the claim of an exemption. A template for the letter and some decisions that deal with invalid appeals are available from the Knowledge Team.

229. Where an applicant believes the development would be subject to the biodiversity gain condition, the application must be accompanied by minimum information set out in Article 7 of The Town and Country Planning (Development Management Procedure) (England) Order 2015:

- Confirmation that the applicant believes that planning permission, if granted, the development would be subject to the biodiversity gain condition;

- The pre-development biodiversity value(s), either on the date of application or earlier proposed date (as appropriate);

- Where the applicant proposes to use an earlier date, this proposed earlier date and the reasons for proposing that date;

- The completed metric calculation tool showing the calculations of the pre-development biodiversity value of the onsite habitat on the date of application (or proposed earlier date) including the publication date of the biodiversity metric used to calculate that value;

- A statement whether activities have been carried out prior to the date of application (or earlier proposed date), that result in loss of onsite biodiversity value (‘degradation’), and where they have:

- A statement to the effect that these activities have been carried out;

- The date immediately before these activities were carried out;

- The pre-development biodiversity value of the onsite habitat on this date;

- The completed metric calculation tool showing the calculations, and

- Any available supporting evidence of this;

- A description of any irreplaceable habitat (as set out in column 1 of the

230. If this information has not been provided, the local planning authority should refuse to validate the application. Within the planning application form, applicants will be asked to confirm whether this information accompanies the application. However, experience shows that validation can still occur in the absence of this information which means that it can be missing at the appeal stage. If this is the case, you should find the appeal invalid and take no further action.

231. The PPG (Paragraph: 019 Reference ID: 74-019-20240214) indicates that it would generally be inappropriate for decision makers, when determining a planning application for a development subject to biodiversity net gain, to refuse an application on the grounds that the biodiversity gain objective will not be met. However, that does not affect the need to be satisfied that there is a valid exemption in place, as referred to in paragraph 222 above.

232. The PPG goes on to say that decision makers may need to consider more broadly whether the biodiversity gain condition is capable of being successfully discharged. The matters referred to in the PPG (and others) may be raised in representations but there is no policy or statutory requirement for an Inspector to assess or settle these matters at appeal stage. For example, issues relating to the quality and extent of the minimum information that has been supplied, such as the quantification of the baseline, or the location of off-site gains, are matters to be resolved through the discharge of the condition. This is because and planning permission cannot begin until a BGP has been approved in writing by the LPA and the BGP should include the pre-development biodiversity value of the on-site habitat as per paragraph 14(2) of Schedule 7A of the Act.

233. A planning condition can be used to secure significant onsite habitat enhancements which are required to be secured and maintained for at least 30 years under paragraph 9 of Schedule 7A. Other potential conditions could include monitoring and reporting arrangements. Planning conditions should be appropriately worded to ensure effective compliance and enforcement of biodiversity net gain. However, in the case of small-scale sites such conditions may not be appropriate and should be carefully considered. If you are allowing an appeal where there are significant on-site enhancements, it may be appropriate to consider the following condition, or something similar, if it has not already been suggested:

The development shall not commence until a Habitat Management and Monitoring Plan (the HMMP), prepared in accordance with the approved Biodiversity Gain Plan has been submitted to, and approved in writing by, the local planning authority and including:

a) a non-technical summary;

234. What counts as a significant enhancement will vary depending on the scale of development and existing habitat, but these would normally be:

- Habitats of medium or higher distinctiveness in the biodiversity metric;

- Habitats of low distinctiveness which create a large number of biodiversity units relative to the biodiversity value of the site before development;

- Habitat creation or enhancement where distinctiveness is increased relative to the distinctiveness of the habitat before development;

- Areas of habitat creation or enhancement which are significant in area relative to the size of the development; or

- Enhancements to habitat condition, for example from poor or moderate to good.

235. Examples of significant enhancements include creating a wildflower meadow or a nature park. Non-significant enhancements are habitat enhancements whose loss will not significantly decrease the development’s biodiversity value but which are nevertheless included in the metric calculations.

236. The other way in which BNG considerations have affected general s78 and s62A casework is through the weight to be afforded to different levels of gain in the planning balance. Holgate LJ helpfully sets out the approach to be taken to applications made

237. It is sometimes suggested that where a development makes provision for something which is required by a policy or by legislation, that it cannot be regarded as a benefit at all. It should be noted that there is no legal principle which supports statements of this kind and that the assessment of how much weight to give to a benefit is a matter of planning judgement.

238. In principle, the same is true of a decision as to whether a measure constitutes a benefit (or a disbenefit or a neutral factor). But all such judgments are subject to the application of public law principles by judicial review or statutory review under s288 of the TCPA. Those principles place the following obligations on us:

i. to take into account considerations that are mandated by legislation or which are “obviously material”;

ii. to disregard legally irrelevant considerations; and

iii. to avoid irrationality in reaching findings and conclusions. One type of irrationality is a conclusion which is so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could ever have come to it, or a decision which is outside the range of reasonable decisions open to a decision-maker. Another type of irrationality involves a demonstrable flaw in the reasoning of the decision-maker, including a serious logical or methodological error.

239. Where a development is required to provide a measure in order to overcome, mitigate or compensate for a harm, such a measure could not rationally be described as a benefit. For example, it is not a benefit if a development would result in a loss of biodiversity and the provision of additional biodiversity on the same site or on other land nearby would completely offset that loss. In overall terms there would be no net reduction in biodiversity attributable to the development and hence no benefit. Holgate LJ describes this as simply the development “consuming its own smoke.”

240. But as the name and definition of BNG indicates, this refers to an overall statutory improvement in biodiversity. That goes beyond offsetting the adverse impacts of a scheme. It increases biodiversity in order to help redress a significant national decline which is not caused by the proposed development that is under consideration. On any view, that would be a clear benefit of the proposed scheme. However, the scale of that benefit and the weight to be attached to it are separate considerations.

241. Whether a measure should be treated as a benefit, depends upon its nature and purpose, including whether it would help to meet a need which is, or is not, related to the development proposed. For example, we often place substantial weight on the provision of market housing where there has been a shortfall in the delivery of

[^121]: Vistry Homes & Fairfax Acquisitions v SoS Levelling Up Housing and Communities [2024] EWHC 2088 (Admin)

<!-- Part3 p.458 -->

dwellings to meet local policy requirements. Sometimes very substantial weight is given to the benefit of providing affordable housing in a scheme.

242. Logically, it is difficult to see why giving no weight at all to the provision of 10% BNG is justified because it equates to the 10% requirement derived from inserting s90A and schedule 7A into the TCPA. The fact that such a requirement is imposed by legislation is simply a mechanism for ensuring that a wide range of developments contribute to a collective effort to improve biodiversity across the whole of England. It does not alter the nature or purpose of the improvement in biodiversity that is provided, or the underlying justification for the requirement to reverse a national decline in biodiversity over a great many years. It also follows that where a development would provide BNG of 20%, we are not entitled to say that only that part of the BNG which exceeds 10% can qualify as a benefit in deciding whether to grant planning permission.

243. Turning to wider questions of weight. If you were to reduce the weight which would otherwise be given to a 40% provision of affordable housing because this would be no more than required by the development plan, then this would also be objectionable. You should be assessing how the developer’s contribution of affordable housing stands in relation to the justification in the development plan for a certain level of affordable housing required by the policy. Key considerations could include the level and nature of the need for affordable housing in the district and any shortfall in delivery.

244. Similarly, you should consider the nature and purposes of the BNG requirement and of the contribution being made. Whilst is could be argued that this benefit is of a generalised nature, this may not always be the case. The gain may be helping to deliver part of a LNRS or it may be contributing to an important strategic scheme. Such a scheme might also be delivering nutrient mitigation and/or carbon offsetting. As such, it would be analogous to the benefit of providing affordable housing as it relates to the highly specific needs identified by a LPA for its area and ensuring that the release of housing land meets the need for affordable housing as well as general housing. Such considerations may affect the weight to be given to benefits but this is a matter for your judgement, provided it is cogently and robustly reasoned.

245. It is for us to determine how much weight to give to generalised benefits on a case-by-case basis. Little help is to be gained from looking at the decisions of other Inspectors as there is usually insufficient information to help determine true comparability. Weights are also not numerical, we use variable terms and a particular weighting may not be fully explained. It can be particularly meaningless to simply compare percentage increases in particular units without also knowing the absolute size of the increase. Large percentage increases can simply result from on-site gains if there is a very low baseline.

246. It is absolutely critical that where the application for permission was made before 14 February or 2 April 2024 that the statutory 10% requirement should not be treated as having been applicable, nor should that be the effect of our reasoning given the NRS Sandon and Weston judgements that have already been highlighted.
