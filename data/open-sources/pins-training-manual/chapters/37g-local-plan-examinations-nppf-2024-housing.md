## Introduction

1. It is the Government’s policy to boost the supply of homes significantly by ensuring that a sufficient amount and variety of land can come forward where it is needed, with the overall aim of meeting an area’s identified housing need. (NPPF 61).

2. Housing is likely to form a large component of most local plan examinations. Local plans, and local plan examinations, will need to consider a wide range of housing topics.

3. This chapter addresses the main issues that arise in the examination of the housing aspect of local plans. It provides references to a range of different policies and considers issues that commonly arise in the examination.

4. It may be regarded as a road map which directs the reader to the relevant policies and procedures and provides information about their practical implications for the local plan examination.

5. It is not a policy document. Users of this document must refer directly to the NPPF and PPG for full details of policies and procedures.

6. The structure of the chapter follows this sequence:

- housing need

- the plan’s housing requirement

- housing land availability

- selecting housing sites

- overall housing land supply

- the 5 year housing land supply

- affordable housing

- plan policies, including site allocation policies, specialist needs and optional technical standards

<!-- Part3 p.19 -->

7. This chapter only addresses plans to which the policies of the December 2024 NPPF apply. For advice on examining plans submitted before 25 January 2019, please refer to Local Plan Examinations: NPPF 2012. For advice on plans examined under NPPF 2021, please refer to Local Plan Examinations: NPPF 2021

8. This chapter takes into account the December 2024 National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF or Framework) and the latest versions of Planning Practice Guidance (PPG), including the transitional arrangements set out in the Framework.

9. The Schedule to the Town and Country Planning (Local Planning) (England) Regulations 2026 sets out transitional provisions for local plans prepared under previous regulations that are currently in examination and paragraphs 234 to 241 and Annex 1 of the December 2024 NPPF set out national policy on the subject. For the purposes of preparing local plans, the policies of the December 2024 NPPF apply from 12 March 2025 unless one or more of the 5 exceptions set out in NPPF 234a to 234e apply, in which case the plan will be examined under the relevant previous version of the Framework. There is a cut-off date of 31 December 2026 for the submission of local plans for examination under the existing system. An issue of interpretation has arisen regarding NPPF 234a in respect of the relationship between housing requirement and local housing need, that may have a bearing on which version of the NPPF should be used at examination, and this is addressed in paragraphs 93-97 below.

10. There are policy references at the end of many of the paragraphs. These allow the reader to look up the subject in more detail. They do not denote quotations from the policy.

11. “Authority” has been used throughout to mean “plan-making authority”. “Local plan” means a full local plan containing strategic policies unless otherwise indicated.

## Some definitions

12. It is very important to use the right terminology when dealing with housing in development plan examinations. The following definitions can all be found in this chapter but are brought together here for clarity.

<!-- Part3 p.20 -->

13. Local housing need is the number of homes identified as being needed through the application of the standard method set out in national planning practice guidance. It is an unconstrained assessment of the minimum number of homes needed in an area. Once the plan is submitted for examination, the housing need figure calculated through the standard method can be relied on for plan making for 2 years from the date of submission.

14. The housing requirement is the minimum number of homes that a plan seeks to provide from all sources during the plan period. It is usually expressed as an annual figure.

15. The strategic housing land availability assessment (SHLAA) identifies how much land in the plan area is suitable, available and achievable for housing (and economic) development during the plan period.

16. Site allocations or broad areas for growth are sites or broad areas identified for development in the plan. They are usually the key components that enable the plan’s overall housing requirement to be met. Selecting housing sites for inclusion in the plan is a separate exercise from the SHLAA.

17. Housing supply is a future supply of land which is suitable, available and achievable for housing and can potentially be delivered over the plan period from the plan’s site allocations, existing commitments, completions since the start of the plan period, and estimated windfalls.

18. The 5 year housing land supply is the supply of specific deliverable sites for the five years following the adoption of the plan, with an appropriate buffer.

## Local housing need

19. NPPF 11 indicates that plans should promote a sustainable pattern of development with aligned growth and infrastructure. Strategic policies should as a minimum provide for objectively assessed needs for housing and other uses, as well as any needs that cannot be met within neighbouring areas (as established through statements of common ground), unless the exceptions listed in NPPF 11b apply.

### Calculating local housing need

<!-- Part3 p.21 -->

20. Local housing need is defined in the NPPF Glossary as the number of homes identified as being needed through the application of the standard method set out in national planning practice guidance. It is an unconstrained assessment of the minimum number of homes needed in an area. It is assessed separately from land availability, the housing requirement and the housing needs of different groups of people. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment”, Paragraph 001).

21. Local housing need should be calculated by means of a local housing need assessment using the standard method in PPG. This should be carried out by strategic plan-making authorities at the start of the plan-making process. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 008).

22. The standard method uses as a baseline 0.8% of the authority’s housing stock, taken from the most recent published data, and applies an affordability adjustment factor based on median workplace-based affordability ratios. (NPPF 61, PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 004 et seq).

23. The method identifies a minimum annual average housing need figure which can be applied to the whole plan period. It does not itself produce a housing requirement figure. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraphs 2 and 12).

24. Previous NPPF content which referred to the standard method as an advisory starting point has been deleted from the December 2024 NPPF.

### The affordability adjustment, and past under and over delivery prior to the plan period

25. The affordability adjustment embedded within the calculation directs more homes to where they are most needed (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 6) and therefore automatically compensates for under-delivery against previous housing requirements prior to the date to which the affordability ratio relates.

26. It follows that there is no requirement to address under-delivery prior to the date to which the affordability ratio relates separately in the calculations of housing need or the housing requirement. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 11). For the same reason it should not be necessary to take account of over-delivery against previous housing requirements prior to that date. The affordability adjustment should account for both under delivery and over delivery.

<!-- Part3 p.22 -->

27. The under-delivery or over-delivery of homes after the date to which the affordability ratio relates is a different matter and is addressed later: see paragraphs 86 to 92.

### Changes to, and the duration of, the housing need figure

28. New estimates of housing stock and updated affordability ratios are published annually, so the housing need figure calculated by the standard method may change between the beginning of the plan-making process and the submission of the plan for examination. The examination should normally be based on the most recent pre-examination standard-method LHN figure, but there may be exceptions: see below.

29. Once the plan is submitted for examination, the housing need figure calculated through the standard method can be relied on for plan making for 2 years from the date of submission. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 008). This provides a stable 2 year basis for the examination of strategic-level local plans and for the production of any subsequent non-strategic plans, such as Part 2 site allocation plans. However, sometimes in strategic-level local plan examinations there might be justification for using a more recent post-submission figure, for example where there have been significant delays in the process combined with changes in the housing need figure since submission; this will depend on individual circumstances. Thus the figure may need to be re-based after the 2 year period, but the new figure should not be retrospectively applied to the plan period before the re-basing date.

30. It should be emphasised that it is not legally within the remit of Part 2 site allocation plans to set or revise the housing requirement, even if there have been substantial changes in housing need from the figures on which the Part 1 Plan is based – unless the plan is specifically charged to do so by an adopted strategic-level local plan. (See also paragraphs 64 to 74).

### Local need assessments covering more than one area

31. Where local needs assessments cover more than one area, such as where strategic policies are being prepared jointly, or where spatial development strategies are being prepared by elected mayors of combined authorities with strategic policy making powers, the housing need figure that is established for the defined area should be at least the sum of the local housing needs for each planning authority within the area. It is up to the strategic planning authority to distribute the total housing requirement. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 13).

### Alternatives to the standard method

<!-- Part3 p.23 -->

32. The NPPF no longer refers to the use of alternative approaches to the standard method, but the PPG continues to recognise the possibility of locally determined methods for identifying housing need figures. There are certain circumstances where an alternative approach may have to be used, such as where strategic policy-making authorities do not align with local authority boundaries. Examples might be the National Park Authorities and the Broads Authority, and local authorities whose boundaries have changed in the last five years. Authorities proposing to use locally determined methods should provide evidence that their approach is consistent with other authorities in similar situations.

33. If an alternative approach is used, it will be tested at examination, giving consideration to whether the assessment provides the basis for a plan that is positively prepared, taking into account available information on existing levels of housing stock and housing affordability. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraphs 14 and 15)

34. It is important to appreciate that the local housing need figure stands separately from other factors. Please note the definitions in paragraphs 12 to

18. It is not the same as the housing requirement, which is dealt with below.

## The plan’s housing requirement

### The definition and purpose of the housing requirement

35. The housing requirement is the minimum number of homes that a plan seeks to provide from all sources during the plan period. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 002). The requirement should apply from the same date as that which the local housing need is based on (under the standard method, the date to which the affordability ratio relates).

36. The overall aim of the housing requirement should be to meet an area’s identified housing need, including an appropriate mix of housing types for the local community and taking into account where appropriate unmet need from nearby authority areas.

### The application of the housing requirement

37. The housing requirement is normally expressed in the plan as an annual average figure, as in X dpa (dwellings per annum). It is used to inform:

- the 5 year housing land supply calculation.

<!-- Part3 p.24 -->

- the number of new homes that the plan should make provision for through new site allocations, and when they are expected to come forward.

- the housing requirement for designated neighbourhood areas.

- the monitoring of delivery.

38. The housing requirement, which is the actual requirement of the plan, should not be confused with the delivery trajectory, which concerns the anticipated supply of new housing over the plan period. To provide clarity on all these matters, it is critical that the housing requirement is set out unambiguously in the plan’s policies.

### Establishing the plan’s housing requirement

39. The starting point for establishing the housing requirement will normally be the local housing need figure derived from the most recent standard method calculation before the submission of the plan for examination. See paragraphs 20 to 24 and paragraphs 28 to 30.

40. The housing requirement may be higher than the local housing need figure (NPPF 69) in order, for example, to:

- accommodate the unmet needs of neighbouring areas;

- meet growth ambitions linked to economic development or infrastructure investment;

- meet all of, or a higher proportion of, affordable housing need, or help towards meeting the needs of older people or other particular groups.

41. The housing requirement may be lower than the local housing need figure in some plan areas where it is not possible to meet local need owing to constraints (NPPF 11b), giving rise to unmet housing need which must be addressed by the authority.

42. These issues are discussed below.

### Raising the housing requirement to accommodate the unmet housing need of neighbouring areas

<!-- Part3 p.25 -->

43. Plans look at least 15 years ahead following adoption, so if evidence demonstrates that one authority will be unable to meet its local housing need within the life of the plan (for example in a city whose boundaries are tightly drawn around the built up area, where a recent local plan has not been able to identify enough sites), the unmet housing need should be explicitly quantified, including the different components of that need, such as affordable housing. This unmet need should be addressed in the local plans of neighbouring or nearby authorities (usually those within the same housing market area) through statements of common ground and/or a memorandum of understanding. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 025) (See also paragraph 62.)

44. Where an authority agrees to make explicit additional housing provision within its plan to accommodate another authority’s unmet need, it should normally be integrated into the plan’s housing requirement either for the whole plan period, or part of the plan period, depending on when the need arises. For example, the exporting authority may have sufficient land for the first few years of the plan but is predicted to be in deficit from a specific year, from which point the receiving authority’s annual housing requirement will increase to take it into account. In such cases a stepped requirement may result (see paragraphs 78 to 85). Thus where an authority is taking a proportion of another’s fully quantified and evidenced unmet need, it should be included in the housing requirement as an average annual figure. This provides the assurance that the unmet need will be met in the recipient authority.

45. Occasionally, if one authority has an oversupply of sites and there is evidence that an adjacent authority has unmet need, a pragmatic approach might be to regard the supply headroom as a potential contribution towards the neighbour’s unmet need. This approach may be helpful where there is plenty of supply headroom in the local plan area and strong evidence of unmet need in a nearby authority which has not been precisely quantified (perhaps because its plan is at an earlier stage in the local plan process or because its unmet need has not been apportioned between different authorities). Care is needed with this however, because if the recipient authority’s site allocations suffer delays and under-delivery, its supply headroom will be eroded and the authority will not meet its neighbour’s unmet need after all, Thus generally it is preferable to integrate the allowance for unmet need into the receiving authority’s housing requirement, which creates an important incentive to ensure that it is met. (See paragraphs 206 to 208).

<!-- Part3 p.26 -->

46. It might be argued that, since unmet need in one authority will exert pressure on its neighbours’ housing markets, the affordability element of the standard method calculation will automatically raise the LHN of the neighbouring authorities such that it should not be necessary for those authorities to make an upward adjustment to their LHN to cater for unmet need. The difficulty with this argument is that LHN only takes into account existing affordability at the time of calculation, whereas local plans look forward over a period of 15 to 20 years and should plan positively to meet identified need over that period. For this reason significant amounts of unmet housing need that have been identified by one authority through its local plan process should be explicitly addressed by neighbouring authorities through their own local plans.

### Raising the housing requirement to meet growth ambitions linked to economic growth or infrastructure investment

47. The government supports authorities who wish to plan for growth. In such cases the housing requirement may be set higher than the identified housing need. Additional housing may be planned for to support growth objectives, encourage innovation and improved productivity, make the best use of existing or new infrastructure or address potential barriers to economic investment, such as inadequate housing to support growth in terms of numbers or mix. (NPPF 8, 69. 86).

48. This approach may have a basis in a national or strategic growth strategy, a more local growth strategy, or in the plan’s specific economic objectives. But it should not normally have to be thoroughly justified at examination. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 2a).

### Raising the housing requirement to meet affordable housing need or the needs of older people or specific groups

<!-- Part3 p.27 -->

49. It is common for the calculation of affordable housing need to produce a figure which exceeds or is proportionally high compared with the housing need figure derived from the standard method. In these situations, the amount of affordable housing delivered through the plan’s policies by applying the affordable housing percentage to major housing developments would fall short of meeting the identified affordable housing need. (See paragraphs 248 to 249).

50. In such circumstances, authorities should ensure that they are seeking the maximum viable percentage of affordable housing. An increase in the plan’s overall housing requirement could be considered where it could help towards delivering the required number of affordable homes. The same effect might be achieved through a housing supply in excess of the housing requirement. The additional housing would be subject to the policy requirement that a certain percentage should be affordable. In this way, more affordable homes would be delivered. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 024).

51. Where an increase in the housing requirement is being proposed for this purpose, there needs to be adequate evidence as to whether, and to what extent, there is demand for the additional market housing in the area, whether the market can deliver the additional number of homes, and whether the additional housing can be brought forward viably. (PPG “Viability”).

52. If all options have been considered, and the plan seeks the maximum amount of affordable housing subject to viability and deliverability (including meeting other important plan objectives), it may be concluded that the objective of meeting affordable housing need through the plan’s policies has been met as far as it is possible to do so.

53. Normally the housing needs of older people and specific groups such as students are met through appropriate policies, but in certain (relatively unusual) instances it might be necessary to raise the housing requirement to rectify the potential under-delivery of specific housing types or to create a different balance of house types to meet plan objectives. (See paragraphs 263 to 264).

54. In the case of students, the standard method figure for local housing need may to a degree reflect current supply pressures, but predicted future changes in student numbers during the life of the plan may need to be taken into account. Planning authorities will therefore need to plan for student accommodation needs. (See paragraphs 278 to 280).

### Second homes, short lets, holiday lets and vacant homes

<!-- Part3 p.28 -->

55. Neither the NPPF nor the PPG indicates that any adjustment is required to the housing requirement to take account of these factors. With the introduction of the standard method, and particularly from December 2024, the affordability component of the calculation should mean that current supply pressures arising from these factors are reflected in the LHN figure. That said, the matter may still need to be considered in certain areas where the issue is acute or where a substantial change is forecast.

### Where the housing requirement might be set lower than local housing need

56. Plans should meet objectively assessed needs. Local authorities are normally expected to meet all their housing needs within their areas. However, the circumstances described in NPPF 11 b) might arise. Owing to constraints, or where the authority’s boundaries are tightly drawn, it might not be possible to meet all the housing needs within the plan boundaries. In these circumstances, the draft plan might propose setting a lower housing requirement than local housing need.

57. Authorities are expected first and foremost to meet their own needs so the evidence put forward to support setting a lower housing requirement will be tested thoroughly at examination. It will be important for the authority to demonstrate convincingly that the best and most efficient use has been made of the sites identified in the SHLAA.

58. The authority will also need to demonstrate convincingly that every possible source of land has been investigated and that available and developable sites have not been discounted for inadequate reasons. The potential for mitigating identified impacts (for example, environmental, landscape or highway impacts) to enable a site to come forward should have been properly considered. It is common for developers and landowners to present arguments that “omission sites” have been inappropriately discounted and are available to meet some or all of the housing need. Omission sites do not form part of the plan and are not subject to examination (see paragraph 180) but where a submitted plan does not meet local housing need yet there is evidence of a number of credible omission sites or SHLAA sites that have not been allocated, this might suggest that further investigation is required into potential opportunities to enable local housing need to be met.

59. In such circumstances the Inspector might ask the local planning authority to identify further housing sites. A pragmatic approach will need to be taken here to avoid delaying the examination process, depending on the extent of the shortfall and the ease with which additional sites can be identified. It is not the role of the Inspector to identify which sites should be selected.

<!-- Part3 p.29 -->

60. Where the plan contains Green Belt, NPPF 146 indicates that the exceptional circumstances necessary to justify the release of land from the Green Belt include instances where an authority cannot meet its identified need for housing through other means. In these circumstances, LPAs should review Green Belt boundaries and propose alterations to meet these needs in full, unless the review provides clear evidence that such alterations would fundamentally undermine the purposes of the remaining Green Belt across the area of the plan as a whole (NPPF 146). Before concluding that exceptional circumstances exist, all other reasonable options for meeting identified need should be examined fully. (NPPF 147) (See also paragraphs 136-148 below).

61. A failure to establish an adequate housing requirement may cause a plan to fail at examination on the grounds that it is unsound. However, if, having thoroughly examined the matters discussed above, it is adequately demonstrated that the plan must contain a lower housing requirement than the figure for local housing need, there will be a level of unmet housing need within the plan area.

62. Authorities which cannot meet all their housing needs should quantify the types of housing for which there is unmet need in their areas and should have a statement of common ground and memorandum of understanding with one or more authorities to give confidence at the time of the examination that it will be accommodated. (See paragraphs 43 to 46). (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 025).

### Where the housing requirement will not normally need to be adjusted

63. The housing requirement will not normally need to be adjusted to address fluctuations in the economy, societal behaviour and the housing market. These are common and generally on a shorter cycle than the life of the plan. Caution needs to be exercised over arguments at examination that circumstances have permanently and substantially changed since the housing need figure was established and the requirement was set. This argument was frequently raised at the time of the COVID pandemic but with little genuine evidence.

<!-- Part3 p.30 -->

### Housing requirement issues in non-strategic plans such as site allocation plans

64. This section applies to non-strategic plans such as Part 2 site allocation plans that have been prepared subsequent to strategic plans to which the policies in the 2024 Framework apply. Non-strategic plans put into effect the policies of an adopted strategic-level local plan by, for example, allocating sites or setting out development management policies.

65. Part 2 site allocation plans should therefore work within the housing requirement set by the Part 1 strategic-level local plan. With the narrow exception described in paragraph 67 below, it is NOT within the remit of non-strategic plans such as Part 2 site allocation plans to set, review or revise the housing requirement, even if there have been substantial changes in housing need from the figures on which the Part 1 Plan is based.

66. That is because regulation 8 of the 2012 Regulations requires that the policies contained in a local plan must be consistent with the adopted development plan, unless they are explicitly intended to supersede policies in the adopted plan. Moreover, site allocations in non-strategic plans that are intended to give effect to the policies in a strategic plan should be consistent with the strategic plan’s housing requirement and spatial strategy. This approach was tested and found sound in Gladman Development Ltd v Wokingham BC [2014] EWHC 2320 (Admin), and Oxted Residential Ltd v Tandridge DC [2016] EWCA Civ 414.

67. The only circumstance under which a Part 2 plan can reconsider the housing requirement is if the Part 1 strategic-level local plan contains a policy that specifically gives this task to the non-strategic plan.

68. The housing need figure calculated through the standard method can be relied on for plan making for 2 years from the date of the strategic level plan. (See paragraphs 28-29) Therefore non-strategic / Part 2 local plans that follow closely behind the Part 1 plan can rely on that figure.

69. If there is a longer time lapse, the implication is that the standard method figure for housing need on which the strategic plan is based cannot be relied on for plan-making.

70. A degree of fluctuation in the local housing need figure over time arising from adjustments to the affordability ratio may reasonably be expected. If more than 2 years have passed since the date of the submission of the strategic plan, a view will need to be taken as to whether the changes to the housing need figure are significant enough to call into question the provisions of the non-strategic plan.

<!-- Part3 p.31 -->

71. After the two year period, if there is evidence of a significant change in circumstances, for example as a result of substantially revised affordability or household projections or national policy, the matter should be addressed through a review of the strategic plan, NOT through a non-strategic / Part 2 plan.

72. This is particularly relevant where land in the Green Belt has been proposed for release for housing on the grounds of exceptional circumstances and the non-strategic plan seeks to allocate such sites. A significant drop in housing need may call into question whether exceptional circumstances still exist to justify the allocation of those sites. A significant rise in housing need may call into question whether there are exceptional circumstances that justify additional sites to be released from the Green Belt.

73. In such instances the strategic plan will need to be reviewed to establish a new housing requirement figure and consider the implications for the allocation of land, including the spatial strategy and the release of Green Belt. It is not appropriate to try to do this through the non-strategic plan. Issues of this sort were considered in Aireborough Neighbourhood Development Forum v Leeds City Council & Others [2020] EWHC 1461 (Admin).

74. Non-strategic plans should be very clear as to their purpose and their relationship with any adopted strategic plan. The purposes of the non-strategic plan should be set out in the text of the plan itself and should also be reflected in the local development scheme (LDS). If the plan and the LDS are unclear on this matter, this will be raised early in the examination. If necessary the appropriate explanation can be inserted through a main modification.

### Expressing the housing requirement

75. The plan’s strategic policies should set out both the overall housing requirement for the plan period as a whole, and the requirements that apply in each year of the plan period. For example:

“15,000 dwellings are required to be provided between 1 April 2025 and 31 March 2040. The annual average requirement is for 1,000 dwellings in each year of that period.”

76. In this example, the total requirement for the plan period is 15,000 dwellings, the plan period is 15 years, and the annual average requirement remains the same over the plan period, at 1,000 dpa (15,000 divided by 15). This is not an annual target of any sort, it is an annual average.

77. The usual expectation is that the housing requirement is set as an annual average figure to be met in each year of the plan period.

<!-- Part3 p.32 -->

### Stepped housing requirements

78. In certain circumstances, the plan’s housing requirement might be stepped. A stepped requirement means that the yearly requirement varies during the plan period.

79. Local plans may set stepped requirements where there is to be a significant change in the level of housing requirement between emerging and previous policies, and/or where strategic sites will have a phased delivery or are likely to be delivered later in the plan period. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” 68-020-20190722).

80. The London Plan provides an example. The London Plan at paragraph 4.1.10 recognises that the increase in housing delivery required by its targets may be achieved gradually and London boroughs are encouraged to set out a realistic and, where appropriate, stepped housing delivery target over a ten-year period.

81. A stepped requirement may be needed to address the situation where housing delivery between the plan’s start date and its adoption has been substantially higher or lower than the annualised housing requirement figure (see above).

82. A stepped requirement might also be used where a plan is accommodating unmet need from another authority and that unmet need is expected to arise at a certain point in the plan period, for example later in the plan period. (See paragraph 44).

83. A stepped requirement might be expressed as follows:

“15,000 dwellings are required to be provided between 1 April 2025 and 31 March 2040. The annual average requirement is for 800 dwellings in each of the five years from 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2030, and for 1,100 dwellings in each of the ten years from 1 April 2030 to 31 March 2040.”

84. In this example the plan’s annual average housing requirement is lower over the first 5 years of the plan period before stepping up for the latter 10 years.

85. Stepped requirements should be justified by evidence and should not be used to unnecessarily delay meeting identified development needs. They should also ensure that the planned housing requirements are met fully within the plan period.

<!-- Part3 p.33 -->

### Addressing lower housing delivery rates (“under-delivery”) between the plan’s start date and its adoption

86. Between the start date of the housing requirement and the plan’s adoption, housing delivery in the plan area (as identified by annual monitoring) may have been lower than the average annual figure required to meet the overall housing requirement.

87. Under-delivery in this period is compensated for by re-setting the average annual requirement, usually for part of the plan period, so that the overall housing requirement is still met. It does not involve any change in the plan’s overall requirement.

88. The PPG expects any shortfall from the early years to be made up within the first five years after adoption unless a longer period can be justified as part of the plan-making and examination process. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 022).

89. This approach will probably result in a stepped housing requirement – see above. It may involve setting two steps, a step up to remedy the shortfall in the first 5 years after adoption, followed by a downward step.

90. A longer period for making up the shortfall is sometimes argued for by the authority on the basis that the plan’s delivery cannot step quickly enough. This will be tested at examination, having regard among other things to past delivery rates, the capacity of the market and the anticipated start dates and delivery rates of pipeline and allocated sites.

91. This requires a realistic approach as to what is achievable. However, if there is a reliance on large sites with long lead in times which makes it difficult to remedy the shortfall over the first 5 years, the potential for releasing smaller sites with shorter lead-in times should be considered.

92. Note that any shortfall should only be counted from the start of the housing requirement and should be calculated using the plan’s housing requirement for the years in question. Any shortfall that occurred before the start of the housing requirement should not be included as it will automatically be accounted for through the standard method.

### Transitional arrangements referring to the housing requirement and LHN

93. NPPF 234 sets out the circumstances under which the policies in the 2024 NPPF should be applied in local plan examinations, and the circumstances under which the plan should instead be examined under the relevant previous version of the NPPF.

<!-- Part3 p.34 -->

94. One of the criteria for assessing whether a previous version of the NPPF applies is whether the plan has reached its Regulation 19 stage on or before 12 March 2025, “and its draft housing requirement meets at least 80% of local housing need”. Participants at some examinations have argued that, to calculate this percentage, any component of a plan’s housing requirement which is designed to meet the unmet needs of a neighbouring authority should first be deducted from the housing requirement. Taking this approach might reduce the relevant figure to less than 80% of local housing need, which would mean that the plan would be examined under the 2024 NPPF rather than any previous version.

95. However, NPPF paragraph 234a does not say explicitly that unmet housing need should first be deducted from the housing requirement figure. Policies should be read on their face and interpretation is a matter for the courts. There is no case law relating directly to NPPF 234a but there have been several judgments which are relevant. Perhaps the closest is that of Tesco Stores Ltd v Stockport MBC [2025] EWCA Civ 610 in which Sir Keith Lindblom SPT stated “As a general rule, the temptation to infer terms the policy-maker has not actually used should be resisted”.

96. Therefore, a straightforward reading of paragraph 234a is that the draft housing requirement, not some subset of it, should be 80% or more of the LHN figure. Interpreting policy to include this additional calculation when it is not specifically stated in the policy invites a risk of challenge. In the absence of an explicit statement to the contrary to the effect, the straightforward reading of NPPF 234a should be followed.

97. Further information on this issue will be set out in a forthcoming PINS Note.

### Transitional arrangements and early review policies

98. It has been common practice in local plan examinations for Inspectors to require ‘early review’ policies to be inserted into the plan through a main metafiction as a pragmatic way of overcoming a soundness problem.

99. Inspectors are able to recommend main modifications introducing policies that commit the local authority to commencing production of a new-style local plan before required to do so by the Local Plan Regulations 2026. But the need for and effectiveness of such policies should be considered in the light of the dates set by the Regulations. Clearly, the sooner the requirement to commence plan preparation under the new system, the less likely a main modification of this sort will be required.

<!-- Part3 p.35 -->

100. As regards partial reviews, the legislative framework for the new system only allows for one local plan to have effect for each local authority area at any one time. There is no clear route for local planning authorities to submit an early partial update policy, and it follows that a policy requiring an early partial review would be ineffective. Early partial review policies and early partial update policies should not be included in main modifications.

101. It is also unlikely, because of the legislative and draft policy framework, that supplementary plans will provide a vehicle for overcoming soundness issues.

102. A fuller explanation of these points is set out in PINS Note 02/2026.

## Assessing housing land availability

### The strategic housing land availability assessment (SHLAA)

103. Strategic plan-making authorities should make an assessment of the number of new homes that can be provided in their area, justified by evidence on land availability, development constraints and other relevant matters They should have a clear understanding of available housing land through the preparation of a strategic housing land availability assessment. This is commonly referred to as a SHLAA. (NPPF 72; PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 040; PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment”).

104. The SHLAA identifies how much land in the plan area is suitable, available and achievable for housing (and usually economic) development during the plan period. In doing so it should recognise constraints, including areas where the Framework would provide strong reasons for restricting the scale, type or distribution of development.

105. The SHLAA only provides information on the range of sites available; it is for the plan itself to decide which of those sites are the most suitable. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraphs 001 and 002).

### Stages in site assessment

106. The PPG sets out 5 stages for the identification of sites and broad locations and it contains a link to a methodology flowchart. Plan-making authorities are expected to have regard to this guidance and any reasons for departing from it should be explained in the plan’s evidence base. Neighbourhood planning bodies may also use it in a proportionate manner, or they may use existing assessments prepared by the local planning authority. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraphs 003 to 005).

<!-- Part3 p.36 -->

### Identification of sites

107. Stage 1 is the identification of all sites and broad locations, to provide a complete audit of available land regardless of the amount of development needed. The area covered by the SHLAA should be the plan-making area, which could be the planning authority area, two or more areas, or areas covered by a spatial development strategy. This stage includes a desktop review, a call for sites and broad locations, and a survey of sites and broad locations. The PPG contains advice on what these elements should include. It also contains a link to a table setting out possible data sources for different types of sites.

108. Policies, constraints and an initial assessment of suitability can be recorded by the survey – but that does not mean that sites should be ruled out at this stage. The NPPF envisages that plan preparation or review might involve the provision of housing on land released from the Green Belt under exceptional circumstances, for example, where an authority cannot meet its identified need for homes. (NPPF 145 and 146).

109. The assessment needs to be undertaken working with other local planning authorities in the relevant housing market area as well as with landowners and promoters, developers, local communities, parish councils and others. A range of site sizes capable of delivering 5 or more dwellings should be considered. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraphs 6 to 15).

### Assessment of sites

110. Stage 2 is a site and broad location assessment. This involves the estimation of the development potential of each site or broad location identified in Stage

1. It can take into account constraints: for example, it should reflect the policies for the topics in Footnote 7 of the Framework. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 002).

111. It can be guided by policy, including local density policies, but should seek to make the most efficient use of land in accordance with policies in the Framework.

<!-- Part3 p.37 -->

112. In addition to assessing development potential, an evaluation should be made of the site’s suitability for housing, its availability, and its achievability, including whether the site is economically viable. National policy, market attractiveness, contribution to regeneration priority areas, potential impacts on landscape, nature and heritage and other factors, policy changes and other emerging sites can all be considered, as can land ownership, the existence of planning permission, lead-in times and the delivery track record of developers or landowners. This exercise will help to establish whether a site can be considered deliverable within the next 5 years or over a longer period. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraphs 016 to 022)

### Windfalls

113. Stage 3 is a windfall assessment where this is justified. Windfall sites are sites that are not specifically identified in the development plan. A windfall allowance can be justified in the anticipated supply if there is sound evidence that it will provide a reliable source of housing land supply. (See paragraphs 158, 159, 202, 232 and 233.) (NPPF 75; PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 22).

### Assessment review

114. Stage 4 is an assessment review which considers the development potential of all the sites to produce an indicative delivery trajectory. This sets out how much housing is likely to come forward from the sites at different points in the future – in other words, within years 1-5 following adoption, years 6-10 and years 11 and beyond, based on the evidence gathered in stages 1 to 3. The trajectory should also be subject to an overall risk assessment as to whether sites will come forward as anticipated. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 024).

115. The presumption is that local authorities should meet all their housing needs. If the assessment indicates that there are insufficient sites or broad locations to meet need, the assessment will need to be revisited; a further call for sites may be carried out and the development potential of particular sites may be re-evaluated to ensure that they make the most efficient use of land. Green Belt boundaries should be reviewed and alterations may be proposed. (See paragraphs 117 to 124 and 136 to 146). (NPPF 146).

116. However, if insufficient land remains, and there is clear evidence that strategic policies cannot meet all the needs, it will be important to establish how needs might be met in adjoining areas through statements of common ground. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 025).

<!-- Part3 p.38 -->

### Green Belt review

117. Green Belt boundaries should only be altered where exceptional circumstances are fully evidenced and justified through the preparation or updating of plans. Exceptional circumstances include instances where an authority cannot meet its identified need for homes.

118. If that is the case, authorities should review Green Belt boundaries and propose alterations to meet those needs in full, unless the review provides clear evidence that doing so would fundamentally undermine the purposes (taken together) of the remaining Green Belt, when considered across the area of the plan. Grey belt land should be identified to inform the review. (NPPF 145 and 146 and PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 001).

119. Local authorities should produce a Green Belt assessment as part of the review of Green Belt boundaries. The review will inform local plan preparation and the preparation of spatial development strategies. (PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 002).

120. PPG “Green Belt” sets out the steps for the Green Belt assessment. The assessment should be based on sufficiently granular areas to enable an assessment to be made of their variable contribution to Green Belt purposes. A small number of large assessment areas will generally not be appropriate. Assessment areas can vary in size depending on circumstances. (PPG “Green Belt” Paragraphs 003 and 004).

121. The PPG also provides a list of considerations to be taken into account in assessing the contribution that assessment areas make to Green Belt purposes. (PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 005).

122. Any assessment area that is not judged to contribute strongly to any one of the purposes a. b. or d. of the Green Belt (to check the unrestricted sprawl of large built up areas, to prevent neighbouring towns merging into one another, and to preserve the setting and special character of historic towns) can be identified as grey belt land – except where the application of policies relating to the areas or assets in footnote 7 to the NPPF (other than Green Belt) would provide a strong reason for refusing or restricting development.(PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 007).

123. Authorities should also consider whether, or the extent to which, the release of Green Belt land (not just grey belt land) would affect the ability of all the remaining Green Belt across the area of the plan from serving all five of the Green Belt purposes in a meaningful way. (PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 008).

<!-- Part3 p.39 -->

124. Land released from the Green Belt for the purposes of meeting housing need will not necessarily be confined to grey belt land. The identification of grey belt will inform the assessment, but there are other important considerations, notably the objectives of the spatial strategy and the need to promote sustainable patterns of development, that will affect the choice of land for Green Belt land release should exceptional circumstances make this necessary. This is discussed further in the next section which deals with the selection of housing sites. (See paragraphs 136 to 146).

### Final evidence base

125. Stage 5 is the compilation of the final evidence base for the SHLAA. The standard outputs are a list of all the sites and broad locations considered, cross referenced to their locations on maps; an assessment of each site or broad location; instances where these have been discounted, including reasons; the potential type and quantity of development, estimated build-out rates and delivery barriers on suitable and available sites; and an indicative development trajectory. It should be reiterated here that it is for the plan to decide which sites are the most suitable to take forward. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 026).

### Steps to be taken if the assessment is found inadequate at examination

126. If the assessment of housing land availability, including any Green Belt review, is found inadequate at examination, the LPA will be asked to try to rectify the deficiencies. This may result in more available land being identified, enabling a higher housing requirement figure to be set. However, the approach must be pragmatic and have regard to what can be achieved in the examination without undue delay.

## Selecting housing sites

### Site selection and allocation

127. Site allocations or broad areas for growth are the key components that enable the plan’s overall housing requirement to be met, when added to existing commitments, windfalls and completions since the start of the plan period.

<!-- Part3 p.40 -->

128. Selecting housing sites for inclusion in the plan is a separate exercise from the SHLAA although it may involve an iterative process. It should be an explicit, clear and logical piece of evidence. As with the SHLAA exercise, it should recognise the potential for realistic mitigation measures to enable good sites to come forward rather than discounting them at the first iteration.

### The relationship to the spatial strategy

129. The site selection process is integral to the achievement of the spatial strategy. The spatial strategy should be coherent, robust and based on positive planning. It should embody all three of the objectives of sustainable development in NPPF 8, economic, social and environmental. It may have growth objectives, support particular economic sectors or be related to infrastructure provision or sustainable movement patterns. It follows that housing site selection, including sites released from the Green Belt (see paragraphs 136 to 146) should not simply be defined by choosing those sites that have the fewest constraints (“sieve mapping”).

130. The authority should have a clear explanatory narrative (not just a tabular sustainability appraisal) about how the selection of new housing sites contributes to the delivery of the plan’s spatial strategy and its objectives. Where large scale strategic housing-led growth is proposed there needs to be clear and logical evidence to support the strategic site selection process. This may usefully be set out for the examination in a site selection topic paper.

### The identification of sites to come forward at different plan stages

131. Plans should identify a supply of specific, deliverable sites for five years following the intended date of the plan’s adoption. Deliverable means the site is available now, offers a suitable location for development now, and is achievable with a realistic prospect that housing will be delivered on the site within 5 years. (NPPF 72 and NPPF Glossary).

132. Plans should also identify a supply of specific, developable sites or broad locations for growth for years 6 - 10 and, where possible, for years 11 - 15. Developable means the site is in a suitable location for housing development with a reasonable prospect that it will be available and could be viably developed at the point envisaged. (NPPF 72 and NPPF Glossary).

133. The combination of sites selected for housing development should allow for appropriate amounts of development to come forward at different stages of the plan to enable a rolling 5 year supply of land to be maintained. The plan should identify a mix of sites, taking into account their availability, suitability and likely economic viability. (NPPF 70).

<!-- Part3 p.41 -->

134. Where, under the NPPF’s Housing Delivery Test, housing delivery has fallen below the threshold of 95% of the housing requirement, the local planning authority will have been required under the NPPF to produce a housing delivery action plan. When producing the local plan, planning authorities should consider the recommendations from the action plan. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 022) These recommendations might, for example, include unblocking stalled sites, increasing engagement with developers and considering additional housing allocations.

### The identification of priority sites for early delivery

135. Plan-making authorities may wish to identify priority sites which can be delivered earlier in the plan period, such as those on brownfield land and where there is supporting infrastructure in place such as transport hubs. These sites will provide additional flexibility and more certainty that authorities will be able to demonstrate a sufficient supply of deliverable sites against the housing requirement. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 012).

### Sites released from the Green Belt

136. Strategic plans should establish the need for any changes to Green Belt boundaries following the review of Green Belt. (See paragraphs 117 to 124).

137. The new Green Belt boundary is usually shown on the policies map in the strategic plan, but sometimes it is shown diagrammatically, with the detail to be resolved through a neighbourhood plan.

138. If there are exceptional circumstances that are considered to justify the release of land from the Green Belt, they must be fully justified by the authority and by the Inspector.

139. Before concluding that exceptional circumstances exist to justify changes to Green Belt boundaries, the strategic policy-making authority should be able to demonstrate that it has examined fully all other reasonable options for meeting its identified need for development. This will be assessed through the examination of its strategic policies, and will consider whether the strategy makes as much use as possible of suitable brownfield sites and underutilised land; optimises the density of development and has been informed by discussions with neighbouring authorities about whether they could accommodate some of the identified need for development. [NPPF 147]

140. In local plans which contain site allocations on land released from the Green Belt, exceptional circumstances need to be justified at two levels:

<!-- Part3 p.42 -->

- the strategic level – meeting housing need that cannot be met on appropriate alternative sites outside the Green Belt, the objectives of the spatial strategy, economic needs and growth considerations, sustainability considerations, the effect on the Green Belt purposes as a whole.

- the local level – the suitability of each selected site in terms of location, sustainability, the relation to the spatial strategy and the effect of the specific site on the purposes of the Green Belt.

141. In the “Calverton case” (Calverton Parish Council v Nottingham City Council & Ors [2015] EWHC 1078 (Admin) (21 April 2015)) Mr Justice Jay set out the steps that should ideally be taken in this process. After establishing the objectively assessed housing need in the area covered by a development plan document, an inspector should ideally consider the acuteness/intensity of the need, the constraints on the supply/availability of land suitable for development, the difficulties in achieving sustainability without impinging on the Green Belt, the nature and extent of the harm to this Green Belt, and how far the impacts on Green Belt purposes could be reduced.

142. When reviewing Green Belt boundaries, the need to promote sustainable patterns of development is relevant. Opportunities should be taken to maximise sustainable transport solutions. (PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 010, NPPF 110 and 115).

143. The site selection exercise needs to take account of the bigger picture: the plan’s objectives, its spatial strategy and its sustainability objectives. Identifying grey belt is part of the process, but the choice of sites to be released from the Green Belt may not be confined to grey belt.

144. For example, it may be necessary to release land from the Green Belt to meet the housing needs of towns and cities, or support new areas of growth, in sustainable locations close to where that need arises. The most appropriate land to achieve that might not be grey belt.

145. If there are potential non-Green Belt sites, but they are demonstrably inferior in terms of sustainability and would fail to support national objectives or the strategic objectives of the plan, such as those described above, there may be exceptional circumstances for releasing more suitable land in the Green Belt.

146. As regards the remaining site allocation plans still in the examination system, it should not be necessary to carry out a further strategic level assessment of exceptional circumstances. provided they are demonstrably in accordance with the strategic plan, However, the chosen sites and their impact on the Green Belt will still need to be assessed at the local level.

<!-- Part3 p.43 -->

### Large site allocations

147. The supply of large numbers of new homes can often be best achieved through planning for larger scale development, such as new settlements or significant extensions to existing villages and towns, provided they are well located and designed, and supported by the necessary infrastructure and facilities (including a genuine choice of transport modes). (NPPF 77).

148. Local plans call these larger sites by different names. For example, they may refer to large sites or groups of related sites as strategic allocations, sustainable urban extensions or new settlements, and use thresholds of 500, 1,000, 2,000 or more dwellings to define them. But the plan’s terminology in this instance is of little or no importance to its soundness. There is no legal or policy definition of what constitutes a strategic site or sustainable community.

149. The advantage of large sites is that they may be better able to support new infrastructure and community investment, take opportunities presented by the area’s economic potential, provide employment and, potentially, a greater range of types of home, including affordable homes, than a scatter of smaller sites. They are more likely to give effect to the spatial strategy. (NPPF 77).

150. However, larger sites tend to have long lead-in times which may be affected by different variables. The lead in time may be extended because of the need to discharge policy requirements and conditions. Delivery of larger sites may be dependent on the delivery and timing of any infrastructure by others, land assembly or on critical negotiations with other parties. The number of sales outlets and the ability of the market to absorb the anticipated number of homes may also be relevant to the delivery rate from the site.

151. In addition, the delivery of large scale developments may need to extend beyond an individual plan period, and the associated infrastructure requirements may not be capable of being identified fully at the outset. (NPPF footnote 38).

152. The ability of any proposed large site allocation to contribute towards the plan’s housing requirement, and the timing of that contribution, will need to be tested at the examination, especially if there is any assumption that the site will contribute to part of the 5 year land supply. Similarly, the links with any related infrastructure project will be considered to ensure that there is a reasonable prospect of the project being funded and the site being brought forward.

### Small and medium sites

<!-- Part3 p.44 -->

153. Small and medium sized sites may have fewer constraints to overcome and be more easily developable in the shorter term, helping to maintain the supply of homes earlier in the plan period until larger sites come on stream. They can make an important contribution to meeting the housing requirement of an area, are essential for Small and Medium Enterprise housebuilders to deliver new homes and are often built out relatively quickly. (NPPF 73).

154. Land to accommodate at least 10% of the housing requirement on sites of no larger than one hectare should be identified by local plans and/or brownfield registers, unless there are strong reasons why this cannot be achieved. The Framework specifically requires land identification, which could be through the plan’s site allocations and/or by identifying particular land parcels on the brownfield register and quantifying how much housing could realistically come forward on them. The plan should be accompanied by evidence to demonstrate that at least 10% of the requirement can be provided on such sites. (NPPF 73).

155. However, whilst small sites have an important role to play, an over-emphasis on them can potentially undermine aspects of the spatial strategy. Undue reliance on the wide dispersal of new housing development on small sites may have an undesirable impact on existing transport and community infrastructure, and it may be more difficult to obtain contributions towards transport and community infrastructure projects.

156. The balance of site sizes will be considered at the examination.

157. Policies should also seek to support small sites for community-led development for housing and self-build and custom-build housing.

### Windfall sites

158. Policies should support the development of windfall sites, giving great weight to the benefits of using suitable sites within existing settlements for homes. (NPPF 73) Sites on the Brownfield Land Register might come forward as windfall sites. Windfall allowances are discussed in relation to housing supply in paragraphs 202 and 232 to 233.

159. The plan should therefore contain general policies which take a very positive approach towards housing development within settlements. These will be examined critically to ensure that they are effective. As with site allocations policies (see paragraphs 319 to 327), there is a risk of impairing the effectiveness of the plan by imposing policy requirements that make it difficult to bring forward sites within settlements including windfall sites.

<!-- Part3 p.45 -->

### Settlement boundaries

160. If a plan contains a policy or policies that apply to specific areas, those areas and their boundaries should usually be shown on the policies map. Plans commonly have different policies for development within settlements, outside settlements or in the countryside. Settlement boundaries provide clarity as to where these different policies apply. They help to direct new development as part of the spatial strategy.

161. However, not all plans have settlement boundaries and there is nothing in national policy or guidance that requires them. They may not be effective in loosely-knit settlements where the edges of development are not well defined. Some plans rely instead on criteria-based policies to assess proposals for development around the edge of settlements or (where there is no settlement boundary) contiguous with the existing built up area.

162. Where settlement boundaries have not been included in the plan, the authority will need to demonstrate that the plan’s policies for development in and around settlement are effective and consistent with the plan’s objectives.

163. Some plans, as well as specifically allocating sites, may draw their settlement boundaries to give room between the built up area and the boundary for new windfall development to take place within the boundary.

164. In addition, some plans allow for windfall development, subject to size and location criteria, on unallocated sites beyond the settlement boundary but contiguous with it. (There are separate policies towards rural exception sites and community-led development: see below).

165. These approaches may be useful to give the plan additional flexibility in housing supply. However, they may be explored at examination to understand why land has not been formally allocated and whether the potential impact of development on such land has been appropriately assessed. Moreover, in the case of development on land outside the settlement boundary, the plan will need to be clear about the policies that will be applied to such sites, because countryside policies generally prevail outside settlement boundaries.

166. The settlement boundaries on the policies map should be drawn to take into account recent permissions, sites under construction and completions within the defined settlement. To leave such sites outside the settlement boundary may undermine the plan’s effectiveness by creating confusion over the policies that apply to the land. A consistent approach should be taken to updating the settlement boundaries with recently permitted development.

<!-- Part3 p.46 -->

167. Sometimes the definition of settlement boundaries in certain locations may be delegated to a subsequent site allocation plan or a neighbourhood plan. However, the scope to delegate to a future site allocation plan has now effectively been closed off by the new Regulations. (See paragraph 172).

168. There is no facility for the Inspector’s report to recommend main modifications to the policies map. The line of the settlement boundary on the policies map is generally a matter for the authority and may not need to come up as a substantive issue at the examination. However, it may need to be considered if it appears to give rise to any illogicality, lack of clarity or policy inconsistency. It may also be necessary to discuss and agree an appropriate change to the settlement boundary at examination to give effect to a policy or a main modification.

169. Overall, the authority will need to demonstrate that it has employed a logical approach to the definition (or otherwise) of settlement boundaries.

### Strategic local plans, site allocation plans and area action plans

170. It has been commonplace under the existing system for strategic-level local plans to contain only the spatial strategy and strategic sites or broad locations for development, leaving the allocation of other housing sites to other plans such as “Part 2” site allocation plans or area action plans.

171. The new plan-making system will not contain these “Part 2” plans. However, this ITM chapter is concerned with plans produced under the previous system and subject to the policies in the December 2024 NPPF. NPPF 29 states that non-strategic plans such as site allocation plans and area action plans may allocate sites. This section is therefore relevant to those Part 1 and Part 2 plans that are at examination or are about to be subject to examination.

172. Government guidance (“Rollout of the new local plan-making system”, updated 6 March 2026) indicates that authorities that are preparing a local plan (which includes a Part 2 plan) under the existing system have until 31 December 2026 to submit the plan for examination, other than in certain circumstances. Against this deadline it is unlikely to be sound for a strategic-level local plan currently at examination to place reliance on a subsequent site allocation plan to meet its housing requirement unless there is convincing evidence that the latter is going be submitted before the deadline. Rather, the strategic-level local plan itself will need to provide full confidence that its housing requirement will be met, including appropriate site allocations.

<!-- Part3 p.47 -->

173. Where a site allocation plan is in examination or has been submitted before the deadline, its role should be clear at the outset. This should be set out in the LDS, in the strategic plan and in the site allocation plan itself. The strategic plan should have set out the amount of housing which the site allocation plan or area action plan policies are expected to provide, the period over which it is to be provided, and whether its purpose includes the provision of a 5 year housing land supply. If there is any doubt over these matters, they will be questioned at the examination.

174. The site allocation plan should comply with the housing requirement of the strategic-level local plan including the amount of housing which the site allocation plan or area action plan is expected to provide. It cannot legally re-visit that housing requirement unless the strategic plan and LDS clearly charge it with that responsibility.

175. Any site allocations plans or area action plans still to be examined in the current system should provide enough sites to ensure that the housing requirement and the delivery trajectory in the strategic plan are met, in combination with other known sources of supply. The allocated sites should be distributed within the plan area in accordance with the strategic plan’s spatial strategy.

176. These issues will be critically examined for soundness.

### Neighbourhood plans

177. Some local plans place reliance the anticipated supply of homes from allocations in future neighbourhood plans to make up part of their housing requirement. If this is the case, the local plan should set out a housing requirement figure for each designated neighbourhood planning area. The neighbourhood requirement figures should reflect the plan’s overall strategy for the pattern and scale of development and any relevant allocations. (NPPF 69) The likelihood of achieving the anticipated level of supply from these sources may need to be critically examined.

178. Many plans do not place reliance on future neighbourhood plans to help meet their overall housing requirement, and in these circumstances the setting of housing requirements for neighbourhood planning areas becomes less critical. Any additional homes delivered from neighbourhood plans in these circumstances will simply be added to the supply figure.

<!-- Part3 p.48 -->

179. Where the neighbourhood plan is already made at the time of the examination, any homes anticipated to be delivered during the plan period on sites allocated by the neighbourhood plan are counted into the supply figure.

### Omission housing sites

180. Omission housing sites are those that have not been included in the plan but have the backing of developers or promoters, who may have purchased or taken an option out on the site. The site may have been identified in the SHLAA or the regulation 18 plan or in a call for sites, but have then been omitted from the submission plan.

181. Often the site promoter will have carried out a great deal of preparatory work in order to promote the site through the local plan process, and will sometimes submit a planning application in parallel to the local plan examination. They may have a strong vested interest in promoting the site, and there may equally be strong local feeling about it.

182. Thus there may be direct or indirect attempts to discuss omission sites at the examination and at the hearings. These may come from the site promoters themselves, but they may also come from concerned parish councils or local people, particularly where there is a parallel or forthcoming planning application.

183. The consideration of the relative merits of omission sites within the examination, and the discussion of omission sites in detail at examination hearings, will be firmly resisted by the Inspector.

184. The Inspector is examining the soundness of the plan itself including its site allocations, but is not examining housing sites that have been omitted from it. If the Inspector were to consider the merits of omission sites, it would be necessary to consider the full range of sites, which is not within the Inspector’s remit. It is the Council’s plan, and it is the Council’s task to produce a spatial strategy and carry out the site selection process.

185. That said, in local plans where LHN is not being met and there is evidence of of a number of potential omission sites and/or credible SHLAA sites, the Inspector may require the Council to investigate the potential for further site allocations, provided the plan is not unduly delayed. Failure to meet local need whilst not allocating deliverable sites is likely to be a matter of soundness. (See paragraph 58).

186. Should it be necessary to identify additional housing sites, because the Inspector has directed the Council to increase either the housing requirement or the housing supply, it is the Council's job to identify appropriate additional sites.

## Housing land supply

### General considerations

187. Housing land supply is a future supply of land which is suitable, available and achievable for housing (NPPF 72; PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 1).

188. The plan should provide for enough homes of the right types at the right time to provide confidence that the overall housing requirement will be met, and that a 5 year supply of land will be maintained.

189. Strategic policies should provide a clear strategy for bringing sufficient land forward, at a sufficient rate, to address housing need over the plan period.

190. This should include planning for and allocating sufficient sites to deliver the strategic priorities of the area unless those needs can be met more appropriately through other mechanisms, such as brownfield registers or policies in, for example neighbourhood plans. (NPPF 23) Except for those site allocation plans in examination, the 2026 Regulations now provide no scope for this to be effected through Part 2 site allocation plans.

191. The plan’s overall housing provision, and the anticipated rate of housing delivery, should ensure that there is an adequate ongoing housing land supply.

192. Policies should identify a supply of specific deliverable sites for 5 years following the date of adoption and specific developable sites or broad locations for growth for years 6-10 and where possible years 11 to 15. (NPPF 72) “Deliverable” and “Developable” are defined in the NPPF Glossary. The issue of the 5 year housing land supply is dealt with below.

193. The plan should aim to identify sites or land for years 11 to 15 if possible, partly because of the considerable lead-in time between site allocation and development, and partly because it will help to add detail to the spatial strategy and demonstrate its realism and developability. If the authority believes that it is not possible to do so, this is likely to be considered at the examination. There will need to be confidence that the requirement can be met up to the end of year 10 or later.

194. The housing land supply will be tested at examination for any plan whose purpose includes the provision of a supply of housing land to meet the full housing requirement.

195. Full local plans will usually identify both the housing requirement and a supply of land to meet that requirement in full.

<!-- Part3 p.50 -->

196. Site allocation plans will often identify a supply of land to meet the full housing requirement that has been established in an earlier strategic plan.

197. Spatial Development Strategies (SDS) set the housing requirement for their area, but the supply to meet the requirement will come forward through subsequent local plans. In areas covered by an SDS, housing land supply will therefore be tested when each local plan comes forward.

### Overall housing supply

198. Overall housing supply, expressed as a global figure over the plan period, is the total number of new homes that could theoretically be delivered over the plan period from the plan’s site allocations, existing commitments, completions since the start of the plan period, and estimated windfalls.

199. Overall housing supply is not the same as the housing requirement. Overall housing supply is a potential figure rather than a firm figure because of the inevitable uncertainties in the timing and rate of housing delivery. For practical reasons discussed below, the potential figure is often higher than the housing requirement, to provide headroom to ensure that the housing requirement is met. (See paragraphs 206 to 208).

200. The calculation of overall housing supply is normally explicitly set out in strategic level plans. The plan should identify all the components which make up its housing land supply. Typically, these will include:

- Dwellings completed (“completions”) since the start of the housing requirement (which should coincide with the base date for the housing need assessment)

- Dwellings with planning permission, or with a resolution to grant permission subject to a planning obligation (“commitments”)

- Sites proposed for allocation in the plan (“allocations”)

- Broad locations for growth, where appropriate (and only from year 6 onwards); and

- A windfall allowance, where justified.

201. Care should be taken that there is no overlap or double counting between these sources of supply – for example between the windfall allowance and dwellings with planning permission on unallocated sites.

202. In certain circumstances there may be a case for including an average windfall allowance. (See also paragraphs 232-233). The windfall allowance is usually based on the average yield from such sites over recent years. As well as unallocated sites, it can include new homes arising from the exercise of permitted development rights, for example the conversion of offices to residential.

<!-- Part3 p.51 -->

203. Student housing provision can count towards overall net housing supply based on:

- the amount of accommodation that new student housing releases in the wider housing market (by allowing existing properties to return to general residential use); and / or

- the extent to which it allows general market housing to remain in such use, rather than being converted for use as student accommodation.

204. The calculation is based on the average number of students living in student only accommodation, using the published census data. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” paragraph 025)

205. Specialist housing provided for older people including residential institutions in Use Class C2 also counts towards the figure for overall housing provision, based on the amount of accommodation released into the housing market, again using census data. (See paragraphs 267 to 275) (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 026).

### Headroom

206. If overall housing supply is greater than the housing requirement, this is known as headroom. There is no specific NPPF or PPG requirement for headroom. But the plan is expected to meet the area’s identified needs, so where (as in many plans) there is uncertainty over the delivery, or rate of delivery, of certain sites (for example those affected by infrastructure constraints), headroom may be relevant to the consideration of whether the area’s needs will be met – in other words, it can be relevant to the effectiveness of the plan. Where there are delivery uncertainties, adequate headroom may give confidence that enough housing land will come forward to meet the 5 year supply and the housing requirement for the first 10 years of the plan period. Headroom is sometimes referred to as a “buffer”, but that word should preferably not be used in this context because buffer has a specific meaning in connection with the calculation of the 5 year housing land supply. Nor should it be regarded as “over provision”.

<!-- Part3 p.52 -->

207. Even with the best efforts at establishing a realistic housing supply trajectory, there are many variables that affect the start date and delivery rate of housing sites, particularly larger allocated sites, including market conditions, uncertainties over the timing of critical infrastructure, site preparation, remediation and mitigation and the complexities of obtaining planning permission and discharging reserved matters. If a plan contains enough headroom, this will provide resilience against potential slippage and under-delivery and provide greater confidence that a rolling 5 year housing land supply will be maintained.

208. It may be appropriate for the plan to contain headroom even where Green Belt sites are allocated, provided the exceptional circumstances are fully justified. This was tested in Compton Parish Council & Ors v Guildford Borough Council [2019] EWHC 3242 (Admin).

### The delivery trajectory

209. Strategic policies should include a trajectory illustrating the expected rate of delivery from allocated sites and all other sources over the plan period, and all plans should consider whether it is appropriate to set out the anticipated rate of development for specific sites. (NPPF 78).

210. The delivery trajectory will be in the form of a graph or table setting out how many dwellings in total are expected to be delivered in each year of the plan period. PINS is producing a trajectory template for use at examinations and advisory visits.

211. To substantiate the delivery trajectory, it is helpful for the authority to produce a table or spreadsheet setting out how many dwellings each committed and allocated site is expected to deliver in each year of the plan period, and the anticipated contribution from windfall sites. It should be accompanied by a supporting narrative to justify the information it contains, which may include both generic assumptions and site-specific evidence.

212. This will be a useful piece of evidence for the examination, and it can form a focus for discussion in the hearings. It will help focus discussion on the sites’ availability, suitability and likely economic viability, and on whether the authority’s expectations are reasonable.

<!-- Part3 p.53 -->

213. This evidence should be tested in a proportionate manner. For many of the sites listed, it may be possible to rely on the information and evidence provided by the authority. But where this appears to require further investigation, or where the evidence is challenged, it is likely to be necessary to explore the following key points:1

- Whether the expected date for the first completions on the site is realistic, taking account of any relevant available evidence on constraints, infrastructure requirements, land ownership and assembly, the status of any planning applications, developer intentions and other relevant factors.

- Whether the expected rate of development is realistic, taking into account local market evidence and, for large sites, the likely number of developers and outlets (distinctive parts of the development, with different products and potentially separate developers).

- Whether the general assumptions on lead-in times, such as the length of time it might take to address all the policy requirements, obtain planning permission, discharge the conditions and prepare the site for development, are justified.

214. The spreadsheet and trajectory can be adjusted through this process. The final version of the spreadsheet will then form the basis for the delivery trajectory in the adopted plan.

### Maintaining adequate housing land supply over the life of the plan

215. Whether the plan allows for an adequate supply of housing land over the life of the plan will be tested at examination. The particular issues arising in connection with the 5 year housing land supply are discussed in the next section.

216. The housing delivery trajectory, including the spreadsheet and its narrative, may highlight potential risks or inadequacies in the supply of housing at some point in the life of the plan. In addition, representations may call into question the ability of certain sites to deliver homes at the rate anticipated by the authority.

[^1]: Annex 1 to this section of the chapter sets out some more detailed questions that could be asked when testing deliverability.

<!-- Part3 p.54 -->

217. There is inevitably a degree of uncertainty about the start dates and build out rates of allocated sites, and the same applies to sites with planning permission on which development has not yet started.

218. The authority can insure against the risk of falling short against its housing requirement in a number of ways:

- By identifying a good range of sites of different types and sizes for homes designed to meet different markets and needs. This will provide the plan with resilience in the face of market uncertainty (See NPPF 73).

- By creating supply headroom – in which potential housing supply is greater than the housing requirement – so that if some sites fall short in delivery the housing requirement will continue to be met. (See paragraphs 206 to 208).

- By careful scrutiny of the factors and constraints influencing start dates and delivery rates, particularly where particular sites are expected to play a key role in delivery. This requires dialogue with site promoters and developers, and the examination should be provided with the latest information from them. It may be helpful for the site promoter or developer of a key or strategic site to be present at the relevant examination hearings to provide detailed information about these matters.

219. Shortfalls in the first 5 years are addressed in the next section.

220. If the evidence at the examination indicates that the plan overall is unlikely to meet the housing requirement, or that there is a significant risk that it will not do so, main modifications may be required to make the plan sound. This could include bringing forward other site allocations or amending policies to allow for more windfall development. However, such changes are likely to require further consultation, with the risk that the plan will be delayed, and a pragmatic approach needs to be taken. Significant failings in this matter are a matter of soundness unless there are very good and justifiable reasons that justify a shortfall against for housing requirement. Sometimes Inspectors have required the insertion of statements or policies in plans which state that a review will be undertaken should annual monitoring indicate that housing supply has fallen below the annual housing requirement, but the latest Regulations have considerably narrowed the scope for this. (See paragraphs 98 to 100).

<!-- Part3 p.55 -->

221. Although the plan should seek to meet the full housing requirement, there may be uncertainty about the contribution of certain sites towards the end of the plan period, and this may appear on the trajectory as a potential shortfall in supply at that time. This may not be as significant as shortfalls in the early years of the plan, because the plan will very likely have been reviewed by then. NPPF 72 only requires sites for years 11-15 to be identified where possible.

222. If the plan’s purpose is not to meet housing requirement or the 5 year housing land in full, but to meet it in conjunction with a neighbourhood plan, the local plan should make this clear. (See paragraphs 177 to 179).

## Five-year housing land supply

223. One of the requirements of the NPPF is that planning policies should identify a sufficient supply of specific deliverable sites for the five years following the adoption of the plan, with an appropriate buffer. This is referred to as a five-year housing land supply (5YHLS). Deliverable sites are available now, offer a suitable location for development now, and are achievable with a realistic prospect that housing will be delivered on the site within 5 years. (See paragraphs 131 to 134), and the NPPF Glossary. Assessing whether there will be a 5 year supply of housing on the anticipated date of the plan’s adoption (normally taken as the next 1 April following the anticipated adoption date) is an important test of the plan’s soundness.

### The calculation of the five-year housing land supply

224. Detailed guidance on calculating the 5YHLS is provided in the PPG “Housing supply and delivery”.

225. The 5YHLS should be assessed looking forward five years from the intended date of adoption of the plan.

226. The 5YHLS is calculated by comparing the supply of deliverable sites for the first 5 years following adoption against the housing requirement for the same period as identified in the plan’s strategic housing policy, with an appropriate buffer. (NPPF 78; PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 005) Annex 1 of this chapter contains some sample questions that might be used in an examination of site deliverability. Annex 2 contains some worked examples of the 5YHLS calculation in different scenarios.

<!-- Part3 p.56 -->

227. The buffer is in effect housing supply which is moved forward from later in the plan period and is added to the housing requirement for the first 5 years following adoption. That housing requirement figure will have been adjusted to account for under or over supply since the start of the requirement period. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 022) The application of the buffer does not change the plan’s overall housing requirement.

228. The buffer is 5% of the 5 year housing requirement as a minimum and 20% where the delivery of housing over the previous 3 years has fallen below 85% of the requirement, as set out in the latest published Housing Delivery Test results. The purpose of the buffer is to ensure choice and competition in the market and, in the case of 20% buffer, to improve the prospect of achieving the planned supply. (NPPF 78; NPPF Glossary; PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 013.)

229. The Housing Delivery Test is published annually and measures net homes delivered in the local authority area. It applies the day following its publication. Further information on this subject can be found at Paragraphs 027 to 038 of the PPG chapter “Housing supply and delivery”.

230. Where there is a stepped housing requirement, each of the annual requirements for the first 5 years are added together to produce a requirement for the 5 year period against which the housing supply is assessed. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 17).

231. Where the housing requirement is expressed as a range, the 5YHLS is measured against the lower end of the range. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 18).

### The role of windfalls in the five-year housing land supply

232. Local planning authorities sometimes seek to include an average allowance for windfalls in the 5YHLS calculation for the last 2 years of the 5 year period. Usually only the last 2 years is included to avoid double counting extant permissions.

<!-- Part3 p.57 -->

233. Neither the NPPF nor the PPG say specifically that an annual average allowance for windfall sites should or should not be included in the calculation. There is a question as to whether windfalls are “specific sites” as referred to in the NPPF. However, local planning authority supply calculations normally include windfalls, and this is generally accepted as a pragmatic approach provided the figures are realistic and evidence-based. Some urban authorities might be heavily reliant on windfalls rather than allocated sites. The annual average figure will be examined to see whether it is realistic, having regard to past and anticipated trends and capacity studies.

### Testing the five year housing land supply at examination

234. In all cases where the plan’s purpose includes the provision of a supply of housing land to meet its housing requirement, the plan will be tested to see whether it provides a 5 year housing land supply in accordance with NPPF 72.

235. This is important because if a planning authority cannot demonstrate a 5 year housing land supply in an application or appeal situation, the policies that are most important for determining the application will be considered out of date – even if the plan is newly adopted – and the provisions of NPPF 11 d will apply. (See also the ITM Housing chapter).

236. Any uncertainty about delivery from sites that are being included in the 5 year housing land supply is likely to be critically exposed at examination. In this regard, supply headroom (in which the potential overall housing provision in the plan area for the first 5 years exceeds the plan’s housing requirement) may help in ensuring the plan is effective. (See paragraphs 206 to 208).

237. For the examination, a schedule of deliverable sites should be provided, with a commentary on their expected delivery trajectories and any potential impediments to delivery. The cut-off date for the provision of 5 year housing land supply evidence for the examination should be as close as reasonably possible to the intended adoption date (normally the 1 April immediately after submission). For practical purposes it will usually coincide with the deadline for the submission of hearing statements or any 5 year housing land supply topic paper.

238. The evidence provided should be sufficient to provide confidence that there will be a 5 year housing land supply from the intended date of adoption. A topic paper on the subject is usually very helpful.

<!-- Part3 p.58 -->

### Where there are difficulties in identifying a 5 year housing land supply

239. Assessing the 5 year housing land supply is not an exact science. There will usually be discussion at the examination around the start dates and delivery rates of sites, and about whether some sites have stalled or will not come forward. There is bound to be uncertainty, and Inspectors will recognise this and take a fair and realistic approach.

240. Demonstrating with confidence that a 5 year housing land supply will exist on adoption can be a challenge for local planning authorities. Authorities should be able to demonstrate that they have made every effort to bring forward sites to meet the 5 year supply requirement. It is not desirable to have a situation where a plan’s housing policies are deemed out of date (for example at appeal) almost immediately after adoption. However, there may be instances where a 5 year housing land supply clearly cannot be demonstrated at examination. In such cases there are various options.

241. One option is for the local planning authority to make additional site allocations during the course of the examination. There are two problems with this. The first is that it might not be possible to bring forward sites that are capable of such quick delivery that they can remedy a deficit in the 5 year supply. The second is that site identification and consultation can delay the examination. This option might be acceptable if the work can be carried out very quickly (for example if there are already brownfield candidates or eligible sites assessed through the SHLAA), in which case the changes can be made through main modifications.

242. A second option is to make policy changes such as adjustments to settlement boundaries, the creation of a permissive policy that allows development adjacent to settlements, or the relaxation of windfall policies. These might assist in raising windfalls, although it would be difficult to put a figure on the contribution such policy changes would make. They might be effective where there is marginal uncertainty about the ability to maintain a 5 year supply.

243. A third option, where justified, might be to step the housing requirement or spread any under-delivery over the whole plan period, thus taking some of the delivery burden away from the first 5 year period. This might be relevant where the authority is trying to compensate for under-delivery in the first year(s) from the start of the housing requirement, or where delivery from its site allocations is expected to come forward later in the plan period. (See paragraphs 86 to 92) However, this approach will have a knock-on effect on the supply requirement in later years and should not simply be used as a means of neutralising the 20% buffer or delaying important decisions on site allocations.

<!-- Part3 p.59 -->

244. Where the process of finding additional sites for early delivery is likely to create undue delay in the examination, it might still be possible to find the plan sound overall without a 5 year housing land supply, provided the plan’s overall supply still meets its overall housing requirement and is sound in other respects.

245. On the other hand, a serious 5 year housing supply deficit without remedy, in a plan with other significant soundness problems, might point towards an overall soundness failure.

### Five year supply in site allocation plans

246. “Part 2” site allocation plans may identify a proportion, rather than all, of the total land supply needed to ensure that the adopted “part 1” plan’s housing requirement can be met. In such cases, it may be necessary to consider whether the distribution and capacity of sites and the pace of providing them in the site allocations plan is consistent with the adopted “part 1” plan which might include the provision of a five year housing land supply. If it is not, or if some of the proposed sites are not sound, then it may be necessary to seek modifications to incorporate further sites in order to ensure consistency with the adopted plan. In this limited sense, the site allocations plan may play a role in helping to maintain a rolling five year supply of housing land but this should not be confused with demonstrating that the Council has a five year supply at any particular point in time.

## Assessing and meeting the need for affordable housing

### Calculating affordable housing need

247. Affordable housing is defined in the NPPF Glossary. The calculation of affordable housing need is explained in PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 022 and PPG “Housing needs of different groups” Paragraph 007. The total net figure for affordable housing need is arrived at by subtracting total affordable housing stock from total gross need. The net figure is then annualised.

248. As part of this calculation, gross affordable housing need is estimated by assessing past trends and current estimates of household numbers within the categories of need listed in PPG “Housing needs of different groups”, Paragraph 006. Projections of affordable housing need should take in the range of socio-economic factors referred to in PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment”, Paragraph 21.

<!-- Part3 p.60 -->

### Policies for affordable housing

249. Where a need for affordable housing is identified, planning policies should specify the type of affordable housing required. The mix should meet identified local needs across Social Rent, other affordable housing for rent and affordable home ownership tenures and policies should specify the minimum proportion of Social Rent homes required. Policies should expect affordable housing to be met on-site unless off-site provision or an appropriate financial contribution in lieu can be robustly justified and the agreed approach contributes to the objective of creating mixed and balanced communities. (NPPF 64 and 66).

250. To achieve this, plans normally include policies that require a percentage of any new major housing development to be provided in the form of affordable housing. Plans may also include affordable housing policies for specific site allocations. The percentage sought is often the same across the plan area, but in some plans certain site allocations or locations may be given a different affordable housing requirement to reflect characteristics, needs, aspirations and viability.

251. Other than in designated rural areas, the provision of affordable housing should not be sought for residential developments that are not major developments (see the NPPG Glossary definition). Planning authorities often try to include policies in their draft plans which seek contributions towards the provision of affordable housing from non-major developments on the grounds that their identified affordable housing need is high and the plan’s site allocations are mostly small. However, this is contrary to the NPPF’s stated policy. Other than for designated rural areas, the NPPF does not allow for any exceptions. (NPPF 65).

252. In designated rural areas (National Parks, National Landscapes and areas designated under s.157 of the Housing Act 1985) policies may set a lower threshold of 5 units or fewer. (NPPF 65).

253. Viability assessment is clearly critical to ensure that development is deliverable. Policy requirements, particularly for affordable housing, should be realistic and deliverable. The affordable housing requirement, including the percentages sought, should be supported by robust viability evidence. PPG ID: 10 provides detailed guidance on this subject. There are special policies for housing development on sites released from the Green Belt: see below.

### Affordable housing on sites released from the Green Belt by the plan

<!-- Part3 p.61 -->

254. Where a new or reviewed plan releases land from the Green Belt for major housing development, a specific affordable housing requirement or requirements should be set for the housing sites that have been released. Under the “Golden Rules”, these should be set higher than elsewhere, and a single rate or different rates can be used for different sites. At least 50% of the housing should be affordable unless it would make the development of the site unviable when tested against national planning practice guidance on viability. (NPPF 67 and 68).

255. Despite the latter statement, PPG “Viability” states that site specific viability assessment should not be undertaken or taken into account in respect of sites released from the Green Belt for the purpose of reducing developer contributions, including affordable housing. The government intends to review viability guidance to consider whether there are circumstances where site specific viability may be taken into account, but such a review has not yet been published. (PPG “Viability” Paragraph 029).

256. It follows that until the review has been completed, site specific viability assessment cannot be taken into account in setting the percentage of affordable housing on these sites. This may present a potential challenge for the authority in demonstrating deliverability, particularly in the case of strategic sites. Indeed, PPG “Viability” advises that it is important to consider the specific circumstances of strategic sites. (PPG “Viability” Paragraph 005).

257. However, the PPG also states that assessing the viability of plans does not require the individual testing of every site. A typology approach may be used. On this basis overall plan viability assessments, including typology approaches, could be used to establish the general viability context for the plan and this could inform the minimum level of affordable housing for sites released from the Green Belt. But this should still be set higher than for other sites. (PPG “Viability” Paragraphs 3 and 4).

258. The approach to addressing the particular housing needs of other groups will vary according to identified need. Plans should allocate sites for, or have positive policies towards, gypsies and travellers, student housing, housing for service families and sheltered housing for older or disabled people, accommodation for looked-after children and private rented housing. The needs of older people and families with children should be addressed by policies on housing mix. Policies could allocate sites for those wishing to build or commission their own homes, or require such provision on large allocated sites.

259. Plan-making authorities should consider how these needs can be addressed within the overall housing need that has been established. As with affordable housing (see below), it may be that the plan is unable meet all the identified needs in full, but all reasonable steps should be taken to meet them as far as is practicable. (PPG “Needs of different groups” Paragraph 1).

<!-- Part3 p.62 -->

### Community-led development for housing

260. Plan policies should support proposals for community-led development for housing. These are developments taken forward by, or with, a not-for-profit organisation that is primarily for the purpose of meeting the needs of its members or the wider local community rather than being a primarily commercial enterprise. The NPPF Glossary contains further detail.

### First homes

261. First homes are a type of discounted market sale housing and meet the definition of affordable housing. They are discounted by a minimum of 30% against the market value and are subject to eligibility criteria. The discount is passed on in subsequent sales. (PPG “First Homes” Paragraph 001).

262. The former requirement to deliver a minimum of 25% of affordable housing as First Homes was removed in the December 2024 NPPF and no longer applies. However, delivery of First Homes can continue where local planning authorities judge that they meet local need.

## Assessing and meeting the specialist housing needs of different groups

263. Note: affordable housing is dealt with in the preceding section.

### Identifying needs

264. As well as establishing the housing need figure from the standard method, the authority should assess the housing needs of different groups, including size, type and tenure. These groups should include (but are not limited to) those who require affordable housing (including Social Rent); families with children; looked after children; older people (including those who require retirement housing, housing-with-care and care homes); students; people with disabilities; service families; travellers; people who rent their homes and people wishing to commission or build their own homes. (NPPF 63, PPG *Housing needs of different groups”).

265. The identification of these housing needs does not alter in any way the figure for local housing need established through the standard method. Rather, specialist housing need is met through appropriate plan policies, and in some circumstances by adjusting the housing requirement. (See paragraph 53). (PPG “Housing needs of different groups” Paragraph 001).

<!-- Part3 p.63 -->

266. PPG “Housing needs of different groups” sets out guidance for calculating private rented sector needs, self-build and custom housebuilding needs, student housing needs and affordable housing needs.

267. Policies dealing with the housing needs of older people, people with disabilities, ethnic Gypsies and Travellers and looked after children are relevant to the Public Sector Equality Duty (PSED), as those groups have protected characteristics under the Equality Act 2010. See the sections of this ITM chapter and other chapters of the ITM dealing with PSED and on Gypsies, Travellers and Travelling Showpeople.

### Older and disabled people

268. Plans should set clear policies to address the housing needs of older and disabled people. (PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” Paragraph 006).

269. The number of people aged 85 and over is expected to double between 2016 and 2041 and the impact of this on specialist housing needs should be considered from the plan’s earliest stages. (PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” Paragraph 1).

270. It is also important to meet the needs of disabled people through their lifetime. Definitions of older people and people with disabilities are included in the NPPF.

271. PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” contains advice on sources of evidence and toolkits for assessing the housing needs of older and disabled people. (PPG *Housing for older and disabled people” Paragraphs 3 to 5).

272. Local authorities are expected to take a positive approach to schemes that propose to address specialist housing needs. Plans should set out how the authority will consider proposals for the different types of housing that these groups are likely to require.

273. Specialist accommodation for older people can include age-restricted general market housing, retirement living or sheltered housing, extra care housing or housing-with-care, residential care homes and nursing homes. However, many older people do not want specialist accommodation and may wish to stay in their own homes or move to more suitable general housing such as bungalows or homes that can be adapted. (PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” Paragraph 010).

<!-- Part3 p.64 -->

274. As well as including relevant policies, plans can provide indicative figures or a range for the number of specialist units.

275. Plans may also include site allocations for older persons’ housing. Such site allocations are often requested at examinations by interested parties. Where evidence justifies it, they could be included in the plan. They may be appropriate where there is an identified unmet need for specialist housing. (PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” Paragraph 013).

276. Housing for older people counts towards the housing land supply, based on the amount of accommodation released back into the market. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 026) Independent self-contained living units for older people are likely to be counted on a one for one basis.

### Accessible housing

277. PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” provides advice on accessible and adaptable housing including wheelchair user dwellings. Where an identified need exists, plans are expected to make use of the optional technical housing standards. Policies can set out the proportion of new housing that will be delivered to the Building Regulations standards of M4(2) “Accessible and adaptable dwellings” and M4(3) “Wheelchair user dwellings”. Policies should be based on evidence of need, viability and site specific factors. (PPG “Housing for older and disabled people”. Paragraph 009)

278. There is a distinction within Part M of the Building Regulations between wheelchair accessible (a home readily accessible by a wheelchair user at the point of completion) and wheelchair adaptable (a home that can be easily adapted to meet the needs of a household including wheelchair users). Local plan policies for wheelchair accessible homes should be applied only to those dwellings where the local authority is responsible for allocating or nominating a person to live in the dwelling. (PPG “Housing optional technical standards” Paragraph 009).

### Student housing

<!-- Part3 p.65 -->

279. Planning authorities need to plan for sufficient student accommodation, engaging with universities and other educational establishments to understand the need for student accommodation in the area. Encouraging more dedicated student accommodation can take the pressure off the private rented sector and increase the overall housing stock. (PPG “Housing needs of different groups” Paragraph 004) (See paragraph 54)

280. Student accommodation can be counted towards housing land supply, based on:

- the amount of accommodation that new student housing releases in the wider housing market (by allowing existing properties to return to general residential use); and / or

- the extent to which it allows general market housing to remain in such use, rather than being converted for use as student accommodation.

281. The calculation is based on the average number of students living in student only accommodation, using published census data. Studio flats are counted on a one for one basis. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 025).

### Self-build and custom housing

282. Self-build and custom build housing, which is defined in the Self-build and Custom Housebuilding Act 2015, helps to diversify the housing market and increase consumer choice. It covers a wide spectrum, from projects where individuals are involved in building or managing the construction of their home from beginning to end, to projects where individuals commission their home, making key design and layout decisions, but the home is built ready for occupation, (PPG “Self-build and custom housing” Paragraphs 016 and 016a)

283. The need for self-build and custom housing should be assessed and reflected in planning policies. (NPPF 63).

284. Adequate attention needs to be given to this subject in plan preparation and at examination. This is important given the duty on local authorities described below.

285. A range of authorities, including district councils and London boroughs, are required to keep a self-build and custom housebuilding register. The list of authorities is set out in PPG “Self-build and custom housebuilding” Paragraph

001. The register includes people (either alone or with others) who are seeking to acquire a serviced plot of land in the relevant authority’s area for their own self-build and custom housebuilding project. There are eligibility tests for inclusion on the register. (PPG “Self-build and custom housebuilding” Paragraph 008).

<!-- Part3 p.66 -->

286. There is a duty on local authorities under the Self-build and Custom Housebuilding Act 2015 (as amended by the Housing and Planning Act 2016) to give suitable development permission (which includes planning permission) to enough serviceable plots of land to meet the demand for self-build and custom housebuilding in their area.

287. The level of demand is established by reference to the number of entries added to an authority’s register during a base period. Base periods run for 12 months from 31 October to 30 October the following year. At the end of the base period, relevant authorities have 3 years to give permission for an equivalent number of plots of land as there are entries for the base period, unless they are granted an exemption by the Secretary of State. Exemptions are granted if the demand on the register is more than 20% of the future deliverable housing supply. (PPG “Self-build and custom housebuilding” Paragraphs 023, 024, 030 and 031).

288. The registers that relate to the area of a local planning authority – and the duty to have regard to them – need to be taken into account in preparing planning policies. (PPG “Self-build and custom housing” Paragraph 014).

289. Therefore, in evaluating the need for self-build and custom homes, plan-making authorities should use the demand data from the registers in their area. This may be supported as necessary by additional data from secondary sources. Secondary sources can include data from building plot search websites, enquiries for building plots recorded by local estate agents and surveys of local residents. Demand assessment tools can also be used. (PPG “Self-build and custom housing” Paragraph 011).

290. A list of ways in which authorities can increase the number of planning permissions suitable for self-build and custom housing is set out in Paragraph 025 of PPG “Self-build and custom housebuilding”.

291. Appropriate planning policies might include supportive general policies, specific site allocations for self-build and custom homes, and site allocation criteria for larger sites which require a portion of the development to be devoted to self-build and custom plots.

292. Policies might be aligned with other plan policies relating to affordable and community-led housing, brownfield site regeneration and the re-use of public sector land. (PPG “Self-build and custom housebuilding”) paragraph 014).

<!-- Part3 p.67 -->

### Mixed tenure, the private rented sector, Build to Rent and Co-living accommodation

293. Plan policies should support the development of mixed tenure sites, although this should not preclude schemes that are mainly, or entirely, for Social Rent or other affordable housing tenures. Mixed tenure sites can provide a range of benefits, including creating diverse communities and supporting timely build out rates. Mixed tenure sites can include a mixture of ownership and rental tenures, including Social Rent, other rented affordable housing and build to rent, as well as housing designed for specific groups such as older people’s housing and student accommodation, and plots sold for custom or self-build. (NPPF 71).

294. There are various sources of information available to assess the future needs for private rented sector housing. (PPG “Housing needs of different groups” Paragraph 002).

295. Planning policies can seek to exert control over tenure in new developments to serve a particular planning purpose. This happens in the case of affordable housing, where control is enforceable through a planning obligation or through conditions. There may be other circumstances where evidence strongly indicates a need for new development to address a particular kind of tenure. This might be a social need, or it might serve a wider objective, such as private rented homes for key workers.

296. Where clear need is demonstrated, plans might contain positive policies towards meeting such needs in new homes, and in the conversion of vacant buildings, where this requires planning permission. Where clearly justified plans might contain specialist housing allocations, or development requirements.

297. Where it can be demonstrated that an existing stock or pool of private rented accommodation plays an important part in meeting a particular social need, or provides accommodation of a particular size, and it is under threat through, for example, conversions to other forms of accommodation, or amalgamations to form larger units, plans might contain policies to resist the loss of that accommodation where it requires planning permission.

298. Recent plans have included policies for Build to Rent and Co-living accommodation, which may be important in meeting the needs of particular groups including younger people. Local planning authorities should demonstrate that they have considered the need for these forms of housing and ways in which provision may be made for them.

### Service families

<!-- Part3 p.68 -->

299. Plan-making authorities should be aware of the needs for service family accommodation and plans should seek to accommodate their needs through positive policies and where relevant through site allocations.

### Families with children, and looked after children

300. The authority should consider the needs of families with children and looked after children and reflect this in the plan policies on housing mix, housing allocations, active travel, social and educational facilities and placemaking. This is important in the wider context of driving up housing provision, increasing densities and making more intensive use of land. Meeting the needs of families with children, including their space requirements and social and recreational needs, is itself part of the process of making the best use of land.

301. Considerations for plans might include the size of dwellings and gardens, open space and playspace provision, the location of schools, the encouragement of active travel and safe walking and cycling routes, the location of childcare and other social facilities, and urban design considerations such as the natural surveillance of playspaces.

302. The authority should demonstrate that the plan gives full consideration to these needs. The issues might be addressed in a variety of ways in plans, such as through policies requiring masterplans to be produced for larger sites, site allocation requirements, and general development management policies, including those which seek to support childcare proposals.

## Housing in rural areas

### Assessing rural housing need

303. Where a plan includes a rural area, the plan making authority will need to demonstrate an understanding of rural housing needs. This will help to inform policies that support housing developments reflecting those needs. (PPG “Housing needs of different groups” 67-009-20190722).

304. In rural areas, planning policies should be responsive to local circumstances and support housing developments that reflect local needs. (NPPF 82).

305. It follows that, in relevant areas, the authority should have clear evidence of the level and type of local housing need and should reflect this in the plan’s policies, including site allocations. (PPG “Housing needs of different groups” Paragraph 009).

<!-- Part3 p.69 -->

306. Housing should be located where it will enhance or maintain the vitality of rural communities. Planning policies should identify opportunities for villages to grow and thrive, especially where this will support local services. Where there are groups of smaller settlements, development in one village may support services in a village nearby. (NPPF 83).

307. A wide range of settlements can play a role in delivering sustainable development in rural areas, so blank policies restricting housing development in certain settlements will need to be supported by robust evidence. (PPG “Housing needs of different groups” Paragraph 009).

308. Neighbourhood plans can allocate housing sites, and where the local plan anticipates that some of the housing requirement is going to be met in this way, it should set targets for the number of homes that are expected to be delivered by each neighbourhood planning area, where relevant. (See paragraphs 177 to 179).

309. In designated rural areas, affordable housing may be sought on non-major sites.

### Rural exception sites

310. Policies should also support opportunities to bring forward rural exception sites that will provide affordable housing to meet identified local needs. These are small sites used for affordable housing in perpetuity where sites would not normally be used for housing. Rural exception sites seek to address the needs of the local community by accommodating households who are either current residents or have an existing family or employment connection. (NPPF 82 and NPPF Glossary).

311. A proportion of market homes may be allowed on the rural exception site at the local planning authority’s discretion, for example where essential to enable the delivery of affordable units without grant funding. This will need to be addressed in the policy.

312. The wording of any plan policy that concerns rural exception sites will need to make sure that it does not contain criteria that unnecessarily impede the ability to bring such sites forward. Location criteria should be avoided that would have the effect of restricting the rural exception sites to land that could be brought forward under other planning policies, rendering the policy ineffective.

<!-- Part3 p.70 -->

### Isolated homes in the countryside

313. Planning policies should seek to avoid the development of isolated homes in the countryside other than in the circumstances listed in NPPF 84. Plans should normally adhere to the wording of NPPF 84 and any proposed variation will need to be supported by clear and convincing local evidence and will be tested at the examination.

314. NPPF “Housing needs of different groups”, paragraph 010, provides a list of considerations that might be taken into account in assessing specific local needs for such housing.

315. The judgement of what is isolated is a matter for the decision maker, but the local plan may assist by providing a definition.

## Plan policies, including site allocation policies

### General policies

316. Plans should take into account all the points that have been discussed above, together with the following.

### Site allocation policies

317. New site allocations are identified by the plan’s policies and are shown on the policies map. Larger sites normally have their own policies which contain development criteria, thresholds and requirements. Smaller sites are sometimes presented in tabular form, along with a brief description of the development requirements expected of them.

### Housing numbers in site allocation policies

318. Each site allocation policy should include the estimated number of homes that the site allocation is expected to deliver. If delivery is going to extend beyond the plan period, the number of homes that the site is expected to deliver during the plan period should be specified, along with an estimate of the full site capacity.

<!-- Part3 p.71 -->

319. Some draft plans have sought to avoid attaching housing numbers to draft allocations, for example on mixed use sites where there are uncertainties around the proportion of housing that will delivered. However, the plan should provide a best estimate based on evidence, if necessary in the form of a range. This is needed for the calculation of overall housing provision, the housing trajectory and the 5 year housing supply and it also enables an assessment to be made of whether the best use is being made of the land.

### Policy criteria

320. The requirements and criteria set out in any site allocation policy need to be critically examined because they should be aligned with the legal requirements of the CIL Regulations which are also set out in the Planning Obligations PPG (32b-002-20190901). That is because development that subsequently takes place on the site may need to be accompanied by a planning obligation and/or be subject to conditions. The development criteria should therefore be:

- necessary to make the development acceptable in planning terms;

- directly related to the development; and

- fairly and reasonably related in scale and kind to the development.

321. The criteria should, where appropriate, seek to further NPPF and plan-wide objectives in order to make the development acceptable in planning terms.

322. The policy and its criteria specific and clear about the affordable housing requirement – the percentage of affordable housing that will be sought on the site.

323. Development criteria could include references to masterplanning, design codes, design review and community engagement, particularly on larger sites. Densities and local distinctiveness will also be relevant, as will any stated objective to re-model and improve an area.

324. Requirements may need to be set out in respect of trees and ancient woodland, heritage assets and protected species and habitats.

325. The plan may also seek on site school, library, open space and community provision, highway works and sustainable transport measures, and contributions towards such works and measures off-site. These will be scrutinised and considered in relation to the Infrastructure Delivery Plan.

<!-- Part3 p.72 -->

326. Not everything may be achievable on all sites; what can be achieved will depend on development constraints and viability. There is also a risk of being over-prescriptive in the list of development requirements. This may impair the plan’s effectiveness by reducing flexibility and making it more difficult for otherwise acceptable schemes to come forward on the allocated site.

327. It may not be possible to be precise anyway. For example, the details of a transport infrastructure project may not have been firmed up and there may be alternative proposals. In some instances, it may be better to express more general objectives and leave the detail to any subsequent planning application.

328. Policy criteria seeking contributions that do not appear to be directly linked to planning or to the development, and those which could or should be addressed by other sources of funding, may not comply with the CIL Regulations. Examples might be contributions towards operational emergency services kit, such as cars, radio and CCTV, or contributions towards the employment of individuals in particular roles (such as community liaison, employment development or sustainable transport officers).

### The GDPO, permitted development rights for changes of use to residential, and the use of Article 4 directions

329. Permitted development rights granted by the GPDO allow, subject to criteria, changes of use to residential without full planning permission. The purpose is to increase housing supply on brownfield land. Generally this issue does not concern local plan examinations, but it occasionally comes up.

330. Firstly, it can have an impact on windfall numbers. For example, it may be argued that the creation of the PD rights for this purpose has led to a bulge in windfall numbers which has now passed, thus casting doubt on the reliability of the Council’s time-series data on windfalls. As with all potential housing sources, the evidence may need to be critically evaluated.

331. Secondly, it is possible that a plan might contain a statement that residential to office permitted development rights will be removed by means of an Article 4 direction. Even if this does not have the force of a DM policy, its inclusion in the plan signals an intended action that could have an impact on the supply of new homes through the conversion of redundant office floorspace, or could result in the failure to make the best use of urban land, both of which are relevant to national policy and the development plan strategy. The removal of national permitted development rights by Article 4 directions should only be used where it is necessary to avoid wholly unacceptable adverse impacts. The implications of such a statement of intent may therefore need to be examined.

<!-- Part3 p.73 -->

### Optional technical housing standards

332. Local planning authorities have the option to set additional technical requirements exceeding the minimum standards required by Building Regulations in respect of access and water, and an optional nationally described space standard.

333. The setting of additional technical requirements should be evidence-based and the authority should consider the implications of using these standards as part of the Local Plan viability assessment.

### Accessible housing policies

334. The issue of accessible housing has been discussed above in relation to the housing needs of older and disabled people. (See paragraphs 267 to 275) PPG “Housing optional technical standards”, Paragraphs 5 to 12, contain guidance on this subject.

### Water consumption, water conservation and grey water

335. Some parts of the country may have the potential for an imbalance of water demand over supply. The plan should adopt proactive strategies that take account of water supply and demand considerations, having regard to climate change. There is a national standard in the Building Regulations of 125 litres/person/day, but where there is a clear local need, local plan policies can require new dwellings to meet the tighter Building Regulations optional requirement of 110 litres/person/day. Guidance on establishing a local need Is set out in PPG “Optional technical standards” Paragraphs 15 to 17.

336. Although not mentioned in the PPG “Optional technical standards”, it may be appropriate for the plan under its climate change obligations to contain policies seeking the conservation of rainwater and the re-cycling of rainwater.

### Internal space standards

<!-- Part3 p.74 -->

337. Local authorities may specify internal space standards for new homes. But they should only do so by reference to the nationally described space standard. The document “Technical housing standards – nationally described space standard” was published in 2015. (PPG “Housing optional technical standards, Paragraph 018).

### Energy standards

338. Some submitted plans contain policies on zero carbon and energy efficiency standards. Advice on dealing with this in the context of relevant legislation and national policy is set out in the local plan ITM chapter on sustainability appraisal and climate change.
