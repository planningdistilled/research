# 37g Local Plan Examinations (NPPF 2024): Housing: Assessing housing land availability

<!-- Part3 p.35 -->

## Assessing housing land availability

### The strategic housing land availability assessment (SHLAA)

103. Strategic plan-making authorities should make an assessment of the number of new homes that can be provided in their area, justified by evidence on land availability, development constraints and other relevant matters They should have a clear understanding of available housing land through the preparation of a strategic housing land availability assessment. This is commonly referred to as a SHLAA. (NPPF 72; PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 040; PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment”).

104. The SHLAA identifies how much land in the plan area is suitable, available and achievable for housing (and usually economic) development during the plan period. In doing so it should recognise constraints, including areas where the Framework would provide strong reasons for restricting the scale, type or distribution of development.

105. The SHLAA only provides information on the range of sites available; it is for the plan itself to decide which of those sites are the most suitable. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraphs 001 and 002).

### Stages in site assessment

106. The PPG sets out 5 stages for the identification of sites and broad locations and it contains a link to a methodology flowchart. Plan-making authorities are expected to have regard to this guidance and any reasons for departing from it should be explained in the plan’s evidence base. Neighbourhood planning bodies may also use it in a proportionate manner, or they may use existing assessments prepared by the local planning authority. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraphs 003 to 005).

<!-- Part3 p.36 -->

### Identification of sites

107. Stage 1 is the identification of all sites and broad locations, to provide a complete audit of available land regardless of the amount of development needed. The area covered by the SHLAA should be the plan-making area, which could be the planning authority area, two or more areas, or areas covered by a spatial development strategy. This stage includes a desktop review, a call for sites and broad locations, and a survey of sites and broad locations. The PPG contains advice on what these elements should include. It also contains a link to a table setting out possible data sources for different types of sites.

108. Policies, constraints and an initial assessment of suitability can be recorded by the survey – but that does not mean that sites should be ruled out at this stage. The NPPF envisages that plan preparation or review might involve the provision of housing on land released from the Green Belt under exceptional circumstances, for example, where an authority cannot meet its identified need for homes. (NPPF 145 and 146).

109. The assessment needs to be undertaken working with other local planning authorities in the relevant housing market area as well as with landowners and promoters, developers, local communities, parish councils and others. A range of site sizes capable of delivering 5 or more dwellings should be considered. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraphs 6 to 15).

### Assessment of sites

110. Stage 2 is a site and broad location assessment. This involves the estimation of the development potential of each site or broad location identified in Stage

1. It can take into account constraints: for example, it should reflect the policies for the topics in Footnote 7 of the Framework. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 002).

111. It can be guided by policy, including local density policies, but should seek to make the most efficient use of land in accordance with policies in the Framework.

<!-- Part3 p.37 -->

112. In addition to assessing development potential, an evaluation should be made of the site’s suitability for housing, its availability, and its achievability, including whether the site is economically viable. National policy, market attractiveness, contribution to regeneration priority areas, potential impacts on landscape, nature and heritage and other factors, policy changes and other emerging sites can all be considered, as can land ownership, the existence of planning permission, lead-in times and the delivery track record of developers or landowners. This exercise will help to establish whether a site can be considered deliverable within the next 5 years or over a longer period. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraphs 016 to 022)

### Windfalls

113. Stage 3 is a windfall assessment where this is justified. Windfall sites are sites that are not specifically identified in the development plan. A windfall allowance can be justified in the anticipated supply if there is sound evidence that it will provide a reliable source of housing land supply. (See paragraphs 158, 159, 202, 232 and 233.) (NPPF 75; PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 22).

### Assessment review

114. Stage 4 is an assessment review which considers the development potential of all the sites to produce an indicative delivery trajectory. This sets out how much housing is likely to come forward from the sites at different points in the future – in other words, within years 1-5 following adoption, years 6-10 and years 11 and beyond, based on the evidence gathered in stages 1 to 3. The trajectory should also be subject to an overall risk assessment as to whether sites will come forward as anticipated. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 024).

115. The presumption is that local authorities should meet all their housing needs. If the assessment indicates that there are insufficient sites or broad locations to meet need, the assessment will need to be revisited; a further call for sites may be carried out and the development potential of particular sites may be re-evaluated to ensure that they make the most efficient use of land. Green Belt boundaries should be reviewed and alterations may be proposed. (See paragraphs 117 to 124 and 136 to 146). (NPPF 146).

116. However, if insufficient land remains, and there is clear evidence that strategic policies cannot meet all the needs, it will be important to establish how needs might be met in adjoining areas through statements of common ground. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 025).

<!-- Part3 p.38 -->

### Green Belt review

117. Green Belt boundaries should only be altered where exceptional circumstances are fully evidenced and justified through the preparation or updating of plans. Exceptional circumstances include instances where an authority cannot meet its identified need for homes.

118. If that is the case, authorities should review Green Belt boundaries and propose alterations to meet those needs in full, unless the review provides clear evidence that doing so would fundamentally undermine the purposes (taken together) of the remaining Green Belt, when considered across the area of the plan. Grey belt land should be identified to inform the review. (NPPF 145 and 146 and PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 001).

119. Local authorities should produce a Green Belt assessment as part of the review of Green Belt boundaries. The review will inform local plan preparation and the preparation of spatial development strategies. (PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 002).

120. PPG “Green Belt” sets out the steps for the Green Belt assessment. The assessment should be based on sufficiently granular areas to enable an assessment to be made of their variable contribution to Green Belt purposes. A small number of large assessment areas will generally not be appropriate. Assessment areas can vary in size depending on circumstances. (PPG “Green Belt” Paragraphs 003 and 004).

121. The PPG also provides a list of considerations to be taken into account in assessing the contribution that assessment areas make to Green Belt purposes. (PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 005).

122. Any assessment area that is not judged to contribute strongly to any one of the purposes a. b. or d. of the Green Belt (to check the unrestricted sprawl of large built up areas, to prevent neighbouring towns merging into one another, and to preserve the setting and special character of historic towns) can be identified as grey belt land – except where the application of policies relating to the areas or assets in footnote 7 to the NPPF (other than Green Belt) would provide a strong reason for refusing or restricting development.(PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 007).

123. Authorities should also consider whether, or the extent to which, the release of Green Belt land (not just grey belt land) would affect the ability of all the remaining Green Belt across the area of the plan from serving all five of the Green Belt purposes in a meaningful way. (PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 008).

<!-- Part3 p.39 -->

124. Land released from the Green Belt for the purposes of meeting housing need will not necessarily be confined to grey belt land. The identification of grey belt will inform the assessment, but there are other important considerations, notably the objectives of the spatial strategy and the need to promote sustainable patterns of development, that will affect the choice of land for Green Belt land release should exceptional circumstances make this necessary. This is discussed further in the next section which deals with the selection of housing sites. (See paragraphs 136 to 146).

### Final evidence base

125. Stage 5 is the compilation of the final evidence base for the SHLAA. The standard outputs are a list of all the sites and broad locations considered, cross referenced to their locations on maps; an assessment of each site or broad location; instances where these have been discounted, including reasons; the potential type and quantity of development, estimated build-out rates and delivery barriers on suitable and available sites; and an indicative development trajectory. It should be reiterated here that it is for the plan to decide which sites are the most suitable to take forward. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 026).

### Steps to be taken if the assessment is found inadequate at examination

126. If the assessment of housing land availability, including any Green Belt review, is found inadequate at examination, the LPA will be asked to try to rectify the deficiencies. This may result in more available land being identified, enabling a higher housing requirement figure to be set. However, the approach must be pragmatic and have regard to what can be achieved in the examination without undue delay.
