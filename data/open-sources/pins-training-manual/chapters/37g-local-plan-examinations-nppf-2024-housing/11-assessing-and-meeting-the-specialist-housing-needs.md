# 37g Local Plan Examinations (NPPF 2024): Housing: Assessing and meeting the specialist housing needs of different groups

<!-- Part3 p.62 -->

## Assessing and meeting the specialist housing needs of different groups

263. Note: affordable housing is dealt with in the preceding section.

### Identifying needs

264. As well as establishing the housing need figure from the standard method, the authority should assess the housing needs of different groups, including size, type and tenure. These groups should include (but are not limited to) those who require affordable housing (including Social Rent); families with children; looked after children; older people (including those who require retirement housing, housing-with-care and care homes); students; people with disabilities; service families; travellers; people who rent their homes and people wishing to commission or build their own homes. (NPPF 63, PPG *Housing needs of different groups”).

265. The identification of these housing needs does not alter in any way the figure for local housing need established through the standard method. Rather, specialist housing need is met through appropriate plan policies, and in some circumstances by adjusting the housing requirement. (See paragraph 53). (PPG “Housing needs of different groups” Paragraph 001).

<!-- Part3 p.63 -->

266. PPG “Housing needs of different groups” sets out guidance for calculating private rented sector needs, self-build and custom housebuilding needs, student housing needs and affordable housing needs.

267. Policies dealing with the housing needs of older people, people with disabilities, ethnic Gypsies and Travellers and looked after children are relevant to the Public Sector Equality Duty (PSED), as those groups have protected characteristics under the Equality Act 2010. See the sections of this ITM chapter and other chapters of the ITM dealing with PSED and on Gypsies, Travellers and Travelling Showpeople.

### Older and disabled people

268. Plans should set clear policies to address the housing needs of older and disabled people. (PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” Paragraph 006).

269. The number of people aged 85 and over is expected to double between 2016 and 2041 and the impact of this on specialist housing needs should be considered from the plan’s earliest stages. (PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” Paragraph 1).

270. It is also important to meet the needs of disabled people through their lifetime. Definitions of older people and people with disabilities are included in the NPPF.

271. PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” contains advice on sources of evidence and toolkits for assessing the housing needs of older and disabled people. (PPG *Housing for older and disabled people” Paragraphs 3 to 5).

272. Local authorities are expected to take a positive approach to schemes that propose to address specialist housing needs. Plans should set out how the authority will consider proposals for the different types of housing that these groups are likely to require.

273. Specialist accommodation for older people can include age-restricted general market housing, retirement living or sheltered housing, extra care housing or housing-with-care, residential care homes and nursing homes. However, many older people do not want specialist accommodation and may wish to stay in their own homes or move to more suitable general housing such as bungalows or homes that can be adapted. (PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” Paragraph 010).

<!-- Part3 p.64 -->

274. As well as including relevant policies, plans can provide indicative figures or a range for the number of specialist units.

275. Plans may also include site allocations for older persons’ housing. Such site allocations are often requested at examinations by interested parties. Where evidence justifies it, they could be included in the plan. They may be appropriate where there is an identified unmet need for specialist housing. (PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” Paragraph 013).

276. Housing for older people counts towards the housing land supply, based on the amount of accommodation released back into the market. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 026) Independent self-contained living units for older people are likely to be counted on a one for one basis.

### Accessible housing

277. PPG “Housing for older and disabled people” provides advice on accessible and adaptable housing including wheelchair user dwellings. Where an identified need exists, plans are expected to make use of the optional technical housing standards. Policies can set out the proportion of new housing that will be delivered to the Building Regulations standards of M4(2) “Accessible and adaptable dwellings” and M4(3) “Wheelchair user dwellings”. Policies should be based on evidence of need, viability and site specific factors. (PPG “Housing for older and disabled people”. Paragraph 009)

278. There is a distinction within Part M of the Building Regulations between wheelchair accessible (a home readily accessible by a wheelchair user at the point of completion) and wheelchair adaptable (a home that can be easily adapted to meet the needs of a household including wheelchair users). Local plan policies for wheelchair accessible homes should be applied only to those dwellings where the local authority is responsible for allocating or nominating a person to live in the dwelling. (PPG “Housing optional technical standards” Paragraph 009).

### Student housing

<!-- Part3 p.65 -->

279. Planning authorities need to plan for sufficient student accommodation, engaging with universities and other educational establishments to understand the need for student accommodation in the area. Encouraging more dedicated student accommodation can take the pressure off the private rented sector and increase the overall housing stock. (PPG “Housing needs of different groups” Paragraph 004) (See paragraph 54)

280. Student accommodation can be counted towards housing land supply, based on:

- the amount of accommodation that new student housing releases in the wider housing market (by allowing existing properties to return to general residential use); and / or

- the extent to which it allows general market housing to remain in such use, rather than being converted for use as student accommodation.

281. The calculation is based on the average number of students living in student only accommodation, using published census data. Studio flats are counted on a one for one basis. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 025).

### Self-build and custom housing

282. Self-build and custom build housing, which is defined in the Self-build and Custom Housebuilding Act 2015, helps to diversify the housing market and increase consumer choice. It covers a wide spectrum, from projects where individuals are involved in building or managing the construction of their home from beginning to end, to projects where individuals commission their home, making key design and layout decisions, but the home is built ready for occupation, (PPG “Self-build and custom housing” Paragraphs 016 and 016a)

283. The need for self-build and custom housing should be assessed and reflected in planning policies. (NPPF 63).

284. Adequate attention needs to be given to this subject in plan preparation and at examination. This is important given the duty on local authorities described below.

285. A range of authorities, including district councils and London boroughs, are required to keep a self-build and custom housebuilding register. The list of authorities is set out in PPG “Self-build and custom housebuilding” Paragraph

001. The register includes people (either alone or with others) who are seeking to acquire a serviced plot of land in the relevant authority’s area for their own self-build and custom housebuilding project. There are eligibility tests for inclusion on the register. (PPG “Self-build and custom housebuilding” Paragraph 008).

<!-- Part3 p.66 -->

286. There is a duty on local authorities under the Self-build and Custom Housebuilding Act 2015 (as amended by the Housing and Planning Act 2016) to give suitable development permission (which includes planning permission) to enough serviceable plots of land to meet the demand for self-build and custom housebuilding in their area.

287. The level of demand is established by reference to the number of entries added to an authority’s register during a base period. Base periods run for 12 months from 31 October to 30 October the following year. At the end of the base period, relevant authorities have 3 years to give permission for an equivalent number of plots of land as there are entries for the base period, unless they are granted an exemption by the Secretary of State. Exemptions are granted if the demand on the register is more than 20% of the future deliverable housing supply. (PPG “Self-build and custom housebuilding” Paragraphs 023, 024, 030 and 031).

288. The registers that relate to the area of a local planning authority – and the duty to have regard to them – need to be taken into account in preparing planning policies. (PPG “Self-build and custom housing” Paragraph 014).

289. Therefore, in evaluating the need for self-build and custom homes, plan-making authorities should use the demand data from the registers in their area. This may be supported as necessary by additional data from secondary sources. Secondary sources can include data from building plot search websites, enquiries for building plots recorded by local estate agents and surveys of local residents. Demand assessment tools can also be used. (PPG “Self-build and custom housing” Paragraph 011).

290. A list of ways in which authorities can increase the number of planning permissions suitable for self-build and custom housing is set out in Paragraph 025 of PPG “Self-build and custom housebuilding”.

291. Appropriate planning policies might include supportive general policies, specific site allocations for self-build and custom homes, and site allocation criteria for larger sites which require a portion of the development to be devoted to self-build and custom plots.

292. Policies might be aligned with other plan policies relating to affordable and community-led housing, brownfield site regeneration and the re-use of public sector land. (PPG “Self-build and custom housebuilding”) paragraph 014).

### Mixed tenure, the private rented sector, Build to Rent and Co-living accommodation

293. Plan policies should support the development of mixed tenure sites, although this should not preclude schemes that are mainly, or entirely, for Social Rent or other affordable housing tenures. Mixed tenure sites can provide a range of benefits, including creating diverse communities and supporting timely build out rates. Mixed tenure sites can include a mixture of ownership and rental tenures, including Social Rent, other rented affordable housing and build to rent, as well as housing designed for specific groups such as older people’s housing and student accommodation, and plots sold for custom or self-build. (NPPF 71).

294. There are various sources of information available to assess the future needs for private rented sector housing. (PPG “Housing needs of different groups” Paragraph 002).

295. Planning policies can seek to exert control over tenure in new developments to serve a particular planning purpose. This happens in the case of affordable housing, where control is enforceable through a planning obligation or through conditions. There may be other circumstances where evidence strongly indicates a need for new development to address a particular kind of tenure. This might be a social need, or it might serve a wider objective, such as private rented homes for key workers.

296. Where clear need is demonstrated, plans might contain positive policies towards meeting such needs in new homes, and in the conversion of vacant buildings, where this requires planning permission. Where clearly justified plans might contain specialist housing allocations, or development requirements.

297. Where it can be demonstrated that an existing stock or pool of private rented accommodation plays an important part in meeting a particular social need, or provides accommodation of a particular size, and it is under threat through, for example, conversions to other forms of accommodation, or amalgamations to form larger units, plans might contain policies to resist the loss of that accommodation where it requires planning permission.

298. Recent plans have included policies for Build to Rent and Co-living accommodation, which may be important in meeting the needs of particular groups including younger people. Local planning authorities should demonstrate that they have considered the need for these forms of housing and ways in which provision may be made for them.

### Service families

<!-- Part3 p.68 -->

299. Plan-making authorities should be aware of the needs for service family accommodation and plans should seek to accommodate their needs through positive policies and where relevant through site allocations.

### Families with children, and looked after children

300. The authority should consider the needs of families with children and looked after children and reflect this in the plan policies on housing mix, housing allocations, active travel, social and educational facilities and placemaking. This is important in the wider context of driving up housing provision, increasing densities and making more intensive use of land. Meeting the needs of families with children, including their space requirements and social and recreational needs, is itself part of the process of making the best use of land.

301. Considerations for plans might include the size of dwellings and gardens, open space and playspace provision, the location of schools, the encouragement of active travel and safe walking and cycling routes, the location of childcare and other social facilities, and urban design considerations such as the natural surveillance of playspaces.

302. The authority should demonstrate that the plan gives full consideration to these needs. The issues might be addressed in a variety of ways in plans, such as through policies requiring masterplans to be produced for larger sites, site allocation requirements, and general development management policies, including those which seek to support childcare proposals.
