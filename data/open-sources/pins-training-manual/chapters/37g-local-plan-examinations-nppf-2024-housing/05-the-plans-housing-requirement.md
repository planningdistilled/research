# 37g Local Plan Examinations (NPPF 2024): Housing: The plan’s housing requirement

<!-- Part3 p.23 -->

## The plan’s housing requirement

### The definition and purpose of the housing requirement

35. The housing requirement is the minimum number of homes that a plan seeks to provide from all sources during the plan period. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 002). The requirement should apply from the same date as that which the local housing need is based on (under the standard method, the date to which the affordability ratio relates).

36. The overall aim of the housing requirement should be to meet an area’s identified housing need, including an appropriate mix of housing types for the local community and taking into account where appropriate unmet need from nearby authority areas.

### The application of the housing requirement

37. The housing requirement is normally expressed in the plan as an annual average figure, as in X dpa (dwellings per annum). It is used to inform:

- the 5 year housing land supply calculation.

<!-- Part3 p.24 -->

- the number of new homes that the plan should make provision for through new site allocations, and when they are expected to come forward.

- the housing requirement for designated neighbourhood areas.

- the monitoring of delivery.

38. The housing requirement, which is the actual requirement of the plan, should not be confused with the delivery trajectory, which concerns the anticipated supply of new housing over the plan period. To provide clarity on all these matters, it is critical that the housing requirement is set out unambiguously in the plan’s policies.

### Establishing the plan’s housing requirement

39. The starting point for establishing the housing requirement will normally be the local housing need figure derived from the most recent standard method calculation before the submission of the plan for examination. See paragraphs 20 to 24 and paragraphs 28 to 30.

40. The housing requirement may be higher than the local housing need figure (NPPF 69) in order, for example, to:

- accommodate the unmet needs of neighbouring areas;

- meet growth ambitions linked to economic development or infrastructure investment;

- meet all of, or a higher proportion of, affordable housing need, or help towards meeting the needs of older people or other particular groups.

41. The housing requirement may be lower than the local housing need figure in some plan areas where it is not possible to meet local need owing to constraints (NPPF 11b), giving rise to unmet housing need which must be addressed by the authority.

42. These issues are discussed below.

### Raising the housing requirement to accommodate the unmet housing need of neighbouring areas

<!-- Part3 p.25 -->

43. Plans look at least 15 years ahead following adoption, so if evidence demonstrates that one authority will be unable to meet its local housing need within the life of the plan (for example in a city whose boundaries are tightly drawn around the built up area, where a recent local plan has not been able to identify enough sites), the unmet housing need should be explicitly quantified, including the different components of that need, such as affordable housing. This unmet need should be addressed in the local plans of neighbouring or nearby authorities (usually those within the same housing market area) through statements of common ground and/or a memorandum of understanding. (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 025) (See also paragraph 62.)

44. Where an authority agrees to make explicit additional housing provision within its plan to accommodate another authority’s unmet need, it should normally be integrated into the plan’s housing requirement either for the whole plan period, or part of the plan period, depending on when the need arises. For example, the exporting authority may have sufficient land for the first few years of the plan but is predicted to be in deficit from a specific year, from which point the receiving authority’s annual housing requirement will increase to take it into account. In such cases a stepped requirement may result (see paragraphs 78 to 85). Thus where an authority is taking a proportion of another’s fully quantified and evidenced unmet need, it should be included in the housing requirement as an average annual figure. This provides the assurance that the unmet need will be met in the recipient authority.

45. Occasionally, if one authority has an oversupply of sites and there is evidence that an adjacent authority has unmet need, a pragmatic approach might be to regard the supply headroom as a potential contribution towards the neighbour’s unmet need. This approach may be helpful where there is plenty of supply headroom in the local plan area and strong evidence of unmet need in a nearby authority which has not been precisely quantified (perhaps because its plan is at an earlier stage in the local plan process or because its unmet need has not been apportioned between different authorities). Care is needed with this however, because if the recipient authority’s site allocations suffer delays and under-delivery, its supply headroom will be eroded and the authority will not meet its neighbour’s unmet need after all, Thus generally it is preferable to integrate the allowance for unmet need into the receiving authority’s housing requirement, which creates an important incentive to ensure that it is met. (See paragraphs 206 to 208).

<!-- Part3 p.26 -->

46. It might be argued that, since unmet need in one authority will exert pressure on its neighbours’ housing markets, the affordability element of the standard method calculation will automatically raise the LHN of the neighbouring authorities such that it should not be necessary for those authorities to make an upward adjustment to their LHN to cater for unmet need. The difficulty with this argument is that LHN only takes into account existing affordability at the time of calculation, whereas local plans look forward over a period of 15 to 20 years and should plan positively to meet identified need over that period. For this reason significant amounts of unmet housing need that have been identified by one authority through its local plan process should be explicitly addressed by neighbouring authorities through their own local plans.

### Raising the housing requirement to meet growth ambitions linked to economic growth or infrastructure investment

47. The government supports authorities who wish to plan for growth. In such cases the housing requirement may be set higher than the identified housing need. Additional housing may be planned for to support growth objectives, encourage innovation and improved productivity, make the best use of existing or new infrastructure or address potential barriers to economic investment, such as inadequate housing to support growth in terms of numbers or mix. (NPPF 8, 69. 86).

48. This approach may have a basis in a national or strategic growth strategy, a more local growth strategy, or in the plan’s specific economic objectives. But it should not normally have to be thoroughly justified at examination. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 2a).

### Raising the housing requirement to meet affordable housing need or the needs of older people or specific groups

<!-- Part3 p.27 -->

49. It is common for the calculation of affordable housing need to produce a figure which exceeds or is proportionally high compared with the housing need figure derived from the standard method. In these situations, the amount of affordable housing delivered through the plan’s policies by applying the affordable housing percentage to major housing developments would fall short of meeting the identified affordable housing need. (See paragraphs 248 to 249).

50. In such circumstances, authorities should ensure that they are seeking the maximum viable percentage of affordable housing. An increase in the plan’s overall housing requirement could be considered where it could help towards delivering the required number of affordable homes. The same effect might be achieved through a housing supply in excess of the housing requirement. The additional housing would be subject to the policy requirement that a certain percentage should be affordable. In this way, more affordable homes would be delivered. (PPG “Housing and economic needs assessment” Paragraph 024).

51. Where an increase in the housing requirement is being proposed for this purpose, there needs to be adequate evidence as to whether, and to what extent, there is demand for the additional market housing in the area, whether the market can deliver the additional number of homes, and whether the additional housing can be brought forward viably. (PPG “Viability”).

52. If all options have been considered, and the plan seeks the maximum amount of affordable housing subject to viability and deliverability (including meeting other important plan objectives), it may be concluded that the objective of meeting affordable housing need through the plan’s policies has been met as far as it is possible to do so.

53. Normally the housing needs of older people and specific groups such as students are met through appropriate policies, but in certain (relatively unusual) instances it might be necessary to raise the housing requirement to rectify the potential under-delivery of specific housing types or to create a different balance of house types to meet plan objectives. (See paragraphs 263 to 264).

54. In the case of students, the standard method figure for local housing need may to a degree reflect current supply pressures, but predicted future changes in student numbers during the life of the plan may need to be taken into account. Planning authorities will therefore need to plan for student accommodation needs. (See paragraphs 278 to 280).

### Second homes, short lets, holiday lets and vacant homes

<!-- Part3 p.28 -->

55. Neither the NPPF nor the PPG indicates that any adjustment is required to the housing requirement to take account of these factors. With the introduction of the standard method, and particularly from December 2024, the affordability component of the calculation should mean that current supply pressures arising from these factors are reflected in the LHN figure. That said, the matter may still need to be considered in certain areas where the issue is acute or where a substantial change is forecast.

### Where the housing requirement might be set lower than local housing need

56. Plans should meet objectively assessed needs. Local authorities are normally expected to meet all their housing needs within their areas. However, the circumstances described in NPPF 11 b) might arise. Owing to constraints, or where the authority’s boundaries are tightly drawn, it might not be possible to meet all the housing needs within the plan boundaries. In these circumstances, the draft plan might propose setting a lower housing requirement than local housing need.

57. Authorities are expected first and foremost to meet their own needs so the evidence put forward to support setting a lower housing requirement will be tested thoroughly at examination. It will be important for the authority to demonstrate convincingly that the best and most efficient use has been made of the sites identified in the SHLAA.

58. The authority will also need to demonstrate convincingly that every possible source of land has been investigated and that available and developable sites have not been discounted for inadequate reasons. The potential for mitigating identified impacts (for example, environmental, landscape or highway impacts) to enable a site to come forward should have been properly considered. It is common for developers and landowners to present arguments that “omission sites” have been inappropriately discounted and are available to meet some or all of the housing need. Omission sites do not form part of the plan and are not subject to examination (see paragraph 180) but where a submitted plan does not meet local housing need yet there is evidence of a number of credible omission sites or SHLAA sites that have not been allocated, this might suggest that further investigation is required into potential opportunities to enable local housing need to be met.

59. In such circumstances the Inspector might ask the local planning authority to identify further housing sites. A pragmatic approach will need to be taken here to avoid delaying the examination process, depending on the extent of the shortfall and the ease with which additional sites can be identified. It is not the role of the Inspector to identify which sites should be selected.

<!-- Part3 p.29 -->

60. Where the plan contains Green Belt, NPPF 146 indicates that the exceptional circumstances necessary to justify the release of land from the Green Belt include instances where an authority cannot meet its identified need for housing through other means. In these circumstances, LPAs should review Green Belt boundaries and propose alterations to meet these needs in full, unless the review provides clear evidence that such alterations would fundamentally undermine the purposes of the remaining Green Belt across the area of the plan as a whole (NPPF 146). Before concluding that exceptional circumstances exist, all other reasonable options for meeting identified need should be examined fully. (NPPF 147) (See also paragraphs 136-148 below).

61. A failure to establish an adequate housing requirement may cause a plan to fail at examination on the grounds that it is unsound. However, if, having thoroughly examined the matters discussed above, it is adequately demonstrated that the plan must contain a lower housing requirement than the figure for local housing need, there will be a level of unmet housing need within the plan area.

62. Authorities which cannot meet all their housing needs should quantify the types of housing for which there is unmet need in their areas and should have a statement of common ground and memorandum of understanding with one or more authorities to give confidence at the time of the examination that it will be accommodated. (See paragraphs 43 to 46). (PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 025).

### Where the housing requirement will not normally need to be adjusted

63. The housing requirement will not normally need to be adjusted to address fluctuations in the economy, societal behaviour and the housing market. These are common and generally on a shorter cycle than the life of the plan. Caution needs to be exercised over arguments at examination that circumstances have permanently and substantially changed since the housing need figure was established and the requirement was set. This argument was frequently raised at the time of the COVID pandemic but with little genuine evidence.

### Housing requirement issues in non-strategic plans such as site allocation plans

64. This section applies to non-strategic plans such as Part 2 site allocation plans that have been prepared subsequent to strategic plans to which the policies in the 2024 Framework apply. Non-strategic plans put into effect the policies of an adopted strategic-level local plan by, for example, allocating sites or setting out development management policies.

65. Part 2 site allocation plans should therefore work within the housing requirement set by the Part 1 strategic-level local plan. With the narrow exception described in paragraph 67 below, it is NOT within the remit of non-strategic plans such as Part 2 site allocation plans to set, review or revise the housing requirement, even if there have been substantial changes in housing need from the figures on which the Part 1 Plan is based.

66. That is because regulation 8 of the 2012 Regulations requires that the policies contained in a local plan must be consistent with the adopted development plan, unless they are explicitly intended to supersede policies in the adopted plan. Moreover, site allocations in non-strategic plans that are intended to give effect to the policies in a strategic plan should be consistent with the strategic plan’s housing requirement and spatial strategy. This approach was tested and found sound in Gladman Development Ltd v Wokingham BC [2014] EWHC 2320 (Admin), and Oxted Residential Ltd v Tandridge DC [2016] EWCA Civ 414.

67. The only circumstance under which a Part 2 plan can reconsider the housing requirement is if the Part 1 strategic-level local plan contains a policy that specifically gives this task to the non-strategic plan.

68. The housing need figure calculated through the standard method can be relied on for plan making for 2 years from the date of the strategic level plan. (See paragraphs 28-29) Therefore non-strategic / Part 2 local plans that follow closely behind the Part 1 plan can rely on that figure.

69. If there is a longer time lapse, the implication is that the standard method figure for housing need on which the strategic plan is based cannot be relied on for plan-making.

70. A degree of fluctuation in the local housing need figure over time arising from adjustments to the affordability ratio may reasonably be expected. If more than 2 years have passed since the date of the submission of the strategic plan, a view will need to be taken as to whether the changes to the housing need figure are significant enough to call into question the provisions of the non-strategic plan.

<!-- Part3 p.31 -->

71. After the two year period, if there is evidence of a significant change in circumstances, for example as a result of substantially revised affordability or household projections or national policy, the matter should be addressed through a review of the strategic plan, NOT through a non-strategic / Part 2 plan.

72. This is particularly relevant where land in the Green Belt has been proposed for release for housing on the grounds of exceptional circumstances and the non-strategic plan seeks to allocate such sites. A significant drop in housing need may call into question whether exceptional circumstances still exist to justify the allocation of those sites. A significant rise in housing need may call into question whether there are exceptional circumstances that justify additional sites to be released from the Green Belt.

73. In such instances the strategic plan will need to be reviewed to establish a new housing requirement figure and consider the implications for the allocation of land, including the spatial strategy and the release of Green Belt. It is not appropriate to try to do this through the non-strategic plan. Issues of this sort were considered in Aireborough Neighbourhood Development Forum v Leeds City Council & Others [2020] EWHC 1461 (Admin).

74. Non-strategic plans should be very clear as to their purpose and their relationship with any adopted strategic plan. The purposes of the non-strategic plan should be set out in the text of the plan itself and should also be reflected in the local development scheme (LDS). If the plan and the LDS are unclear on this matter, this will be raised early in the examination. If necessary the appropriate explanation can be inserted through a main modification.

### Expressing the housing requirement

75. The plan’s strategic policies should set out both the overall housing requirement for the plan period as a whole, and the requirements that apply in each year of the plan period. For example:

“15,000 dwellings are required to be provided between 1 April 2025 and 31 March 2040. The annual average requirement is for 1,000 dwellings in each year of that period.”

76. In this example, the total requirement for the plan period is 15,000 dwellings, the plan period is 15 years, and the annual average requirement remains the same over the plan period, at 1,000 dpa (15,000 divided by 15). This is not an annual target of any sort, it is an annual average.

77. The usual expectation is that the housing requirement is set as an annual average figure to be met in each year of the plan period.

<!-- Part3 p.32 -->

### Stepped housing requirements

78. In certain circumstances, the plan’s housing requirement might be stepped. A stepped requirement means that the yearly requirement varies during the plan period.

79. Local plans may set stepped requirements where there is to be a significant change in the level of housing requirement between emerging and previous policies, and/or where strategic sites will have a phased delivery or are likely to be delivered later in the plan period. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” 68-020-20190722).

80. The London Plan provides an example. The London Plan at paragraph 4.1.10 recognises that the increase in housing delivery required by its targets may be achieved gradually and London boroughs are encouraged to set out a realistic and, where appropriate, stepped housing delivery target over a ten-year period.

81. A stepped requirement may be needed to address the situation where housing delivery between the plan’s start date and its adoption has been substantially higher or lower than the annualised housing requirement figure (see above).

82. A stepped requirement might also be used where a plan is accommodating unmet need from another authority and that unmet need is expected to arise at a certain point in the plan period, for example later in the plan period. (See paragraph 44).

83. A stepped requirement might be expressed as follows:

“15,000 dwellings are required to be provided between 1 April 2025 and 31 March 2040. The annual average requirement is for 800 dwellings in each of the five years from 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2030, and for 1,100 dwellings in each of the ten years from 1 April 2030 to 31 March 2040.”

84. In this example the plan’s annual average housing requirement is lower over the first 5 years of the plan period before stepping up for the latter 10 years.

85. Stepped requirements should be justified by evidence and should not be used to unnecessarily delay meeting identified development needs. They should also ensure that the planned housing requirements are met fully within the plan period.

<!-- Part3 p.33 -->

### Addressing lower housing delivery rates (“under-delivery”) between the plan’s start date and its adoption

86. Between the start date of the housing requirement and the plan’s adoption, housing delivery in the plan area (as identified by annual monitoring) may have been lower than the average annual figure required to meet the overall housing requirement.

87. Under-delivery in this period is compensated for by re-setting the average annual requirement, usually for part of the plan period, so that the overall housing requirement is still met. It does not involve any change in the plan’s overall requirement.

88. The PPG expects any shortfall from the early years to be made up within the first five years after adoption unless a longer period can be justified as part of the plan-making and examination process. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 022).

89. This approach will probably result in a stepped housing requirement – see above. It may involve setting two steps, a step up to remedy the shortfall in the first 5 years after adoption, followed by a downward step.

90. A longer period for making up the shortfall is sometimes argued for by the authority on the basis that the plan’s delivery cannot step quickly enough. This will be tested at examination, having regard among other things to past delivery rates, the capacity of the market and the anticipated start dates and delivery rates of pipeline and allocated sites.

91. This requires a realistic approach as to what is achievable. However, if there is a reliance on large sites with long lead in times which makes it difficult to remedy the shortfall over the first 5 years, the potential for releasing smaller sites with shorter lead-in times should be considered.

92. Note that any shortfall should only be counted from the start of the housing requirement and should be calculated using the plan’s housing requirement for the years in question. Any shortfall that occurred before the start of the housing requirement should not be included as it will automatically be accounted for through the standard method.

### Transitional arrangements referring to the housing requirement and LHN

93. NPPF 234 sets out the circumstances under which the policies in the 2024 NPPF should be applied in local plan examinations, and the circumstances under which the plan should instead be examined under the relevant previous version of the NPPF.

<!-- Part3 p.34 -->

94. One of the criteria for assessing whether a previous version of the NPPF applies is whether the plan has reached its Regulation 19 stage on or before 12 March 2025, “and its draft housing requirement meets at least 80% of local housing need”. Participants at some examinations have argued that, to calculate this percentage, any component of a plan’s housing requirement which is designed to meet the unmet needs of a neighbouring authority should first be deducted from the housing requirement. Taking this approach might reduce the relevant figure to less than 80% of local housing need, which would mean that the plan would be examined under the 2024 NPPF rather than any previous version.

95. However, NPPF paragraph 234a does not say explicitly that unmet housing need should first be deducted from the housing requirement figure. Policies should be read on their face and interpretation is a matter for the courts. There is no case law relating directly to NPPF 234a but there have been several judgments which are relevant. Perhaps the closest is that of Tesco Stores Ltd v Stockport MBC [2025] EWCA Civ 610 in which Sir Keith Lindblom SPT stated “As a general rule, the temptation to infer terms the policy-maker has not actually used should be resisted”.

96. Therefore, a straightforward reading of paragraph 234a is that the draft housing requirement, not some subset of it, should be 80% or more of the LHN figure. Interpreting policy to include this additional calculation when it is not specifically stated in the policy invites a risk of challenge. In the absence of an explicit statement to the contrary to the effect, the straightforward reading of NPPF 234a should be followed.

97. Further information on this issue will be set out in a forthcoming PINS Note.

### Transitional arrangements and early review policies

98. It has been common practice in local plan examinations for Inspectors to require ‘early review’ policies to be inserted into the plan through a main metafiction as a pragmatic way of overcoming a soundness problem.

99. Inspectors are able to recommend main modifications introducing policies that commit the local authority to commencing production of a new-style local plan before required to do so by the Local Plan Regulations 2026. But the need for and effectiveness of such policies should be considered in the light of the dates set by the Regulations. Clearly, the sooner the requirement to commence plan preparation under the new system, the less likely a main modification of this sort will be required.

<!-- Part3 p.35 -->

100. As regards partial reviews, the legislative framework for the new system only allows for one local plan to have effect for each local authority area at any one time. There is no clear route for local planning authorities to submit an early partial update policy, and it follows that a policy requiring an early partial review would be ineffective. Early partial review policies and early partial update policies should not be included in main modifications.

101. It is also unlikely, because of the legislative and draft policy framework, that supplementary plans will provide a vehicle for overcoming soundness issues.

102. A fuller explanation of these points is set out in PINS Note 02/2026.
