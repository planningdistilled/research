# 37g Local Plan Examinations (NPPF 2024): Housing: Plan policies, including site allocation policies

<!-- Part3 p.70 -->

## Plan policies, including site allocation policies

### General policies

316. Plans should take into account all the points that have been discussed above, together with the following.

### Site allocation policies

317. New site allocations are identified by the plan’s policies and are shown on the policies map. Larger sites normally have their own policies which contain development criteria, thresholds and requirements. Smaller sites are sometimes presented in tabular form, along with a brief description of the development requirements expected of them.

### Housing numbers in site allocation policies

318. Each site allocation policy should include the estimated number of homes that the site allocation is expected to deliver. If delivery is going to extend beyond the plan period, the number of homes that the site is expected to deliver during the plan period should be specified, along with an estimate of the full site capacity.

<!-- Part3 p.71 -->

319. Some draft plans have sought to avoid attaching housing numbers to draft allocations, for example on mixed use sites where there are uncertainties around the proportion of housing that will delivered. However, the plan should provide a best estimate based on evidence, if necessary in the form of a range. This is needed for the calculation of overall housing provision, the housing trajectory and the 5 year housing supply and it also enables an assessment to be made of whether the best use is being made of the land.

### Policy criteria

320. The requirements and criteria set out in any site allocation policy need to be critically examined because they should be aligned with the legal requirements of the CIL Regulations which are also set out in the Planning Obligations PPG (32b-002-20190901). That is because development that subsequently takes place on the site may need to be accompanied by a planning obligation and/or be subject to conditions. The development criteria should therefore be:

- necessary to make the development acceptable in planning terms;

- directly related to the development; and

- fairly and reasonably related in scale and kind to the development.

321. The criteria should, where appropriate, seek to further NPPF and plan-wide objectives in order to make the development acceptable in planning terms.

322. The policy and its criteria specific and clear about the affordable housing requirement – the percentage of affordable housing that will be sought on the site.

323. Development criteria could include references to masterplanning, design codes, design review and community engagement, particularly on larger sites. Densities and local distinctiveness will also be relevant, as will any stated objective to re-model and improve an area.

324. Requirements may need to be set out in respect of trees and ancient woodland, heritage assets and protected species and habitats.

325. The plan may also seek on site school, library, open space and community provision, highway works and sustainable transport measures, and contributions towards such works and measures off-site. These will be scrutinised and considered in relation to the Infrastructure Delivery Plan.

<!-- Part3 p.72 -->

326. Not everything may be achievable on all sites; what can be achieved will depend on development constraints and viability. There is also a risk of being over-prescriptive in the list of development requirements. This may impair the plan’s effectiveness by reducing flexibility and making it more difficult for otherwise acceptable schemes to come forward on the allocated site.

327. It may not be possible to be precise anyway. For example, the details of a transport infrastructure project may not have been firmed up and there may be alternative proposals. In some instances, it may be better to express more general objectives and leave the detail to any subsequent planning application.

328. Policy criteria seeking contributions that do not appear to be directly linked to planning or to the development, and those which could or should be addressed by other sources of funding, may not comply with the CIL Regulations. Examples might be contributions towards operational emergency services kit, such as cars, radio and CCTV, or contributions towards the employment of individuals in particular roles (such as community liaison, employment development or sustainable transport officers).

### The GDPO, permitted development rights for changes of use to residential, and the use of Article 4 directions

329. Permitted development rights granted by the GPDO allow, subject to criteria, changes of use to residential without full planning permission. The purpose is to increase housing supply on brownfield land. Generally this issue does not concern local plan examinations, but it occasionally comes up.

330. Firstly, it can have an impact on windfall numbers. For example, it may be argued that the creation of the PD rights for this purpose has led to a bulge in windfall numbers which has now passed, thus casting doubt on the reliability of the Council’s time-series data on windfalls. As with all potential housing sources, the evidence may need to be critically evaluated.

331. Secondly, it is possible that a plan might contain a statement that residential to office permitted development rights will be removed by means of an Article 4 direction. Even if this does not have the force of a DM policy, its inclusion in the plan signals an intended action that could have an impact on the supply of new homes through the conversion of redundant office floorspace, or could result in the failure to make the best use of urban land, both of which are relevant to national policy and the development plan strategy. The removal of national permitted development rights by Article 4 directions should only be used where it is necessary to avoid wholly unacceptable adverse impacts. The implications of such a statement of intent may therefore need to be examined.

### Optional technical housing standards

332. Local planning authorities have the option to set additional technical requirements exceeding the minimum standards required by Building Regulations in respect of access and water, and an optional nationally described space standard.

333. The setting of additional technical requirements should be evidence-based and the authority should consider the implications of using these standards as part of the Local Plan viability assessment.

### Accessible housing policies

334. The issue of accessible housing has been discussed above in relation to the housing needs of older and disabled people. (See paragraphs 267 to 275) PPG “Housing optional technical standards”, Paragraphs 5 to 12, contain guidance on this subject.

### Water consumption, water conservation and grey water

335. Some parts of the country may have the potential for an imbalance of water demand over supply. The plan should adopt proactive strategies that take account of water supply and demand considerations, having regard to climate change. There is a national standard in the Building Regulations of 125 litres/person/day, but where there is a clear local need, local plan policies can require new dwellings to meet the tighter Building Regulations optional requirement of 110 litres/person/day. Guidance on establishing a local need Is set out in PPG “Optional technical standards” Paragraphs 15 to 17.

336. Although not mentioned in the PPG “Optional technical standards”, it may be appropriate for the plan under its climate change obligations to contain policies seeking the conservation of rainwater and the re-cycling of rainwater.

### Internal space standards

<!-- Part3 p.74 -->

337. Local authorities may specify internal space standards for new homes. But they should only do so by reference to the nationally described space standard. The document “Technical housing standards – nationally described space standard” was published in 2015. (PPG “Housing optional technical standards, Paragraph 018).

### Energy standards

338. Some submitted plans contain policies on zero carbon and energy efficiency standards. Advice on dealing with this in the context of relevant legislation and national policy is set out in the local plan ITM chapter on sustainability appraisal and climate change.
