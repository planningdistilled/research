# 37g Local Plan Examinations (NPPF 2024): Housing: Selecting housing sites

<!-- Part3 p.39 -->

## Selecting housing sites

### Site selection and allocation

127. Site allocations or broad areas for growth are the key components that enable the plan’s overall housing requirement to be met, when added to existing commitments, windfalls and completions since the start of the plan period.

<!-- Part3 p.40 -->

128. Selecting housing sites for inclusion in the plan is a separate exercise from the SHLAA although it may involve an iterative process. It should be an explicit, clear and logical piece of evidence. As with the SHLAA exercise, it should recognise the potential for realistic mitigation measures to enable good sites to come forward rather than discounting them at the first iteration.

### The relationship to the spatial strategy

129. The site selection process is integral to the achievement of the spatial strategy. The spatial strategy should be coherent, robust and based on positive planning. It should embody all three of the objectives of sustainable development in NPPF 8, economic, social and environmental. It may have growth objectives, support particular economic sectors or be related to infrastructure provision or sustainable movement patterns. It follows that housing site selection, including sites released from the Green Belt (see paragraphs 136 to 146) should not simply be defined by choosing those sites that have the fewest constraints (“sieve mapping”).

130. The authority should have a clear explanatory narrative (not just a tabular sustainability appraisal) about how the selection of new housing sites contributes to the delivery of the plan’s spatial strategy and its objectives. Where large scale strategic housing-led growth is proposed there needs to be clear and logical evidence to support the strategic site selection process. This may usefully be set out for the examination in a site selection topic paper.

### The identification of sites to come forward at different plan stages

131. Plans should identify a supply of specific, deliverable sites for five years following the intended date of the plan’s adoption. Deliverable means the site is available now, offers a suitable location for development now, and is achievable with a realistic prospect that housing will be delivered on the site within 5 years. (NPPF 72 and NPPF Glossary).

132. Plans should also identify a supply of specific, developable sites or broad locations for growth for years 6 - 10 and, where possible, for years 11 - 15. Developable means the site is in a suitable location for housing development with a reasonable prospect that it will be available and could be viably developed at the point envisaged. (NPPF 72 and NPPF Glossary).

133. The combination of sites selected for housing development should allow for appropriate amounts of development to come forward at different stages of the plan to enable a rolling 5 year supply of land to be maintained. The plan should identify a mix of sites, taking into account their availability, suitability and likely economic viability. (NPPF 70).

<!-- Part3 p.41 -->

134. Where, under the NPPF’s Housing Delivery Test, housing delivery has fallen below the threshold of 95% of the housing requirement, the local planning authority will have been required under the NPPF to produce a housing delivery action plan. When producing the local plan, planning authorities should consider the recommendations from the action plan. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 022) These recommendations might, for example, include unblocking stalled sites, increasing engagement with developers and considering additional housing allocations.

### The identification of priority sites for early delivery

135. Plan-making authorities may wish to identify priority sites which can be delivered earlier in the plan period, such as those on brownfield land and where there is supporting infrastructure in place such as transport hubs. These sites will provide additional flexibility and more certainty that authorities will be able to demonstrate a sufficient supply of deliverable sites against the housing requirement. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 012).

### Sites released from the Green Belt

136. Strategic plans should establish the need for any changes to Green Belt boundaries following the review of Green Belt. (See paragraphs 117 to 124).

137. The new Green Belt boundary is usually shown on the policies map in the strategic plan, but sometimes it is shown diagrammatically, with the detail to be resolved through a neighbourhood plan.

138. If there are exceptional circumstances that are considered to justify the release of land from the Green Belt, they must be fully justified by the authority and by the Inspector.

139. Before concluding that exceptional circumstances exist to justify changes to Green Belt boundaries, the strategic policy-making authority should be able to demonstrate that it has examined fully all other reasonable options for meeting its identified need for development. This will be assessed through the examination of its strategic policies, and will consider whether the strategy makes as much use as possible of suitable brownfield sites and underutilised land; optimises the density of development and has been informed by discussions with neighbouring authorities about whether they could accommodate some of the identified need for development. [NPPF 147]

140. In local plans which contain site allocations on land released from the Green Belt, exceptional circumstances need to be justified at two levels:

<!-- Part3 p.42 -->

- the strategic level – meeting housing need that cannot be met on appropriate alternative sites outside the Green Belt, the objectives of the spatial strategy, economic needs and growth considerations, sustainability considerations, the effect on the Green Belt purposes as a whole.

- the local level – the suitability of each selected site in terms of location, sustainability, the relation to the spatial strategy and the effect of the specific site on the purposes of the Green Belt.

141. In the “Calverton case” (Calverton Parish Council v Nottingham City Council & Ors [2015] EWHC 1078 (Admin) (21 April 2015)) Mr Justice Jay set out the steps that should ideally be taken in this process. After establishing the objectively assessed housing need in the area covered by a development plan document, an inspector should ideally consider the acuteness/intensity of the need, the constraints on the supply/availability of land suitable for development, the difficulties in achieving sustainability without impinging on the Green Belt, the nature and extent of the harm to this Green Belt, and how far the impacts on Green Belt purposes could be reduced.

142. When reviewing Green Belt boundaries, the need to promote sustainable patterns of development is relevant. Opportunities should be taken to maximise sustainable transport solutions. (PPG “Green Belt” Paragraph 010, NPPF 110 and 115).

143. The site selection exercise needs to take account of the bigger picture: the plan’s objectives, its spatial strategy and its sustainability objectives. Identifying grey belt is part of the process, but the choice of sites to be released from the Green Belt may not be confined to grey belt.

144. For example, it may be necessary to release land from the Green Belt to meet the housing needs of towns and cities, or support new areas of growth, in sustainable locations close to where that need arises. The most appropriate land to achieve that might not be grey belt.

145. If there are potential non-Green Belt sites, but they are demonstrably inferior in terms of sustainability and would fail to support national objectives or the strategic objectives of the plan, such as those described above, there may be exceptional circumstances for releasing more suitable land in the Green Belt.

146. As regards the remaining site allocation plans still in the examination system, it should not be necessary to carry out a further strategic level assessment of exceptional circumstances. provided they are demonstrably in accordance with the strategic plan, However, the chosen sites and their impact on the Green Belt will still need to be assessed at the local level.

### Large site allocations

147. The supply of large numbers of new homes can often be best achieved through planning for larger scale development, such as new settlements or significant extensions to existing villages and towns, provided they are well located and designed, and supported by the necessary infrastructure and facilities (including a genuine choice of transport modes). (NPPF 77).

148. Local plans call these larger sites by different names. For example, they may refer to large sites or groups of related sites as strategic allocations, sustainable urban extensions or new settlements, and use thresholds of 500, 1,000, 2,000 or more dwellings to define them. But the plan’s terminology in this instance is of little or no importance to its soundness. There is no legal or policy definition of what constitutes a strategic site or sustainable community.

149. The advantage of large sites is that they may be better able to support new infrastructure and community investment, take opportunities presented by the area’s economic potential, provide employment and, potentially, a greater range of types of home, including affordable homes, than a scatter of smaller sites. They are more likely to give effect to the spatial strategy. (NPPF 77).

150. However, larger sites tend to have long lead-in times which may be affected by different variables. The lead in time may be extended because of the need to discharge policy requirements and conditions. Delivery of larger sites may be dependent on the delivery and timing of any infrastructure by others, land assembly or on critical negotiations with other parties. The number of sales outlets and the ability of the market to absorb the anticipated number of homes may also be relevant to the delivery rate from the site.

151. In addition, the delivery of large scale developments may need to extend beyond an individual plan period, and the associated infrastructure requirements may not be capable of being identified fully at the outset. (NPPF footnote 38).

152. The ability of any proposed large site allocation to contribute towards the plan’s housing requirement, and the timing of that contribution, will need to be tested at the examination, especially if there is any assumption that the site will contribute to part of the 5 year land supply. Similarly, the links with any related infrastructure project will be considered to ensure that there is a reasonable prospect of the project being funded and the site being brought forward.

### Small and medium sites

<!-- Part3 p.44 -->

153. Small and medium sized sites may have fewer constraints to overcome and be more easily developable in the shorter term, helping to maintain the supply of homes earlier in the plan period until larger sites come on stream. They can make an important contribution to meeting the housing requirement of an area, are essential for Small and Medium Enterprise housebuilders to deliver new homes and are often built out relatively quickly. (NPPF 73).

154. Land to accommodate at least 10% of the housing requirement on sites of no larger than one hectare should be identified by local plans and/or brownfield registers, unless there are strong reasons why this cannot be achieved. The Framework specifically requires land identification, which could be through the plan’s site allocations and/or by identifying particular land parcels on the brownfield register and quantifying how much housing could realistically come forward on them. The plan should be accompanied by evidence to demonstrate that at least 10% of the requirement can be provided on such sites. (NPPF 73).

155. However, whilst small sites have an important role to play, an over-emphasis on them can potentially undermine aspects of the spatial strategy. Undue reliance on the wide dispersal of new housing development on small sites may have an undesirable impact on existing transport and community infrastructure, and it may be more difficult to obtain contributions towards transport and community infrastructure projects.

156. The balance of site sizes will be considered at the examination.

157. Policies should also seek to support small sites for community-led development for housing and self-build and custom-build housing.

### Windfall sites

158. Policies should support the development of windfall sites, giving great weight to the benefits of using suitable sites within existing settlements for homes. (NPPF 73) Sites on the Brownfield Land Register might come forward as windfall sites. Windfall allowances are discussed in relation to housing supply in paragraphs 202 and 232 to 233.

159. The plan should therefore contain general policies which take a very positive approach towards housing development within settlements. These will be examined critically to ensure that they are effective. As with site allocations policies (see paragraphs 319 to 327), there is a risk of impairing the effectiveness of the plan by imposing policy requirements that make it difficult to bring forward sites within settlements including windfall sites.

### Settlement boundaries

160. If a plan contains a policy or policies that apply to specific areas, those areas and their boundaries should usually be shown on the policies map. Plans commonly have different policies for development within settlements, outside settlements or in the countryside. Settlement boundaries provide clarity as to where these different policies apply. They help to direct new development as part of the spatial strategy.

161. However, not all plans have settlement boundaries and there is nothing in national policy or guidance that requires them. They may not be effective in loosely-knit settlements where the edges of development are not well defined. Some plans rely instead on criteria-based policies to assess proposals for development around the edge of settlements or (where there is no settlement boundary) contiguous with the existing built up area.

162. Where settlement boundaries have not been included in the plan, the authority will need to demonstrate that the plan’s policies for development in and around settlement are effective and consistent with the plan’s objectives.

163. Some plans, as well as specifically allocating sites, may draw their settlement boundaries to give room between the built up area and the boundary for new windfall development to take place within the boundary.

164. In addition, some plans allow for windfall development, subject to size and location criteria, on unallocated sites beyond the settlement boundary but contiguous with it. (There are separate policies towards rural exception sites and community-led development: see below).

165. These approaches may be useful to give the plan additional flexibility in housing supply. However, they may be explored at examination to understand why land has not been formally allocated and whether the potential impact of development on such land has been appropriately assessed. Moreover, in the case of development on land outside the settlement boundary, the plan will need to be clear about the policies that will be applied to such sites, because countryside policies generally prevail outside settlement boundaries.

166. The settlement boundaries on the policies map should be drawn to take into account recent permissions, sites under construction and completions within the defined settlement. To leave such sites outside the settlement boundary may undermine the plan’s effectiveness by creating confusion over the policies that apply to the land. A consistent approach should be taken to updating the settlement boundaries with recently permitted development.

<!-- Part3 p.46 -->

167. Sometimes the definition of settlement boundaries in certain locations may be delegated to a subsequent site allocation plan or a neighbourhood plan. However, the scope to delegate to a future site allocation plan has now effectively been closed off by the new Regulations. (See paragraph 172).

168. There is no facility for the Inspector’s report to recommend main modifications to the policies map. The line of the settlement boundary on the policies map is generally a matter for the authority and may not need to come up as a substantive issue at the examination. However, it may need to be considered if it appears to give rise to any illogicality, lack of clarity or policy inconsistency. It may also be necessary to discuss and agree an appropriate change to the settlement boundary at examination to give effect to a policy or a main modification.

169. Overall, the authority will need to demonstrate that it has employed a logical approach to the definition (or otherwise) of settlement boundaries.

### Strategic local plans, site allocation plans and area action plans

170. It has been commonplace under the existing system for strategic-level local plans to contain only the spatial strategy and strategic sites or broad locations for development, leaving the allocation of other housing sites to other plans such as “Part 2” site allocation plans or area action plans.

171. The new plan-making system will not contain these “Part 2” plans. However, this ITM chapter is concerned with plans produced under the previous system and subject to the policies in the December 2024 NPPF. NPPF 29 states that non-strategic plans such as site allocation plans and area action plans may allocate sites. This section is therefore relevant to those Part 1 and Part 2 plans that are at examination or are about to be subject to examination.

172. Government guidance (“Rollout of the new local plan-making system”, updated 6 March 2026) indicates that authorities that are preparing a local plan (which includes a Part 2 plan) under the existing system have until 31 December 2026 to submit the plan for examination, other than in certain circumstances. Against this deadline it is unlikely to be sound for a strategic-level local plan currently at examination to place reliance on a subsequent site allocation plan to meet its housing requirement unless there is convincing evidence that the latter is going be submitted before the deadline. Rather, the strategic-level local plan itself will need to provide full confidence that its housing requirement will be met, including appropriate site allocations.

<!-- Part3 p.47 -->

173. Where a site allocation plan is in examination or has been submitted before the deadline, its role should be clear at the outset. This should be set out in the LDS, in the strategic plan and in the site allocation plan itself. The strategic plan should have set out the amount of housing which the site allocation plan or area action plan policies are expected to provide, the period over which it is to be provided, and whether its purpose includes the provision of a 5 year housing land supply. If there is any doubt over these matters, they will be questioned at the examination.

174. The site allocation plan should comply with the housing requirement of the strategic-level local plan including the amount of housing which the site allocation plan or area action plan is expected to provide. It cannot legally re-visit that housing requirement unless the strategic plan and LDS clearly charge it with that responsibility.

175. Any site allocations plans or area action plans still to be examined in the current system should provide enough sites to ensure that the housing requirement and the delivery trajectory in the strategic plan are met, in combination with other known sources of supply. The allocated sites should be distributed within the plan area in accordance with the strategic plan’s spatial strategy.

176. These issues will be critically examined for soundness.

### Neighbourhood plans

177. Some local plans place reliance the anticipated supply of homes from allocations in future neighbourhood plans to make up part of their housing requirement. If this is the case, the local plan should set out a housing requirement figure for each designated neighbourhood planning area. The neighbourhood requirement figures should reflect the plan’s overall strategy for the pattern and scale of development and any relevant allocations. (NPPF 69) The likelihood of achieving the anticipated level of supply from these sources may need to be critically examined.

178. Many plans do not place reliance on future neighbourhood plans to help meet their overall housing requirement, and in these circumstances the setting of housing requirements for neighbourhood planning areas becomes less critical. Any additional homes delivered from neighbourhood plans in these circumstances will simply be added to the supply figure.

<!-- Part3 p.48 -->

179. Where the neighbourhood plan is already made at the time of the examination, any homes anticipated to be delivered during the plan period on sites allocated by the neighbourhood plan are counted into the supply figure.

### Omission housing sites

180. Omission housing sites are those that have not been included in the plan but have the backing of developers or promoters, who may have purchased or taken an option out on the site. The site may have been identified in the SHLAA or the regulation 18 plan or in a call for sites, but have then been omitted from the submission plan.

181. Often the site promoter will have carried out a great deal of preparatory work in order to promote the site through the local plan process, and will sometimes submit a planning application in parallel to the local plan examination. They may have a strong vested interest in promoting the site, and there may equally be strong local feeling about it.

182. Thus there may be direct or indirect attempts to discuss omission sites at the examination and at the hearings. These may come from the site promoters themselves, but they may also come from concerned parish councils or local people, particularly where there is a parallel or forthcoming planning application.

183. The consideration of the relative merits of omission sites within the examination, and the discussion of omission sites in detail at examination hearings, will be firmly resisted by the Inspector.

184. The Inspector is examining the soundness of the plan itself including its site allocations, but is not examining housing sites that have been omitted from it. If the Inspector were to consider the merits of omission sites, it would be necessary to consider the full range of sites, which is not within the Inspector’s remit. It is the Council’s plan, and it is the Council’s task to produce a spatial strategy and carry out the site selection process.

185. That said, in local plans where LHN is not being met and there is evidence of of a number of potential omission sites and/or credible SHLAA sites, the Inspector may require the Council to investigate the potential for further site allocations, provided the plan is not unduly delayed. Failure to meet local need whilst not allocating deliverable sites is likely to be a matter of soundness. (See paragraph 58).

186. Should it be necessary to identify additional housing sites, because the Inspector has directed the Council to increase either the housing requirement or the housing supply, it is the Council's job to identify appropriate additional sites.
