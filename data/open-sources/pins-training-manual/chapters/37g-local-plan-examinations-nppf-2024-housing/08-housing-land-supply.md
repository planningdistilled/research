# 37g Local Plan Examinations (NPPF 2024): Housing: Housing land supply

<!-- Part3 p.49 -->

## Housing land supply

### General considerations

187. Housing land supply is a future supply of land which is suitable, available and achievable for housing (NPPF 72; PPG “Housing and economic land availability assessment” Paragraph 1).

188. The plan should provide for enough homes of the right types at the right time to provide confidence that the overall housing requirement will be met, and that a 5 year supply of land will be maintained.

189. Strategic policies should provide a clear strategy for bringing sufficient land forward, at a sufficient rate, to address housing need over the plan period.

190. This should include planning for and allocating sufficient sites to deliver the strategic priorities of the area unless those needs can be met more appropriately through other mechanisms, such as brownfield registers or policies in, for example neighbourhood plans. (NPPF 23) Except for those site allocation plans in examination, the 2026 Regulations now provide no scope for this to be effected through Part 2 site allocation plans.

191. The plan’s overall housing provision, and the anticipated rate of housing delivery, should ensure that there is an adequate ongoing housing land supply.

192. Policies should identify a supply of specific deliverable sites for 5 years following the date of adoption and specific developable sites or broad locations for growth for years 6-10 and where possible years 11 to 15. (NPPF 72) “Deliverable” and “Developable” are defined in the NPPF Glossary. The issue of the 5 year housing land supply is dealt with below.

193. The plan should aim to identify sites or land for years 11 to 15 if possible, partly because of the considerable lead-in time between site allocation and development, and partly because it will help to add detail to the spatial strategy and demonstrate its realism and developability. If the authority believes that it is not possible to do so, this is likely to be considered at the examination. There will need to be confidence that the requirement can be met up to the end of year 10 or later.

194. The housing land supply will be tested at examination for any plan whose purpose includes the provision of a supply of housing land to meet the full housing requirement.

195. Full local plans will usually identify both the housing requirement and a supply of land to meet that requirement in full.

<!-- Part3 p.50 -->

196. Site allocation plans will often identify a supply of land to meet the full housing requirement that has been established in an earlier strategic plan.

197. Spatial Development Strategies (SDS) set the housing requirement for their area, but the supply to meet the requirement will come forward through subsequent local plans. In areas covered by an SDS, housing land supply will therefore be tested when each local plan comes forward.

### Overall housing supply

198. Overall housing supply, expressed as a global figure over the plan period, is the total number of new homes that could theoretically be delivered over the plan period from the plan’s site allocations, existing commitments, completions since the start of the plan period, and estimated windfalls.

199. Overall housing supply is not the same as the housing requirement. Overall housing supply is a potential figure rather than a firm figure because of the inevitable uncertainties in the timing and rate of housing delivery. For practical reasons discussed below, the potential figure is often higher than the housing requirement, to provide headroom to ensure that the housing requirement is met. (See paragraphs 206 to 208).

200. The calculation of overall housing supply is normally explicitly set out in strategic level plans. The plan should identify all the components which make up its housing land supply. Typically, these will include:

- Dwellings completed (“completions”) since the start of the housing requirement (which should coincide with the base date for the housing need assessment)

- Dwellings with planning permission, or with a resolution to grant permission subject to a planning obligation (“commitments”)

- Sites proposed for allocation in the plan (“allocations”)

- Broad locations for growth, where appropriate (and only from year 6 onwards); and

- A windfall allowance, where justified.

201. Care should be taken that there is no overlap or double counting between these sources of supply – for example between the windfall allowance and dwellings with planning permission on unallocated sites.

202. In certain circumstances there may be a case for including an average windfall allowance. (See also paragraphs 232-233). The windfall allowance is usually based on the average yield from such sites over recent years. As well as unallocated sites, it can include new homes arising from the exercise of permitted development rights, for example the conversion of offices to residential.

<!-- Part3 p.51 -->

203. Student housing provision can count towards overall net housing supply based on:

- the amount of accommodation that new student housing releases in the wider housing market (by allowing existing properties to return to general residential use); and / or

- the extent to which it allows general market housing to remain in such use, rather than being converted for use as student accommodation.

204. The calculation is based on the average number of students living in student only accommodation, using the published census data. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” paragraph 025)

205. Specialist housing provided for older people including residential institutions in Use Class C2 also counts towards the figure for overall housing provision, based on the amount of accommodation released into the housing market, again using census data. (See paragraphs 267 to 275) (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 026).

### Headroom

206. If overall housing supply is greater than the housing requirement, this is known as headroom. There is no specific NPPF or PPG requirement for headroom. But the plan is expected to meet the area’s identified needs, so where (as in many plans) there is uncertainty over the delivery, or rate of delivery, of certain sites (for example those affected by infrastructure constraints), headroom may be relevant to the consideration of whether the area’s needs will be met – in other words, it can be relevant to the effectiveness of the plan. Where there are delivery uncertainties, adequate headroom may give confidence that enough housing land will come forward to meet the 5 year supply and the housing requirement for the first 10 years of the plan period. Headroom is sometimes referred to as a “buffer”, but that word should preferably not be used in this context because buffer has a specific meaning in connection with the calculation of the 5 year housing land supply. Nor should it be regarded as “over provision”.

<!-- Part3 p.52 -->

207. Even with the best efforts at establishing a realistic housing supply trajectory, there are many variables that affect the start date and delivery rate of housing sites, particularly larger allocated sites, including market conditions, uncertainties over the timing of critical infrastructure, site preparation, remediation and mitigation and the complexities of obtaining planning permission and discharging reserved matters. If a plan contains enough headroom, this will provide resilience against potential slippage and under-delivery and provide greater confidence that a rolling 5 year housing land supply will be maintained.

208. It may be appropriate for the plan to contain headroom even where Green Belt sites are allocated, provided the exceptional circumstances are fully justified. This was tested in Compton Parish Council & Ors v Guildford Borough Council [2019] EWHC 3242 (Admin).

### The delivery trajectory

209. Strategic policies should include a trajectory illustrating the expected rate of delivery from allocated sites and all other sources over the plan period, and all plans should consider whether it is appropriate to set out the anticipated rate of development for specific sites. (NPPF 78).

210. The delivery trajectory will be in the form of a graph or table setting out how many dwellings in total are expected to be delivered in each year of the plan period. PINS is producing a trajectory template for use at examinations and advisory visits.

211. To substantiate the delivery trajectory, it is helpful for the authority to produce a table or spreadsheet setting out how many dwellings each committed and allocated site is expected to deliver in each year of the plan period, and the anticipated contribution from windfall sites. It should be accompanied by a supporting narrative to justify the information it contains, which may include both generic assumptions and site-specific evidence.

212. This will be a useful piece of evidence for the examination, and it can form a focus for discussion in the hearings. It will help focus discussion on the sites’ availability, suitability and likely economic viability, and on whether the authority’s expectations are reasonable.

<!-- Part3 p.53 -->

213. This evidence should be tested in a proportionate manner. For many of the sites listed, it may be possible to rely on the information and evidence provided by the authority. But where this appears to require further investigation, or where the evidence is challenged, it is likely to be necessary to explore the following key points:1

- Whether the expected date for the first completions on the site is realistic, taking account of any relevant available evidence on constraints, infrastructure requirements, land ownership and assembly, the status of any planning applications, developer intentions and other relevant factors.

- Whether the expected rate of development is realistic, taking into account local market evidence and, for large sites, the likely number of developers and outlets (distinctive parts of the development, with different products and potentially separate developers).

- Whether the general assumptions on lead-in times, such as the length of time it might take to address all the policy requirements, obtain planning permission, discharge the conditions and prepare the site for development, are justified.

214. The spreadsheet and trajectory can be adjusted through this process. The final version of the spreadsheet will then form the basis for the delivery trajectory in the adopted plan.

### Maintaining adequate housing land supply over the life of the plan

215. Whether the plan allows for an adequate supply of housing land over the life of the plan will be tested at examination. The particular issues arising in connection with the 5 year housing land supply are discussed in the next section.

216. The housing delivery trajectory, including the spreadsheet and its narrative, may highlight potential risks or inadequacies in the supply of housing at some point in the life of the plan. In addition, representations may call into question the ability of certain sites to deliver homes at the rate anticipated by the authority.

[^1]: Annex 1 to this section of the chapter sets out some more detailed questions that could be asked when testing deliverability.

<!-- Part3 p.54 -->

217. There is inevitably a degree of uncertainty about the start dates and build out rates of allocated sites, and the same applies to sites with planning permission on which development has not yet started.

218. The authority can insure against the risk of falling short against its housing requirement in a number of ways:

- By identifying a good range of sites of different types and sizes for homes designed to meet different markets and needs. This will provide the plan with resilience in the face of market uncertainty (See NPPF 73).

- By creating supply headroom – in which potential housing supply is greater than the housing requirement – so that if some sites fall short in delivery the housing requirement will continue to be met. (See paragraphs 206 to 208).

- By careful scrutiny of the factors and constraints influencing start dates and delivery rates, particularly where particular sites are expected to play a key role in delivery. This requires dialogue with site promoters and developers, and the examination should be provided with the latest information from them. It may be helpful for the site promoter or developer of a key or strategic site to be present at the relevant examination hearings to provide detailed information about these matters.

219. Shortfalls in the first 5 years are addressed in the next section.

220. If the evidence at the examination indicates that the plan overall is unlikely to meet the housing requirement, or that there is a significant risk that it will not do so, main modifications may be required to make the plan sound. This could include bringing forward other site allocations or amending policies to allow for more windfall development. However, such changes are likely to require further consultation, with the risk that the plan will be delayed, and a pragmatic approach needs to be taken. Significant failings in this matter are a matter of soundness unless there are very good and justifiable reasons that justify a shortfall against for housing requirement. Sometimes Inspectors have required the insertion of statements or policies in plans which state that a review will be undertaken should annual monitoring indicate that housing supply has fallen below the annual housing requirement, but the latest Regulations have considerably narrowed the scope for this. (See paragraphs 98 to 100).

<!-- Part3 p.55 -->

221. Although the plan should seek to meet the full housing requirement, there may be uncertainty about the contribution of certain sites towards the end of the plan period, and this may appear on the trajectory as a potential shortfall in supply at that time. This may not be as significant as shortfalls in the early years of the plan, because the plan will very likely have been reviewed by then. NPPF 72 only requires sites for years 11-15 to be identified where possible.

222. If the plan’s purpose is not to meet housing requirement or the 5 year housing land in full, but to meet it in conjunction with a neighbourhood plan, the local plan should make this clear. (See paragraphs 177 to 179).
