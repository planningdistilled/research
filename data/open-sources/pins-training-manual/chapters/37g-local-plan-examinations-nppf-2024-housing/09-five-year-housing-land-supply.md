# 37g Local Plan Examinations (NPPF 2024): Housing: Five-year housing land supply

<!-- Part3 p.55 -->

## Five-year housing land supply

223. One of the requirements of the NPPF is that planning policies should identify a sufficient supply of specific deliverable sites for the five years following the adoption of the plan, with an appropriate buffer. This is referred to as a five-year housing land supply (5YHLS). Deliverable sites are available now, offer a suitable location for development now, and are achievable with a realistic prospect that housing will be delivered on the site within 5 years. (See paragraphs 131 to 134), and the NPPF Glossary. Assessing whether there will be a 5 year supply of housing on the anticipated date of the plan’s adoption (normally taken as the next 1 April following the anticipated adoption date) is an important test of the plan’s soundness.

### The calculation of the five-year housing land supply

224. Detailed guidance on calculating the 5YHLS is provided in the PPG “Housing supply and delivery”.

225. The 5YHLS should be assessed looking forward five years from the intended date of adoption of the plan.

226. The 5YHLS is calculated by comparing the supply of deliverable sites for the first 5 years following adoption against the housing requirement for the same period as identified in the plan’s strategic housing policy, with an appropriate buffer. (NPPF 78; PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 005) Annex 1 of this chapter contains some sample questions that might be used in an examination of site deliverability. Annex 2 contains some worked examples of the 5YHLS calculation in different scenarios.

<!-- Part3 p.56 -->

227. The buffer is in effect housing supply which is moved forward from later in the plan period and is added to the housing requirement for the first 5 years following adoption. That housing requirement figure will have been adjusted to account for under or over supply since the start of the requirement period. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 022) The application of the buffer does not change the plan’s overall housing requirement.

228. The buffer is 5% of the 5 year housing requirement as a minimum and 20% where the delivery of housing over the previous 3 years has fallen below 85% of the requirement, as set out in the latest published Housing Delivery Test results. The purpose of the buffer is to ensure choice and competition in the market and, in the case of 20% buffer, to improve the prospect of achieving the planned supply. (NPPF 78; NPPF Glossary; PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 013.)

229. The Housing Delivery Test is published annually and measures net homes delivered in the local authority area. It applies the day following its publication. Further information on this subject can be found at Paragraphs 027 to 038 of the PPG chapter “Housing supply and delivery”.

230. Where there is a stepped housing requirement, each of the annual requirements for the first 5 years are added together to produce a requirement for the 5 year period against which the housing supply is assessed. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 17).

231. Where the housing requirement is expressed as a range, the 5YHLS is measured against the lower end of the range. (PPG “Housing supply and delivery” Paragraph 18).

### The role of windfalls in the five-year housing land supply

232. Local planning authorities sometimes seek to include an average allowance for windfalls in the 5YHLS calculation for the last 2 years of the 5 year period. Usually only the last 2 years is included to avoid double counting extant permissions.

<!-- Part3 p.57 -->

233. Neither the NPPF nor the PPG say specifically that an annual average allowance for windfall sites should or should not be included in the calculation. There is a question as to whether windfalls are “specific sites” as referred to in the NPPF. However, local planning authority supply calculations normally include windfalls, and this is generally accepted as a pragmatic approach provided the figures are realistic and evidence-based. Some urban authorities might be heavily reliant on windfalls rather than allocated sites. The annual average figure will be examined to see whether it is realistic, having regard to past and anticipated trends and capacity studies.

### Testing the five year housing land supply at examination

234. In all cases where the plan’s purpose includes the provision of a supply of housing land to meet its housing requirement, the plan will be tested to see whether it provides a 5 year housing land supply in accordance with NPPF 72.

235. This is important because if a planning authority cannot demonstrate a 5 year housing land supply in an application or appeal situation, the policies that are most important for determining the application will be considered out of date – even if the plan is newly adopted – and the provisions of NPPF 11 d will apply. (See also the ITM Housing chapter).

236. Any uncertainty about delivery from sites that are being included in the 5 year housing land supply is likely to be critically exposed at examination. In this regard, supply headroom (in which the potential overall housing provision in the plan area for the first 5 years exceeds the plan’s housing requirement) may help in ensuring the plan is effective. (See paragraphs 206 to 208).

237. For the examination, a schedule of deliverable sites should be provided, with a commentary on their expected delivery trajectories and any potential impediments to delivery. The cut-off date for the provision of 5 year housing land supply evidence for the examination should be as close as reasonably possible to the intended adoption date (normally the 1 April immediately after submission). For practical purposes it will usually coincide with the deadline for the submission of hearing statements or any 5 year housing land supply topic paper.

238. The evidence provided should be sufficient to provide confidence that there will be a 5 year housing land supply from the intended date of adoption. A topic paper on the subject is usually very helpful.

### Where there are difficulties in identifying a 5 year housing land supply

239. Assessing the 5 year housing land supply is not an exact science. There will usually be discussion at the examination around the start dates and delivery rates of sites, and about whether some sites have stalled or will not come forward. There is bound to be uncertainty, and Inspectors will recognise this and take a fair and realistic approach.

240. Demonstrating with confidence that a 5 year housing land supply will exist on adoption can be a challenge for local planning authorities. Authorities should be able to demonstrate that they have made every effort to bring forward sites to meet the 5 year supply requirement. It is not desirable to have a situation where a plan’s housing policies are deemed out of date (for example at appeal) almost immediately after adoption. However, there may be instances where a 5 year housing land supply clearly cannot be demonstrated at examination. In such cases there are various options.

241. One option is for the local planning authority to make additional site allocations during the course of the examination. There are two problems with this. The first is that it might not be possible to bring forward sites that are capable of such quick delivery that they can remedy a deficit in the 5 year supply. The second is that site identification and consultation can delay the examination. This option might be acceptable if the work can be carried out very quickly (for example if there are already brownfield candidates or eligible sites assessed through the SHLAA), in which case the changes can be made through main modifications.

242. A second option is to make policy changes such as adjustments to settlement boundaries, the creation of a permissive policy that allows development adjacent to settlements, or the relaxation of windfall policies. These might assist in raising windfalls, although it would be difficult to put a figure on the contribution such policy changes would make. They might be effective where there is marginal uncertainty about the ability to maintain a 5 year supply.

243. A third option, where justified, might be to step the housing requirement or spread any under-delivery over the whole plan period, thus taking some of the delivery burden away from the first 5 year period. This might be relevant where the authority is trying to compensate for under-delivery in the first year(s) from the start of the housing requirement, or where delivery from its site allocations is expected to come forward later in the plan period. (See paragraphs 86 to 92) However, this approach will have a knock-on effect on the supply requirement in later years and should not simply be used as a means of neutralising the 20% buffer or delaying important decisions on site allocations.

<!-- Part3 p.59 -->

244. Where the process of finding additional sites for early delivery is likely to create undue delay in the examination, it might still be possible to find the plan sound overall without a 5 year housing land supply, provided the plan’s overall supply still meets its overall housing requirement and is sound in other respects.

245. On the other hand, a serious 5 year housing supply deficit without remedy, in a plan with other significant soundness problems, might point towards an overall soundness failure.

### Five year supply in site allocation plans

246. “Part 2” site allocation plans may identify a proportion, rather than all, of the total land supply needed to ensure that the adopted “part 1” plan’s housing requirement can be met. In such cases, it may be necessary to consider whether the distribution and capacity of sites and the pace of providing them in the site allocations plan is consistent with the adopted “part 1” plan which might include the provision of a five year housing land supply. If it is not, or if some of the proposed sites are not sound, then it may be necessary to seek modifications to incorporate further sites in order to ensure consistency with the adopted plan. In this limited sense, the site allocations plan may play a role in helping to maintain a rolling five year supply of housing land but this should not be confused with demonstrating that the Council has a five year supply at any particular point in time.
