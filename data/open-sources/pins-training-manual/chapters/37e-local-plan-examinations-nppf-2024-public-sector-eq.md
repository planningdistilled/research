## Introduction

1. This section sets out how Human Rights (HR) and the Public Sector Equality Duty (PSED) relate to Inspectors’ work examining plans. HR and PSED, often overlapping considerations, typically come into sharper focus in casework (to which relevant case law principally relates). Further detail is therefore contained in the ITM casework chapter on Human Rights and Equalities, to which this ITM section is designed to sit alongside. If there is any divergence between that chapter and this section, for example addressing the implications of recent case law, the former prevails.

## Statutory context

2. HR or ‘Convention rights’ are those set out in the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) as incorporated into domestic statute via the Human Rights Act 1998 (HRA98). Section 6(1) of the HRA98 sets out that it is unlawful for a public authority to act in a way which is incompatible with a Convention right. Inspectors examining local plans are public authorities in that context (HRA98, section 6(3)). Article 1 of the First Protocol (A1FP) to the ECHR ‘protection of property’, and ECHR Article 8 the ‘right for respect for private and family life’ are often particularly relevant in planning (as in Stevens v SSCLG [2013] EWHC 792 (Admin)).

3. An Inspector examining a plan is also a public authority carrying out a ‘public function’ pursuant to section 150(5) of the Equality Act 2010 (EA). Section 149 of the EA describes the PSED. The PSED requires that ‘due regard’ is had to what are known as the ‘three aims’ of EA section 149:

‘(1) A public authority must, in the exercise of its functions, have due regard to the need to-

(a) eliminate discrimination, harassment, victimisation and any other conduct that is prohibited by or under this Act;

(b) advance equality of opportunity between persons who share a relevant protected characteristic and persons who do not share it;

(c) foster good relations between persons who share a relevant protected characteristic and persons who do not share it.’

4. Relevant protected characteristics are listed at EA section 149(7), and are age, disability, gender reassignment, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex and sexual orientation.

## Policy and guidance

5. Whilst part of the social dimension of planning, neither the HRA98, EA, nor equalities are expressly mentioned in the NPPF. The PPG refers in broad terms to the housing needs of older and disabled people as well as the health and safety of communities. Examining Inspectors will need to exercise duties under the HRA98 and EA in line with established legal principles set out in this chapter.

## The role of an examining Inspector in relation to HR and the PSED

6. The role of the Inspector is to consider whether the plan is sound as defined in

legislation (s20 of the PCPA 2004) and national policy. In this case, the ‘public function’ is the examination of the plan. It therefore follows that the PSED requires you to have ‘due regard’ when assessing whether or not the plan is sound and when considering any main modifications to make it so.

7. The requirement to have ‘due regard’ does not require the Inspector (or specifically

empower the Inspector) to depart from s20 of the PCPA 2004, from national planning policy or the planning practice guidance which explains how the national planning policy should be implemented.

## How HR and PSED considerations apply at examination compared to casework

8. Policies and allocations in plans have the potential to result in HR and PSED implications. That is likely to be particularly relevant to policies addressing the needs of certain individuals, such as older people or travellers, or where community facilities important to those who share a particular protected characteristic are proposed to be redeveloped.

9. Policies and allocations in a plan may also constrain the ability to account for HR and PSED considerations in decision-taking, for example where redevelopment is contingent on the demolition of existing homes or where there would be agent of change implications for neighbouring uses. Where HR or PSED implications are relevant, the duties should be engaged with substantively as opposed to in tick-box fashion (Stuart Bracking & Ors v Secretary of State for Work and Pensions [2013] EWCA Civ 1345, ‘Bracking’).

10. The Bracking judgement expanded upon the ‘Brown principles’ in respect of the PSED as in R. (Brown) v Secretary of State for Work and Pensions [2008]

<!-- Part3 p.11 -->

EWHC 3158 (Admin). In short the Brown principles, which Inspectors should adhere to in examining plans are:

a. general regard to equality issues is not the same as specific regard by way of conscious approach to the statutory criteria,

b. officials reporting to Ministers or other decision-takers on matters material to the discharge of the duty must be rigorous in enquiring and reporting,

c. it is for the Courts to decide if due regard has been had, but providing this is done, it is for the decision-maker to decide what weight to give to the equality implications of the decision,

d. the duty of due regard requires public authorities to be properly informed before taking a decision. If relevant material is not available, there is a duty to acquire it and further consideration with appropriate groups may be required, and

e. the duty concerns the impact of the proposal on all persons with the protected characteristic and any class of persons within a protected characteristic who might most obviously be adversely affected [such as older people with the protected characteristic of age].

11. Practically applying those principles to examinations, Inspectors should ask specifies questions as to the HR and PSED implications of a plan with a focus on those areas where there are clearly likely to be implications. That may be by way of initial question, as part of matters, issues and questions and via questions during hearing sessions. Further information should be sought where the information before you is insufficient (LDRA Ltd. v Secretary of State [2016] EWHC 950 Admin).

12. There have been no recent challenges to plans on the basis of failing to discharge HR or PSED duties. Given the statutory basis for decision taking, there will in most instance be further opportunity for more detailed consideration of HR and PSED implications in decision-taking. Therefore whilst examining Inspectors should robustly assess implications, that should be on the basis of proportionate evidence (in line with the ‘justified’ test of soundness).

## Sources of HR and PSED evidence at examination

13. PCPA section 20(5)(a) sets out that it is one of the purposes of examination to determine whether a plan satisfied the requirements of PCPA section 19. In turn PCPA section 19(3) sets out that in preparing local development documents, of

<!-- Part3 p.12 -->

which development plan documents are one form,1 there must be compliance with the local planning authority’s statement of community involvement (SCI).2 The SCI is likely to contain information as to who will be engaged in plan-making and how. The SCI may therefore be an indicator as to how the local planning authority has engaged with those whose rights may be affected, and those who have different protected characteristics.

14. The Town and Country Planning (Local Planning) (England) Regulations 2012 as amended sit under various sections of primary legislation. The Regulations define ‘general consultation bodies’ at regulation 2(1). Those include voluntary bodies and bodies which represent the interests of racial, ethnic, national and religious groups and disabled persons. Regulation 18(2)(b) and 19(b) set out how a local planning authority should notify and invite each of the general consultation bodies as they ‘consider appropriate’ to make representations at those draft and publication plan-making stages. Engagement and views expressed in that context, along with how they have influenced the approach in the plan, may be relevant evidence in terms of how the Inspector has due regard to HR and PSED matters.

15. PCPA section 19(5) sets out that the local planning authority must carry out an appraisal of the sustainability of the proposals in each development plan document, to which the Environmental Assessment of Plans and Programmes Regulations 2004 as amended relate (with more information available in the relevant ITM examination chapter). Echoing proportionality in NPPF paragraph 36.b) the PPG sets out how sustainability appraisal should ‘focus on the environmental, economic and social impacts that are likely to be significant’.3 The ‘social’ impacts in that respect may contain information regarding HR and PSED implications.

16. Plans are often accompanied by an equalities impact assessment (EQIA). They are recommended by sectoral guidance, notably that produced by the Planning Advisory Service. There is, however, no requirement for an EQIA to be produced. As with any planning matter, it is not necessarily the case that representors or you will agree with the findings of any EQIA or evidence in respect of HR/ PSED implications.

17. In circumstances where Inspectors do not consider there is adequate and proportionate evidence, Inspectors should ask questions through their matters, issues and questions. That may particularly be the case where there are representations alluding to some interference with HR or to differential effects in

18. Inspectors can expect to deal with cases and local plans involving diverse people with myriad circumstances and needs. You can expect to see evidence concerning traditions, cultures and identifies that they are not personally familiar with. Nevertheless be wary of other arguments made which may appear to be relate to HR or equalities but which are not specifically governed by legislation.

19. An example might be income inequality. Although poverty is often an indicator of certain protected characteristics, it is not a protected characteristic in itself. It is always better to speak to you manager if you encounter ambiguous arguments, circumstances with which you are not familiar, or if you are considering seeking further information. The casework ITM chapter on HR and the PSED contains further information on that matter, along with how to approach wording reasoning so as to strike an appropriate balance between specificity and sensitivity without using incorrect phrasing or referencing personal or sensitive information.
