# 42 Necessary Wayleaves: Relevant Considerations (Wayleaves)

<!-- Part3 p.529 -->

## Relevant Considerations (Wayleaves)

65. The role of the inspector is to consider evidence regarding whether it is necessary or expedient for the electric line to cross the land in question, and the effects of the line on the use and enjoyment of the land.

66. Sometimes landholders object to the application on the grounds that the compensation offered by the licence holder is inadequate. Issues which relate to the impact on the use or enjoyment of the land and may subsequently be the subject of a claim for compensation can be raised in evidence. However, the Secretary of State does not have power under Schedule 4 to the 1989 Act to prescribe financial conditions in a necessary wayleave case or to resolve disputes regarding the level of compensation. Compensation will fall to be settled by agreement between the parties or, failing agreement, by the Upper Tribunal (Lands Chamber) at the request of either party.

67. Whilst it may be appropriate to note those issues that do not fall within the relevant tests in the report, they should not influence the recommendation to the Secretary of State.

<!-- Part3 p.530 -->

### Necessary and Expediency Tests

68. It only needs to be established that it is necessary or expedient for the electric line to be installed or remain over the land in question. The ‘necessary test’ is more exacting than the ‘expediency test’ and relates to cases where there is absolutely no alternative to the route included in the application.

69. The Inspector’s conclusions on Necessity and Expediency should be set out under separate sub-headings, with distinct conclusions on each.

70. The basic requirement of these tests is to determine why a proposed or an existing electric line is needed. This will invariably relate to the licence holder’s licence conditions to transmit or distribute electricity in accordance with either NETS SQSS or EREC P2/7.

71. Once the need for the electric line has been established consideration should be given to whether the land in question can be reasonably avoided. Landholders may seek to argue that the application should be refused on the ground that there is a suitable route elsewhere or an alternative option is available, such as placing the line underground.

72. Where the licence holder has identified alternatives, DESNZ’s position is that the existence of such alternatives means the line cannot be regarded as necessary, irrespective of the cost or disruption associated with implementing them.

73. In the case of a proposed electric line, the licence holder would normally carry out a routing study which takes into account the uses of the land along the proposed and alternative routes. The licence holder would have been involved in negotiations with the owners and occupiers of the various landholdings affected by the proposed electric line, and some may have entered into voluntary wayleave agreements. If the land in question cannot be avoided without reopening negotiations with the grantors’ then this is material to the issue of expediency. Other factors that would normally be considered material to the issue of expediency include timescales, costs and the licence holders’ statutory duties under section 9 of the 1989 Act to develop and maintain an efficient, co-ordinated and economical system of electricity distribution/transmission.

74. Inspectors should not propose their own alternative alignments or routes.

75. In the case of an existing electric line, the licence holder would normally submit options for complying with a Notice to Remove. These options could impact on other land (such as overhead line diversion or cable sealing-end towers), involve new rights from other landholders, disruption to the local area and expense. Such factors are material to the issue of expediency.

76. There is no prescribed cost threshold for assessing the reasonableness of alternatives. Inspectors must exercise judgement, noting that DNOs often state that any alternative costs will ultimately be borne by the public.

### Use and Enjoyment Tests

77. Account must be taken of the effect of the electric line and apparatus on the use and enjoyment of the land. The focus is on private land interests and the evidence must be site specific.

78. Reasons for landholders objecting to an application may include matters such as:

- The impact of the construction requirements on the land in question (new electricity lines).

- Site access requirements during construction and for future maintenance of the electric line.

- The effect of the siting of the line supports on the land (overhead lines).

- Drainage requirements and effects on existing land drainage.

- The effects of the line on views from principal buildings (overhead lines).

79. While licence holders often note that a property was acquired with existing equipment in situ, and that this may have been reflected in the purchase price, Inspectors should recognise that perceptions and circumstances can change over time. The lived experience at the property may reveal impacts not appreciated at purchase, and changes such as having children, grandchildren or animals may influence the landowner’s concerns.
