# 24 Green Belts: Step 5: Do very special circumstances exist?

<!-- Part2 p.92 -->

## Step 5: Do very special circumstances exist?

47. If the other considerations do not clearly outweigh the totality of the harm, very special circumstances cannot exist (GB6 of NPPF) and the appeal should be dismissed.

48. If the weight of the other considerations clearly outweighs the totality of the harm, it is likely that very special circumstances exist. This would be likely to lead to the appeal being allowed.

49. Before reaching this conclusion, do a common sense check. Do the factors in support of the proposal clearly outweigh the harm? It is not sufficient for them to merely outweigh. In other words, for the appeal to succeed, the overall balance would have to favour the appellants’ case, not just marginally, but decisively. The NPPF states that substantial weight should be given to any harm to the Green Belt and the Government attaches great importance to Green Belts. Does the reasoning clearly and logically lead to a conclusion?

50. Other considerations do not have to be rare or uncommon to be special. However, rarity may be a relevant consideration. In Wychavon v SSCLG & Butler [2008] EWCA Civ 692 the Court of Appeal found that the High Court judge was wrong:

“…. to treat the words "very special" in the paragraph 3.2 of the guidance as simply the converse of "commonplace". Rarity may of course contribute to the "special" quality of a particular factor, but it is not essential, as a matter of ordinary language or policy. The word "special" in the guidance connotes not a quantitative test, but a qualitative judgment as to the weight to be given to the particular factor for planning purposes. Thus, for example, respect for the home is in one sense a "commonplace", in that it reflects an aspiration shared by most of humanity. But it is at the same time sufficiently

51. This is consistent with the comments of in Basildon v FSS & Temple [2004] EWHC 2759 (Admin)7 and in Basildon DC v SSETR & Others [2000]8. The circumstances do not have to be unique, and the possibility that similar circumstances might arise elsewhere does not prevent a finding of very special circumstances in any particular case.

52. In Sefton Metropolitan Borough Council v SSHCLG & Jerry Doherty [2021] EWHC 1082 (Admin) it was alleged that the Inspector should have applied substantial weight to each of the Green Belt harms identified but it was found that there was no error in not attaching separate substantial weight to each element of harm (paragraph 61). This might include harm by reason of inappropriate development or the effect on Green Belt openness or purposes. There is therefore no need to attach substantial weight to each of those harms individually. The judge also said (paragraph 34) that:

“When paragraphs 143 and 144 are read together they can be seen as explaining that very special circumstances are needed before inappropriate development in the Green Belt can be permitted. In setting out that explanation they emphasise the seriousness of harm to the Green Belt in order to ensure that the decision maker understands and has in mind the nature of the very special circumstances requirement. They require the decision maker to have real regard to the importance of the Green Belt and the seriousness of any harm to it. They do not, however, require a particular mathematical exercise nor do they require substantial weight to be allocated to each element of harm as a mathematical exercise with each tranche of substantial weight then to be added to a balance. The exercise of planning judgement is not to be an artificially sequenced two stage process but a single exercise of judgement to assess whether there are very special circumstances which justify the grant of permission notwithstanding the particular importance of the Green Belt.”

53. In undertaking the Green Belt balancing exercise, it is important to show that all the harms and benefits have been considered. The weight or importance attached to them should be consistent with that identified within the main body of the decision. Where the amount of harm is significant and the other considerations in favour of the proposal are also significant, it will make the decision more convincing and credible if this is acknowledged. It will assist the losing party to understand which factors in particular caused the outcome to go against them. However, it is not necessary to undertake a hypothetical balancing exercise assuming that the considerations at play were different in some way. This should generally be avoided.

54. A possible outcome of the balancing exercise is that you find that there are very special circumstances. It is a conclusion you reach after the balancing exercise and so should only feature towards the end of your reasoning.

55. Do not return to any other matters or other considerations after this conclusion. It is possible to have separate sections of your decision dealing with other considerations and other matters. The former would include considerations advanced in support of the proposal. The latter would typically include any alleged harm which you have not

56. To ensure the correct approach and use of terminology do not:

- state that it is the very special circumstances that outweigh/don’t outweigh the harm (it is the other considerations)

- use the phrase exceptional circumstances when referring to development proposals. The NPPF uses this term in relation to the establishment of new Green Belts and alterations to the boundaries of existing ones.

57. Make your final conclusion clear – for example:

Very special circumstances do not exist - The other considerations in this case do not clearly outweigh the harm identified. Consequently, the very special circumstances necessary to justify the development do not exist.

Very special circumstances do exist - The other considerations in this case clearly outweigh the harm identified. Consequently, very special circumstances exist which justify the development.
