# 24 Green Belts: Step 1a: Is the development inappropriate?

## Step 1a: Is the development inappropriate?

10. You will firstly need to decide what type of development you are dealing with and assess it against relevant development plan policies, and the NPPF (GB6, GB7 and

<!-- Part2 p.86 -->

GB8). Are the development plan policies consistent with the NPPF? (see above) If the inconsistency is significant, the critical judgement is likely to be whether the proposal falls into one of the exceptions to inappropriate development in the Green Belt set out in GB7 of the NPPF.

11. Further advice about particular development types is provided later in this chapter.

12. If the proposed development would fall within any of those exceptions and is therefore not inappropriate development, there is no need to consider it against any of the other exceptions – even if they might be applicable and have been raised by the parties. However, where there are arguments about which exception should apply and you are finding that the proposal would be inappropriate development it is likely to be necessary to consider all of those cited or which are clearly relevant.

13. In some cases, the proposal may contain different elements which do not precisely fall into any of the exception categories. For example, the re-use of an existing building and the redevelopment of previously developed land. In those instances, it is advisable to consider the proposal against all relevant exceptions that apply rather than to find that it would be inappropriate development when judged against a single category. This will particularly be the case if the various elements of the proposal could, in theory, be proposed separately. Therefore, in the example above, you would assess the proposal against both GB7(1)(b) and GB7(1)(e).

14. If you consider that the development is not inappropriate:

- Deal with the proposal as you would for any other s78 or s174 ground (a) appeal.

- Do not carry out steps 3 (other considerations), 4 (the Green Belt balancing exercise) or 5 (‘very special circumstances’). See below for advice on dealing with ‘openness’ (step 1b).

- Address any other alleged non-Green Belt harm (for example, to character and appearance) in the usual way. A finding that a development is not inappropriate does not automatically mean that it is acceptable in terms of other planning issues (step 2).

### Inappropriateness by reason of effects on openness

15. The NPPF (chapter 13) states that the objective of Green Belt policy is to “prevent urban sprawl by keeping land permanently open. The government attaches great importance to Green Belts, the essential features of which are their openness and permanence.”

16. Openness may be a consideration in identifying exceptions to inappropriate development. Certain exceptions within GB7 of the NPPF require the decision maker to first assess the effect of the development on openness. It may also be a matter that requires consideration for proposals that are found to be inappropriate development but do not require this initial assessment.

### What is openness?

17. The PPG on Green Belt sets out factors that can be taken into account when considering the potential impact of development on the openness of the Green Belt (Reference ID: 64-013-20250225). These include but are not limited to visual and spatial aspects, duration and remediability and degree of activity. Any such assessment requires a judgment based on the circumstances of the case and the courts have identified a number of such matters.

Spatial and visual aspects

18. The Court of Appeal in Turner v SSCLG & East Dorset Council [2016] EWCA Civ 466 confirmed that the openness of the Green Belt has a spatial aspect as well as a visual aspect. This means that the absence of visual intrusion does not in itself mean that there is no impact on the openness of the Green Belt as a result. But equally this does not mean that the openness of the Green Belt has no visual dimension (paragraph 25).

19. The Supreme Court in R (on the application of Samuel Smith Old Brewery (Tadcaster) and others) (Respondents) v North Yorkshire County Council (Appellant) [2020] UKSC 3 endorsed paragraph 14 of Turner to the effect that the word openness is open textured and a number of factors are capable of being relevant when it comes to applying it to the particular facts of a specific case. However, how to take account of the visual effects is a matter of planning judgement rather than one of legal principle (paragraph 26). In this case it was concluded that there was no error of law in the officer report as there is no express or implied requirement to refer to visual impact. The Supreme Court also highlighted that openness is the counterpart of urban sprawl and that it does not imply freedom from any [emphasis added] form of development. Furthermore, the visual qualities of the land may be an aspect of the planning judgement in applying this broad policy concept (paragraph 22).

20. In conclusion the Supreme Court confirmed that “the matters relevant to openness in any particular case are a matter of planning judgement, not law” (paragraph 39). So whilst visual impact can be relevant to openness it is not necessarily relevant in every case. Nevertheless, Inspectors are best advised to have regard to potential visual impacts rather than simply to ignore or not refer to them at all.

21. The Turner judgment also clarified that “The visual dimension of the openness of the Green Belt does not exhaust all relevant planning factors relating to visual impact when a proposal for development in the Green Belt comes up for consideration” (paragraph 16). This means that it is possible that a development which would harm openness could be acceptable visually and vice versa. Therefore, it is advisable that you clearly separate out your assessment of any effects on openness from any assessment of effects on character and appearance.

Duration and activity

22. The effect on openness might not be confined solely to permanent physical works. For example, cars in a car park, boats in a marina and play equipment in a garden might all have some effect on openness. The extent of the effect on openness may vary depending on the extent of any car parking or mooring of boats and the frequency.

### The effect on openness and purposes

23. Paragraphs GB7(1)(e) and GB7(1)(f), contain distinct tests relating to openness, in determining whether the proposal should be categorised as inappropriate development. Paragraph GB7(1)(e) refers to not causing substantial harm to openness. Paragraph GB7(1)(f) requires the impact on the openness of the Green Belt to be minimised and there to be no significant conflict with the Green Belt purposes. Paragraph GB7(1)(g)(i) refers to development undermining the purposes (taken together) of the remaining Green Belt land across the area of the plan, where the development would utilise grey belt land. These tests need to be applied to determine whether a proposal should be categorised as inappropriate development. In so doing, regard should be had to the aspects of openness outlined above. Further guidance on assessing development under GB7(1)(g) – Grey Belt – is set out below.

24. Whilst in reference to the tests in the previous NPPF, the concepts of the following are helpful in assessing the effect on openness. In Samuel Smith Old Brewery (Tadcaster) & Oxton Farm v North Yorkshire CC & Darrington Quarries Ltd [2018] EWCA Civ 489 it was acknowledged that some forms of development, including mineral extraction, may in principle be appropriate, and compatible with the concept of openness. Similarly in Euro Garages Ltd v SSCLG & Anor [2018] EWHC 1753 (Admin) the judge indicated at paragraph 42 that rather than treating any change as having a greater impact on openness of the Green Belt, the correct approach is to consider the impact or harm, if any, wrought by the change. Whether or not any change will have an adverse impact, and so cause harm to openness, might depend on factors such as the scale of the development, its locational context, and its spatial and/or visual implications (paragraph 32).
