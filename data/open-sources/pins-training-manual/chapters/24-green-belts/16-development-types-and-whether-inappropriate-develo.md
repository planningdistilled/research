# 24 Green Belts: Development types and whether inappropriate development

<!-- Part2 p.95 -->

## Development types and whether inappropriate development

64. The NPPF states development in the Green Belt is inappropriate unless one of several exceptions applies (Policy GB7). These are considered in more detail below. All other development not covered by any of the exceptions is therefore inappropriate. This encompasses walls, fences, wind turbines, floodlights, attached structures and outbuildings.

Development for agriculture, horticulture and forestry, or which is solely for nature conservation, restoration and/or enhancement - GB7(1)(a)

65. The NPPF does not set out any limiting criteria relating to size or any other matters. Consequently, if the proposed development is for agriculture, horticulture and forestry, or which is solely for nature conservation, restoration and/or enhancement, it would not be inappropriate development.

66. If raised by the parties, you will need to consider whether the proposed development would be for agriculture, horticulture and forestry, or which is solely for nature conservation, restoration and/or enhancement. However, a proposal should generally be determined as applied for, unless the evidence firmly indicates that it would not be for agriculture, horticulture and forestry, or which is solely for nature conservation, restoration and/or enhancement.10

67. The requirement in The Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (England) Order 2015 (Part 6, Classes A and B - ‘Agricultural and Forestry’) that buildings and other works must be “reasonably necessary for the purposes of agriculture within that unit” relates solely to the consideration of whether a proposal would be permitted development. It should not be applied when considering the merits of a planning application seeking permission for an agricultural building in the Green Belt.

68. Separate advice on ‘dwellings for rural workers’ (agricultural workers dwellings) is provided below.

### Reuse, extension, alteration or replacement of buildings – GB7(1)(b)

69. Proposals relating to the reuse of a building may include any associated uses of land of minor operations such as external storage, garden areas, hardstanding or car parking boundary walling or fencing. However, if a proposal to re-use a building includes any extensions or alterations, these would also stand to be considered under GB7(1)(b)

70. In Smith v SSCLG [2017] EWHC 2562 (Admin) the judgment confirmed that there was no legal error by the Inspector in concluding that proposed fencing, bin storage, car parking space and domestic paraphernalia would fail to preserve openness.

71. Also in Baynham v SSCLG & East Herts DC [2017] EWHC 3049 (Admin) the Judge endorsed the Inspector’s approach to the consideration of urban sprawl and openness in relation to the re-use of a building for residential purposes (paragraph 26). The Judge also found that there is no need to identify a particular large built-up area in deciding whether there would be urban sprawl.

72. The extension or alteration of a building is not inappropriate development provided that it does not result in a disproportionate increase in size compared to the original building. In settling this it will be important to firstly establish the size of the original building before deciding whether the proposal would, when taken in combination with any previous additions to the original building, result in a disproportionate increase in terms of its size. In other words, when taken together, would the sum total of existing and proposed extensions to the original building be disproportionate in size? It may therefore follow that a small extension could represent a cumulative disproportionate addition if the building has previously been extended.

73. There are different ways of assessing and measuring proportionality. Development plans and SPDs may contain specific limits in terms of floorspace and/or volume. However, the NPPF refers to size. Consequently, you should primarily look at the overall increase in terms of volume, floorspace, footprint and external dimensions. This exercise should not consider any effect on openness. However, in a marginal case,

visual impact may also be relevant rather than simply making a numerical comparison. For example, a sprawling extension may give rise to a finding that it is disproportionate compared to one of the same size that is less bulky.

74. Some development plans define the circumstances in which an outbuilding is to be treated as an extension to a building. However, if this is not the case then the provisions of the NPPF should be applied. In Warwick DC v SSLUHC, Mr J Storer & Mrs A Lowe [2022] EWHC 2145 (Admin) the judge concluded that paragraph 154 (c) (NPPF 2024)

“…. is not to be interpreted as being confined to physically attached structures but that an extension for the purposes of that provision can include structures which are physically detached from the building of which they are an extension.” (paragraph 52)

In other words, it would be unlawful to find that an outbuilding should not be treated as an “extension” for the purposes of GB7(1)(b) solely on the basis that it was not linked to another building. Whether a detached structure would amount to an extension of the existing building is a matter of fact and degree.

- For residential outbuildings, it would be reasonable to take into account whether the proposal was a “normal domestic adjunct” (an expression used in the earlier judgment of Sevenoaks DC v SSE & Dawe [1997]). In addition, consideration could be given to the purpose and use of the proposed building, its relationship with the original building and its size.

- If a curtilage building is held not to be an “extension” then it would not fall within GB7(1)(b) and so would be inappropriate development. If it does amount to an “extension” then an assessment should be made as to whether it would result in a disproportionate increase in size compared to the original building.

75. NPPF Footnote 40 defines the term ‘original building’ as:

‘The building as it existed on 1 July 1948 or, if constructed after 1 July 1948, as it was built originally’.

76. Therefore, extensions which were added to a building before 1 July 1948 should be regarded as part of the ‘original building’. Where an extension to a building constructed after 1 July 1948 is proposed, the comparison will be between the building as first built and the building as proposed to be extended, together with any existing extensions constructed since the building was first built.

77. The proposal may be to extend a building which replaced a previous building (most commonly a replacement dwelling). In relation to buildings constructed after 1 July 1948, the definition of ‘original building’ in footnote 40 of the NPPF does not expressly deal with replacements. For the purposes of the NPPF it would be reasonable to conclude that the ‘original building’ in such a case would be the replacement dwelling itself, as originally built, and that should form the baseline against which subsequent extensions and alterations should be measured. However, the development plan may contain more detailed policies relating to replacement dwellings in the Green Belt, which can be given weight depending on their degree of consistency with the NPPF.

- In Guildford BC v SSLUHC & Mr C Weeks [2023] EWHC 575 (Admin) the Court held that the definition of “original building” in the Local Plan policy

referred to the original building as it existed on 1 July 1948 or the first building as originally built after that date. It was therefore this which should have been used as the starting point and not the replacement building that existed at the time of application. The decision was quashed as the Inspector misinterpreted the policy and took the replacement dwelling on the site as the baseline for assessing whether the proposal would be disproportionate. The case was therefore decided on the basis of the particular local circumstances rather than setting out a universal approach to this matter.

78. Many buildings will have permitted development rights11 which will allow some extensions to be added without requiring planning permission. However, the assessment should be made on the basis of what is now proposed. In other words against the ‘original building’, not the ‘original building’ plus extensions potentially allowed under permitted development rights. If the existence of permitted development rights is argued in favour of a development, you should deal with this as an ‘other consideration’.

79. The question of how to define the relevant ‘building’ may arise. For example, this might occur when dealing with a terraced or semi-detached dwelling. The definition in s336 states that a building includes “any part of a building.” However, no judicial authority exists to the effect that there is a requirement to interpret this word as meaning, for example, that the entire terrace of which a dwelling forms part should be considered to be the original building for this purpose. Therefore, in the context of GB7(1)(b), the word “building” should be construed as relating to the individual building to be extended, as shown within the appeal site.

80. With regard to a replacement building, in order to be ‘not inappropriate’, it must be in the same use as the building it will replace and not materially larger.

81. In considering whether a replacement building would be materially larger “The exercise was ‘primarily an objective one by reference to size’. Which physical dimension is most relevant for the purpose of assessing the relative size of the existing and replacement dwellinghouse, will depend on the circumstances of the particular case. It may be floor space, footprint, built volume, height, width, etc.”12 It should not consider any impact on openness. However, according to the Heath and Hampstead judgment the assessment is not solely a mathematical comparison, but neither is it a decision about whether the replacement would be more visually intrusive. Therefore, if increase in dimensions is marginal, then some regard may be had to matters such as bulk, height, mass and prominence.

82. The decision maker’s role is to assess whether the proposed replacement building would be materially larger than the existing building to be replaced as per Athlone House Ltd v SSCLG [2015] EWHC 3524 (Admin)13. The extent of physical built development is essentially a question of fact but judgement is required at the next stage when that baseline is compared with the proposal and the extent of any change gauged against the tests which are set out in the exceptions. Furthermore, the judge concluded that an unimplemented planning permission (which may include permitted development rights) could not, as a material fallback, count as part of the baseline.

### Limited infilling in villages lying within the Green Belt - GB7(1)(c)

85. In line with the NPPF the matters to address are whether the proposal would be in a village; whether it would be infilling; and, if so would that infilling be limited.

86. The term “village” in GB7(1)(c) should not be confused with the definition of “settlement” in the Glossary at Annex B to the NPPF. That definition excludes villages which lie within and are defined as part of the Green Belt in the development plan. Such locations therefore fall to be considered under Policy S5 rather than Policy S4. This does not prevent consideration of whether a proposal constitutes limited infilling in a village under GB7(1)(c).

87. In the CoA case in Julian Wood v SSCLG and Gravesham Borough Council [2015] it was common ground between the parties that the boundary of a village defined in a local plan would not necessarily be determinative for this purpose although it would be a relevant consideration. Therefore, when considering whether a settlement is a village or whether a site is in a village, Inspectors should have regard to the situation “on the ground” as well as any relevant policies. Such a judgement is likely to depend on factors such as the number of buildings or properties that are grouped together, their inter-relationship and spacing, the facilities and services available and the juxtaposition of the site with surrounding buildings and any open land beyond.

88. The terms ‘limited’ and ‘infilling’ are not defined in the NPPF and these will be essentially a question of fact and planning judgement for the planning decision-maker having regard, for example, to the nature and size of the development itself, the location of the application site and its relationship to other, existing development adjoining and adjacent to it (see paragraph 37 of R (Tate) v Northumberland County Council [2018] EWCA Civ 1519).

### Limited affordable housing for local community needs - GB7(1)(d)

89. Consideration will need to be given to you whether the proposed affordable housing is limited and whether it would meet local community needs, as set out in the NPPF or development plan (including policies for rural exception sites).

### Redevelopment of previously developed land - GB7(1)(e)

90. Relevant considerations are:

- Is the site previously developed land? (see the definition in Annex B to the NPPF)

- If so, would the proposal redevelop the site, or include a material change of use to residential or mixed-use including residential?

- Would it cause substantial harm to the openness of the Green Belt.

91. The definition of previously developed land in Annex B has changed slightly compared to the 2024 version. In addition to minerals extraction and waste disposal by landfill, the exclusion relating to land subject to restoration now also encompasses renewable and low carbon energy development where provision for restoration exists. It still refers to “land comprising large areas of fixed surface infrastructure such as large areas of hardstanding”. There is also a reference here and at the start of the definition to land which has been lawfully developed. It will be a matter of judgement based on the evidence as to whether this is the case or not. However, if the development or infrastructure have become lawful due to the passage of time, there would be no reason to not treat them as “lawful” for this purpose. To do otherwise would be contrary to the way in which the planning system operates.

- Where land is no longer occupied by a permanent structure or the building upon it is no longer used, but that land was last occupied by a building for an agricultural or forestry use, it will not be previously developed land. Further, where land is currently occupied by a permanent building which has a different use, having changed its use from agricultural or forestry use, it will be previously developed land. The exception will not apply, as the building would no longer be considered agricultural or for forestry.

92. Previously developed land is or was occupied by permanent structures and includes any associated curtilage. Simply because a site contains structures that would meet the definition of previously developed land does not mean that the whole site should be considered as such (and vice versa). Within a site, for example, there may be structures such as agricultural buildings which are excluded from the definition and it will be necessary to consider the different parts of the site accordingly.

93. Residential gardens which are not in ‘built-up areas’ are not excluded from the general definition of previously developed land in Annex B to the NPPF (as held in Dartford BC v SSCLG [2017] EWCA Civ 141). However, if this is a relevant issue, then a view will have to be reached as to whether the site in question is within a ‘built-up area’ as this is not defined.
