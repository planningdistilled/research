## Introduction

1. This chapter of the Inspector training manual (ITM) concerns human rights and equality considerations in Appeal, Application, Notice and Order casework, with regard to the Human Rights Act 1998 (HRA98) and the Equality Act 2010 (EA10). For Local Plan Inspectors, this chapter supplements LP06 Public Sector Equality Duty in the Local Plan Examinations ITM.

2. This chapter is written primarily for Inspectors but will be relevant to APOs, Appointed Persons and other colleagues with casework duties. Any reference to ‘Inspectors’ in this chapter may be read as encompassing such others.

3. Inspectors must act in accordance with the law, that is, meaning that they must correctly apply the law as it stands to the evidence. The HRA98 and/or EA10 may be relevant when making any decision or recommendation where the decision-maker exercises discretion.

4. That applies to all procedural and casework decisions except where the latter concerns what is lawful or what public rights exist in law. There is no scope to consider human rights or equality implications in a Lawful Development Certificate appeal, in respect of grounds (e), (b), (c), (d) and/or (f) in an Enforcement appeal or in relation to a Definitive Map Modification Order, as set out in the Enforcement and Public Rights of Way chapters.

5. Inspectors can expect to determine cases brought by or involving diverse people with myriad circumstances and needs. Inspectors can expect to see evidence concerning traditions and cultures that they are not personally familiar with. It is always better to seek advice from your line manager, mentor or Knowledge Centre as appropriate – and/or more evidence from the parties – than risk making an error or giving other cause for complaint.

## The Legal Framework

### International Human Rights Law

6. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) was adopted by the United Nations (UN) General Assembly on 10 December 1948. It recognises the ‘inherent dignity and…equal and inalienable rights of all members of the human family’ and sets out those ‘universal’ rights in thirty articles. The principle of universalism underlies the concept of human rights. The UDHR is not binding on member states, but its contents are reflected in treaties which are.

7. The European Convention on Human Rights (the Convention) came into force on 3 September 1953. It drew on the UDHR as well as national documents such as the 1689 Bill of Rights (England) and 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen (France). Section I of the Convention sets out the main rights and freedoms; Section II establishes the European Court of Human Rights (Strasbourg Court or ECHR).

<!-- Part2 p.451 -->

8. Article 1 of Section I of the Convention binds the signatory parties to secure the rights under Articles 2-18. Protocol 1 to the Convention sets out rights that the signatories could not agree to place in the Convention itself.

9. In deciding cases concerning Convention rights, the Strasbourg Court has developed and applies two legal doctrines:

- The Convention is a ‘living instrument’ and human rights are interpreted so that they are relevant to contemporary circumstances. Human rights may be engaged in respect of issues such as climate change or new technologies which were unforeseeable when the Convention was signed.

- Convention rights are subject to a ‘margin of appreciation’, respecting that the different signatory nations have different legal and cultural traditions and should therefore have scope to decide on how the rights apply in practice. The ‘margin of appreciation’ is linked to the concept of ‘subsidiarity’, the principle that social and political issues should be dealt with at the lowest or most local level consistent with their resolution.

10. All Council of Europe member states are party to the Convention and bound by judgments of the Strasbourg Court. The Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement 1998 refers to the Convention as a safeguard and includes a commitment by the UK Government to incorporate the Convention into the law of Northern Ireland.

11. The HRA98 was passed to ‘give further effect’ in UK law of the ‘rights and freedoms guaranteed’ under the Convention. It came into force in October 2000. Article 2 of the ‘Northern Ireland Protocol’ to the Brexit Withdrawal Agreement provides that there shall be no diminution of rights, safeguards or equality of opportunity as set out in the Good Friday Agreement.

12. The amended protocol, set out in the Windsor Framework of 23 February 2023, recognises ‘the need…to protect all dimensions of the Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement’. It states that ‘the Protocol as amended will be subject to the general principles of public international law, as set out under the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties…the fundamental underpinning of this arrangement is in international law, not EU law and the EU institutions’.

13. The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) came into force on 2 September 1990. Article 3(1) provides that ‘in all actions concerning children… undertaken by public or private social welfare institutions, courts of law, administrative authorities or legislative bodies, the best interests of the child shall be a primary consideration’. The Equality and Human Rights Commission (the Commission) provides guidance on the UNCRC.

14. Article 3(1) is not incorporated into the Convention or HRA98, but the Supreme Court considered its application in a public law context in ZH (Tanzania) v SSHD [2011] UKSC 4. It was held that ‘in making the proportionality assessment under Article 8 [of the Convention], the best interests of the child

<!-- Part2 p.452 -->

must be a primary consideration’1. That principle was distilled and applied in a planning context in Stevens v SSCLG [2013] EWHC 792 (Admin), endorsed in Collins v SSCLG [2013] EWCA 1193, as described below.

29. The EA10 was introduced to ‘reform and harmonise equality law’. ### Family Life and Home

75. Article 8(1) provides that everyone has the right to respect for their private and family life, home and correspondence. The term ‘family life’ refers to matters that are essential for a person to enjoy a relationship with their family, which need not be limited to a nuclear family or blood and/or marital relatives.

76. The definitions of ‘single household’, ‘same family’, ‘couple’ and ‘relative’ set out in s258 of the Housing Act 2004 will sometimes be applicable to casework. Generally, whether people are in a family will depend on the nature and cultural context as much as the legal status of their relationships.

### Cases where Article 8 may be Engaged

85. It was held in Stevens that ‘given the nature of those rights and the scope of planning decisions, it is likely that Article 8 will be engaged in many planning decision-making exercises.’ Rights under Article 8 are typically engaged where:

- The decision will result in the imminent or short-term loss of

someone’s home22. That might be the case where an enforcement notice alleges the construction of a dwelling or change to residential use, the building or land is occupied, the notice requires the removal of the building or cessation of the use, and the Inspector is minded to refuse planning permission and uphold the notice. Confirming a Housing CPO may also cause the imminent loss of a home. Any such decision is liable to result in a serious interference with rights under Article 8 in respect of family life as well as home and the best interests of any child(ren).

- The decision could realistically lead to the future loss of someone’s

home. This may arise, for example, where enforcement action is liable to follow dismissal of a s78 appeal for a retrospective grant of planning permission for residential development. It could also arise where outline planning permission or a CPO or local plan allocation or action plan is sought for the redevelopment of existing housing, but further applications or proceedings would be required. An occupier can expect to lose their home in the future if it is subject to temporary permission.

- The decision would or could result in the partial loss of someone’s

home, perhaps a house extension or annex that is already built and occupied. Inspectors should be alert to evidence that the development may be needed to alleviate overcrowding, make the home suitable for a disabled member of the family or allow an extended family to live together.

- The decision could adversely affect living standards or living conditions – ie, the health, wellbeing or quality of life – of persons

### Reasoning

88. Making a decision which is compatible in substance with the HRA98 involves two consecutive stages:

- Identifying the issue(s) whereby human rights are engaged and making reasoned findings on the issue(s).

- Taking account of those findings, with or without express reference to the HRA98 as appropriate, in the overall Conclusion and decision.

89. The Inspector must be alert to and consider properly any evidence that the development would (potentially) have one or more direct or indirect effects on the private or family life or home of one or more persons. Such effects or issues may be significant, if not decisive, even if the parties do not refer expressly to Article 8 or human rights. When framing the (main) issues in a decision letter or report, the Inspector should be mindful as to whether and how the outcome could be an interference with or breach of Article 8 rights.

90. As explained in the Approach to Decision-making ITM (Part 1), ‘the main issues are the essence of the disagreement between the parties and the matters on which your decision will turn…although most main issues in appeal decisions will derive from the reasons for refusal, this is not always the case’. The main issues in any appeal may include matters not covered by the reasons for refusal, such as any need for or benefits of the development raised by the appellant, and/or harms raised by interested parties.

91. Since the main issues provide a structure for your reasoning, they should be framed in terms that allow you to properly identify and ascribe weight to the policy and/or real world effects of the development. Main issues should rarely

<!-- Part2 p.469 -->

be framed in terms of ‘human rights’; whether your decision would accord with the HRA98 is a question for the overall conclusion. And the risk of framing any one issue in terms of human rights is that the Inspector misses the implications of other issues for the Article 8 rights of the individual(s) too.

92. For example, in appeals relating to Traveller sites, as set out in the Gypsy and Traveller Casework ITM, the main issues often include the general need for and supply of Traveller sites; whether there are suitable, affordable, available and acceptable alternative sites; the personal need of the occupier(s) for a Traveller site; and/or other personal circumstances, including the needs of the children to access health services and/or schooling. All of those issues and others such as flood risk may bear on the Article 8 assessment in the overall conclusion.

93. When framing the main issues, focus on what is at the heart of the case for the parties and whether you will need to address (in terms or otherwise) potential interference with Article 8 rights in the conclusion. Understanding what is at the heart of the case can mean bringing emotional intelligence to bear particularly in respect of the evidence of the losing party. Article 8 may be engaged where any of the following is a main issue for one or more of the parties. The list contains examples and is not exhaustive or to be read prescriptively:

- The supply/availability of [accessible] [x bedroom plus] [sheltered] [other specialist] housing/residential land…

- The supply/availability of suitable, affordable and acceptable housing/ residential land within [the catchment area] [commuting distance] of X [School] [Hospital]

- The effects of the development on air quality in the surrounding area and the health of [nearby occupiers]

- The [appellant’s] [occupiers’] personal circumstances and need for the [proposed] [development] [with regard to…]

- The benefits of the [proposed] [development] to the [appellant’s] family

- The consequences of a refusal of permission for the [appellant’s] family

- The effect of the [proposed] [development] on the [living conditions] [private and family life] of nearby occupiers…

94. It follows that ‘personal circumstances’ is not the only issue that is potentially relevant to Article 8. Nonetheless, Inspectors can expect to determine cases where the appellant and/or interested party is concerned about the particular impacts of development on them. In such cases, subject to the evidence, personal circumstances are capable of being a main issue and indeed decisive.

95. The PPG states that ‘planning permission usually runs with the land’, and it is rarely appropriate to provide otherwise. There may be exceptional occasions where development that would normally be permitted may be justified on

<!-- Part2 p.470 -->

planning grounds because of who would benefit from the permission’23. Regard should always be had to that guidance where any party relies in whole or part on personal circumstances, but ‘exceptional’ does not mean ‘never’.

96. Lord Scarman held in Westminster CC v Great Portland Estates [1984] UKHL 10 that the ‘personal circumstances of an occupier, personal hardship, the difficulties of businesses which are of value to the character of a community are not to ignored in the administration of planning control’. If, for example, the appellant says that the proposed extension is required to house an elderly relative or equipment for a disabled child, that will at the heart of the case for them. It will be why they are proposing to carry out what, for them, would be disruptive and expensive works.

97. Issues relevant to private and family life or home often fall to be considered under ‘Other Matters’, but the Inspector should always consider whether that is appropriate in the circumstances. One test is whether the issue is or could be decisive. Also, it can appear unnecessarily insensitive for the Inspector to relegate, for example, personal circumstances to ‘Other Matters’. Doing so can give the impression that the Inspector does not fully appreciate what is at the heart of the case for that person, and indeed it runs the risk of downplaying the issue in the Inspector’s mind.

98. Moreover, it will in some cases be necessary to balance competing Article 8 rights of different parties who should perceive that they have been treated equally. Subject again to the quality of the evidence, it may be hard for parties to understand, for example, why the living conditions of neighbours is a main issue, but the personal circumstances of the appellant are not. That said, Inspectors can and should deal with any claimed impact on family life and home sensitively whether the issue is a main one or not; see Annex B.

99. It should finally be noted in relation to reasoning that the Inspector does not need to be ‘certain’ and hearsay evidence is still evidence. The decision should be based on the probable impacts of the development.

### Conditions

108. It is crucial for Inspectors in their reasoning to consider whether any conditions could be imposed to reduce or overcome any identified harm – and thus alter the proportionality assessment – as explained fully in the Conditions ITM.

109. ### ‘Interference’, ‘Proportionality’, the Conclusion and Decision

111. Article 8(2) states that there shall be no interference by a public authority with the exercise of this right [under Article 8(1)] except such as is in accordance with the law and is necessary in a democratic society in the interests of national security, public safety or the economic well-being of the country, for the prevention of disorder or crime, for the protection of health or morals, or for the protection of the rights and freedoms of others.

112. In other words – and this applies to all qualified rights – it must be clear in decision letters and reports that any engaged human right was weighed against all other material considerations bearing on the legitimate interests of others and the wider public, before the decision or recommendation was made. Interference with the Article 8 right may be permissible if there is a clear legal basis for it and it is necessary in a democratic society.

113. The Courts have held that any likely interference with engaged Article 8 rights must be dealt with as an integral part of the reasoning that leads to the final decision30. The concept of proportionality is crucial because a disproportionate or unjustified interference can result in a ‘violation’ or breach of the individual’s rights. The ‘victim’ of a violation can rely on the HRA98 to seek redress of the

### Evidence and Procedure

137. It is not unusual for the rights of individuals under Article 8 to be engaged in cases proceeding by the written representations procedure. Inspectors should be alert to any claims or evidence which point to a risk of the decision resulting in an interference, whether or not the parties refer expressly to human rights.

138. In most written representation cases, it should be clear from the original file whether and how the rights of individuals to a family and private life and home may be affected. It should be straightforward for the Inspector to properly identify the main issues and make a demonstrably ‘proportionate’ decision.

139. Inspectors should not set too high a bar to what they treat as evidence. Assertion or anecdote is evidence that can be accepted. If it is claimed, for example, that a house extension is required to create an extra child’s bedroom, the Inspector should accept that unless there is evidence to the contrary. The appellant does not need to provide copies of birth certificates or other proof as to how many children they have.

140. However, evidence that is slender, vague or unsubstantiated may carry less weight or simply prove insufficient to make that party’s case. Using the same example, the mere claim that another bedroom is essential may be accepted but would be unlikely to outweigh any harm caused by the development. The appellant might need to show that the extension is needed on particular family or health grounds for their circumstances to be ascribed significant or greater weight. That said, having large families or living with extended families are

<!-- Part2 p.479 -->

traditions associated with particular religious and racial groups and so the PSED as well as Article 8 may be engaged where overcrowding is an issue.

141. Inspectors may seek such additional information that is needed to support a soundly reasoned decision, as described in relation to the PSED below, while being alert to data protection requirements as set out above and in Annex 5 to the Approach to Decision-making (Part 1) ITM.

142. If there is a prospect of a serious interference with Article 8 rights, it may be necessary to determine the case though the hearing or inquiry procedure, so the evidence is properly tested, and Article 8 considerations are fully aired. Inspectors must be pro-active and inquisitorial at such events so that all the main issues are identified and discussed, and all relevant evidence is secured for the proper undertaking of a proportionality assessment.

144. In Waverley BC v Gray & Others [2023] EWHC 2161 (KB), the Council sought an interim injunction despite having ‘little or no information about the personal circumstances of the defendants’. It was held that, as matters including medical needs, and a lack of alternative sites came to light, ‘it was incumbent on the [Council] to investigate matters and to re-assess the balance of factors...the proportionality of the decision should have been revisited…’ For that reason, and since planning and enforcement appeals had been made, the judge declined to make a final injunction in respect of some defendants.

The CoA held in Collins v SSCLG & Fylde BC [2013] EWCA Civ 1193 that, where an appellant is professionally represented, the Inspector may assume that relevant evidence regarding the best interests of the child(ren) is known to their representative, unless something shows a need for further investigation. In practice, an agent may not be so well-informed, and Inspectors must consider what investigation is required where Article 8 may be engaged.

146. Inspectors should also be proactive if they suspect that there may be a ‘safeguarding’ issue affecting any person, including but not limited to children or disabled or elderly people on the site. In such a situation, the Inspector should inform the Police and their line manager as set out in the Approach to Decision-making ITM.

## The PSED and the ‘Three Aims’

### Overview

177. It is best in decisions, or reports, or at events, to refer initially to ‘the Public Sector Equality Duty set out in s149 of the Equality Act 2010’ before using the ‘PSED’ initialism. While the HRA98 enshrines the rights and freedoms of individual ‘members of the human family’, the PSED protects both individuals and groups or communities. Where the word ‘person’ appears in s149, it should be read as meaning a person and/or persons as applicable.

178. The PSED is not a duty to eliminate discrimination, advance opportunity or foster good relations. But it is a duty to have ‘due regard’ to the three aims when making decisions, meaning that:

- It is a duty to understand that eliminating discrimination, advancing opportunity and fostering good relations are the aims of the law. It is a duty to seek to achieve the three aims where possible.

- It is a duty to ensure that any decision giving rise to any negative impacts in relation to the three aims is informed and made with regard to any less harmful alternative outcome44.

179. The Commission explains that ‘the purpose of the PSED is to make sure that public authorities and organisations carrying out public functions think about how they can improve society and promote equality in every aspect of their day-to-day business. This means they must consider, and keep reviewing, how they are promoting equality in decision-making, internal and external policies,

The second and third aims are clearly worded to that end45. S149(6) states that ‘compliance with the [PSED] may involve treating some persons more favourably than others but that is not to be taken as permitting’ prohibited conduct46.

### Eliminating Prohibited Conduct

181. The first aim set out under s149(1)(a) is that a public authority must, in the exercise of its functions, have due regard to the need to eliminate discrimination, harassment, victimisation and any other conduct that is prohibited by or under the EA10.

182. Prohibited conduct includes:

- Direct discrimination: treating a person less favourably because of a protected characteristic (s13); the circumstances must be similar enough for a valid comparison to be made.

- Combined discrimination: treating a person less favourably because of a combination of two relevant protected characteristics (s14).

- Indirect discrimination: applying a provision, criterion or practice (PCP) to everyone so as to result in discrimination in relation to a relevant protected characteristic (s19). Indirect discrimination may be lawful if the organisation can show there is a good reason or ‘objective justification’ for the PCP.

- Harassment: engaging in unwanted conduct related to a relevant protected characteristic or of a sexual nature, and the conduct has the purpose or effect of violating the person’s dignity or creating and intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating or offensive environment for that person (s26).

- Victimisation: subjecting a person to a detriment because they have done, are believed to have done or may do a ‘protected act’ such as bringing proceedings under the EA10 (s27).

### Advancing Equality of Opportunity

186. The second aim of the PSED, set under s149(1)(b), is to have due regard to the need to advance equality of opportunity between persons who share a relevant protected characteristic and persons who do not. Having due regard to that need involves, under s149(3), having due regard in particular to the need to:

a) remove or minimise disadvantages suffered by persons who share a relevant protected characteristic that are connected to that characteristic.

b) take steps to meet the needs of persons who share a relevant protected characteristic that are different from the needs of those who do not share it.

c) encourage persons who share a relevant protected characteristic to participate in public life or in any other activity in which participation by such persons is disproportionately low.

187. The word ‘advance’ is critical; meeting the second aim means actively seeking ‘to increase equality of opportunity’ as set out in the preamble to the EA10.

### Fostering Good Relations

188. The third aim set out under s149(1)(c) is that public authorities must have due regard to the need to foster good relations between persons who share a relevant protected characteristic and persons who do not share it. S149(5) explains that having due regard to the need to foster good relations…involves having due regard, in particular, to the need to (a) tackle prejudice, and (b) promote understanding.

189. Thus, like the second aim, the third is worded to secure positive, not just remedial action. It reinforces the advice given in respect of the POA86 and Article 10 that Inspectors must reject threatening, abusive or insulting oral or written representations.

## The Protected Characteristics

### Overview

190. S149(7) sets out the protected characteristics for the purposes of the PSED: age, disability, gender reassignment, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex and sexual orientation. The protected characteristics for the EA10 as a whole include marriage and civil partnership. An organisation subject to the PSED must have due regard to the need to eliminate discrimination in employment because of marital status. But the three aims of the PSED do not apply to that protected characteristic47.

191. S1 of the EA10 sets out a ‘public sector duty’ regarding socio-economic inequalities; that does not apply to Inspectors49. However, as the Guidance on the Compulsory Purchase Process (MHCLG, 2024) states, ‘although low income is not a protected characteristic, it is not uncommon for people from ethnic minorities, the elderly or people with a disability to be over-represented in low income groups’.

192. Inspectors should be alert to any evidence in any casework that the local community, whether ‘low income’ or not, includes groups of people with protected characteristics – such as race and/or religion, as well as age and/or disability – who may be affected by the decision on the case. Similarly, the ‘community’ affected by the decision may not be geographical but dispersed and united by their protected characteristic(s), such as the users of an LGBTQ+ venue that is proposed to be built or demolished.

193. Protected characteristics should not be considered as a matter of lifestyle or unusual, even where they apply temporarily or the individual has made an active choice, such as where someone is pregnant or has converted to a religion. The Oxford English Dictionary (OED) defines ‘characteristic’ as ‘a feature or quality belonging typically to a person…and serving to identify them’. Protected characteristics are integral to who people are.

194. Inspectors should ensure that the protected characteristics or people with them are not ‘othered’, that is, described or treated in such a way as to mark them out as not fitting in with the dominant social group, or as different or alien to oneself. Protected characteristics should always be discussed with sensitivity and respect. Advice is set out below on how to proceed where it is unclear as to whether a person’s characteristics are protected for the purposes of the PSED in the circumstances of the case, and how to write about and address people.

195. As noted above, s14 of the EA10 gives protection from discrimination because of a combination of protected characteristics. ‘Intersectionality’ is a word

198. A reference to an age group is a reference to a group of persons defined by reference to age, whether that be a particular age or a range of ages.

The provision of housing for a particular age group would normally fit into that category.

### Disability

203. S6(1) provides that a person has a disability if (a) they have a physical or mental impairment and (b) the impairment has a substantial and long-term adverse effect on their ability to carry out normal day-to-day activities. Schedule 1 of the EA10 contains supplementary provisions on the determination of disability, and there is also guidance from the Commission.

204. The EA10 is based on the ‘social model’ of disability, which is that disability is created by barriers in society, particularly environmental, attitudinal and organisational barriers. Focussing on the removal or mitigation of such barriers

– by making reasonable adjustments – is a more positive and inclusive approach than the ‘medical model’ to try and ‘fix’ disabled people in order that they can participate in society.

- It is not necessary for the cause of the impairment to be established or the impairment to be the result of illness.

208. It is not vital or necessarily helpful for a person to have a formal diagnosis when establishing whether they are disabled for the purposes of the PSED51. For some conditions, there are long waiting lists to get a diagnosis which may not even result in medical treatment. A diagnosis shows the cause of an impairment, but the key questions are how a person is impaired (what their symptoms are), how the impairment affects normal day-to-day activities, and whether the effects are substantial and long-term.

209. For example, if it is claimed that a house extension is needed for a relative with pulmonary fibrosis, the Inspector may gain little useful information from medical records. The crucial details would be that the person is impaired by severe ‘long-term’ breathlessness and fatigue so that they need a separate bedroom with a hospital bed and oxygen equipment, and there is no space for that in the dwelling as it stands. A person with a different diagnosis could have the same requirements, while a person with the same diagnosis might not.

210. Similarly, if a participant asks that a hearing is adjourned because of their anxiety, evidence of a diagnosis would not aid the decision as to accept or refuse the request. The questions to ask are why anxiety would prevent the person from attending the event on the scheduled date, whether things would realistically be different on another date, and whether steps less drastic than adjournment could overcome the problem. It might be that the person experiences panic when speaking in public but could prepare a written statement for a friend or the Inspector to read out instead.

### ‘Substantial’ and ‘Long Term’

211. S212(1) of the EA10 defines ‘substantial’ as meaning ‘more than minor or trivial’. The Guidance on Matters suggests that, when considering whether an effect is substantial, regard could be had to the time that the person takes to carry out their normal day-to-day activities, the way in which they carry them out, cumulative adverse effects of the impairment on more than one activity, how far a person can be reasonably expected to modify or reduce the effects of an impairment, and environmental conditions.

212. The effect of treatment should normally be disregarded when addressing whether an impairment has a substantial effect. Paragraph 5 of Schedule 1 of the EA10 is explicit that ‘an impairment is to be treated as having a substantial adverse effect…if measures are being taken to treat or correct it and, but for those measures, the impairment would be likely to have that effect’.

213. The term ‘measures’ includes medical treatment and use of prosthesis or other aid, but not spectacles or contact lenses. For example, if a person has

### ‘Normal Day-to-Day Activities’

215. The EA10 does not define ‘normal day-to-day activities’ but the Guidance on Matters suggests that the term encompasses regular or daily activities such as personal care, shopping, using the phone, and work or study-related activities such as using a computer or interacting with colleagues or customers.

216. ‘Normal day-to-day activities’ do not include those which are normal only for a particular person or small group, but equally they do not need to be done by a majority of the population. Breast-feeding, for example, is a normal day-to-day activity. Activities may be normal and day-to-day even if one carries them out at an unusual time, such as where a shift worker sleeps during the day.

217. Participation in casework proceedings should be treated as involving normal day-to-day activities, whether or not it is actually normal for the particular party or person. That is because activities such as writing emails, letters or statements, or speaking in meetings or events, can all be considered normal and day-to-day.

218. Thus, if a person has difficulty participating in any aspect of casework by reason of disability, the Inspector must have due regard as to whether they or the Inspectorate can take reasonable steps to meet the person’s needs.

### Discrimination and ‘Reasonable Adjustments’

219. For the second aim of the PSED, s149(4) states that the steps involved in meeting the needs of disabled persons…include, in particular, steps to take account of disabled persons’ disabilities. Such ‘steps’ should be considered in the context of the following other provisions of the EA10.

220. S13(3) of the EA10 states that, if the protected characteristic is disability, and B is not a disabled person, A does not discriminate against B only because A treats or would treat disabled persons more favourably than A treats B. In other words, making reasonable adjustments for disabled persons does not amount to discrimination against persons who are not disabled.

221. Under s15, a disabled person is discriminated against if they are treated unfavourably because of a consequence of their disability, and that is not shown to be a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim. This does not apply if the person treating the disabled person unfavourably did not know and could not have been reasonably expected to know of the disability.

222. The duty to make reasonable adjustments under s20(1)-(2) comprises three requirements: to take such steps as is reasonable to avoid disabled persons

A disabled person is discriminated against if there is a failure to comply with that duty in relation to them. S20(7) provides that a person who is subject to a duty to make reasonable adjustments is not entitled, unless there is express provision to the contrary, to require a disabled person to pay to any extent the costs of complying with the duty.

224. What is ‘reasonable’ will depend on many factors and therefore be fact sensitive but still be an objective test. Considerations may include how effective the adjustment would be in avoiding the disadvantage the disabled person would otherwise experience, its practicality, the cost, the resources and size of the organisation and the availability of financial support53.

### Gender Reassignment

225. In Goodwin v UK [2002] ECHR 588, the Strasbourg Court held that the claimant’s rights under Articles 8 and 12 (right to marriage) were breached by the UK Government’s failure to make provision for ‘transsexuals’ to obtain legal recognition of their gender reassignment.

### Religion or Belief

240. S10(1) and (2) of the EA10 provides that religion means any religion, and belief means any religious or philosophical belief; a reference to religion or belief also includes a reference to a lack of religion or belief, so that atheism and agnosticism are protected.

241. S10(3) provides that a reference to a person with a particular protected characteristic is a reference to a person of a particular religion or belief; persons who share that protected characteristic have the same religion or belief. This will include particular religious denominations such as Catholicism.

242. The Census 2021 indicated that, for the first time, less than half the population (46.5%) of England and Wales identified as Christian. While the figure had fallen markedly from 59.3% in 2011, Christianity remained the largest religion in England and Wales. The second most common response was “no religion”, up from 25.2% in 2011 to 37.2% in 2021. 243. The criteria for a ‘protected belief’ were laid down in Grainger Plc & Others v Nicholson [2009] UKEAT/0219/09: it must i) be genuinely held; ii) be a belief and not an opinion or viewpoint; iii) relate to a weighty and substantial aspect of

244. The Commission gives belief in man-made climate change as an example of a protected belief; others include humanism, pacifism and vegetarianism. Beliefs in racial superiority or that the Holocaust did not occur are not protected since they are incompatible with human dignity and the fundamental rights of others. 245. It is unlikely to ever be necessary to decide for casework purposes whether a religion or belief is protected. Doing so would rarely assist in making the decision anyway; the important question, as set out below, is rather the relevance of the belief to (in a planning appeal, for example) the proposed development.

246. Direct discrimination in respect of religion or belief cannot be legally justified except where there is an exemption in the EA10. ## The PSED and Casework

### ‘Due Regard’

261. The Government Equalities Office advises that 'having due regard means consciously thinking about the three aims of the Equality Duty as part of the process of decision-making. This means that consideration of equality issues must influence the decisions reached by public bodies…’59 More informally, the Commission advises that to have ‘due regard’ means ‘that you have made yourself fully aware of – and understood – what the PSED means, and that you have put this knowledge into practice’.

262. In R (oao Brown) v SSWP [2008] EWHC (Admin) a case which concerned the then disability equality duty, Aikens LJ put forward general principles as to how a public authority could fulfil its duty to have due regard ‘to the identified goals’:

a. Those in the public authority taking decisions that might affect persons with protected characteristics must be made aware of the duty of due regard.

b. The duty must be fulfilled before and when a [decision] that will or might affect persons with protected characteristics is being considered by the public authority; ‘it involves a conscious approach and state of mind’.

c. The duty must be exercised in substance, with rigour and an open mind. The duty has to be integrated with the discharge of public functions; it is not a question of ‘ticking boxes’.

d. That a public authority has not specifically mentioned the duty is not determinative of whether the duty has been performed. It is good practice for the decision-maker to refer to s149 where in play.

e. The duty is a non-delegable one.

f. The duty is a continuing one.

g. It is good practice for those exercising public functions to keep an adequate record showing that they had adequately considered the duty and pondered relevant questions. Proper record-keeping encourages transparency and conscientious undertaking of the duty.

263. In Bracking v SSWP [2013] EWCA Civ 1345, McCombe LJ endorsed and expanded upon the ‘Brown principles’:

- General regard to equality issues is not the same as specific regard by way of conscious approach to the statutory criteria.

### Cases where the PSED may be Engaged

267. The PSED is engaged in appeal, application, order or plan casework where the decision will involve the creation or loss of, or other significant impact in relation to land or development that is solely or mainly used by persons with one or more protected characteristics.

268. Such cases could include a proposed allocation or development of land for use as a Traveller site; the construction of or change of use of a building to a church, mosque or synagogue; the erection of a house extension for an elderly or disabled relative; changes to the layout of a listed building to facilitate wheelchair access; the felling of trees within the grounds of extra care housing; the diversion of a public path to a school; or the redevelopment of the site of an existing community centre.

269. It may not be evident from the description that the case directly concerns persons with protected characteristics. In R (oao Harris) v Haringey LBC [2010] EWCA Civ 703, the Council permitted the ‘demolition of existing buildings and erection of mixed use developments…’ The buildings to be demolished in fact included an indoor market known as the ‘Latin Village’, where people from South American, Caribbean and African communities met to socialise as well as buy or sell goods and services.

270. The PSED is engaged in casework that does not specifically concern but would have particular impacts on persons with protected characteristics. The list of such cases could be endless. The construction or loss of any development subject to a planning or enforcement appeal, the making of CPO, the felling of any tree or stopping up of any path may, in the circumstances, have particular implications for a person or persons with one or more protected characteristics.

### Reasoning

275. The advice given here is similar to that pertaining to Article 8. The Inspector should identify the issue(s) whereby protected characteristics are engaged, make reasoned findings on those issues, and taken account of them, with or without reference to the PSED, in their overall Conclusion and decision.

276. The PSED is likely to be engaged in casework where the development would be liable to have specific impacts upon a person, group or community with one or more protected characteristics because of those protected characteristics. Whether or not any party refers to the PSED, any such real-life impacts (and relevant policies) must be addressed in the reasoning before an assessment is made in the overall conclusion as to the equality implications of the decision.

277. Given that the PSED ‘must be exercised in substance, with rigour and an open mind’, Inspectors should scrutinise the evidence before them so as to identify whether any persons with protected characteristics are party to the case, and whether those characteristics could be relevant to the decision because of the implications – positive or negative – for those persons.

278. If the evidence suggests that an issue related to protected characteristics is significant for those persons and/or potentially decisive, it should be a main issue or consideration in the decision letter or report. This advice particularly applies if there is a prospect of the decision being at odds with the PSED.

279. The Inspector should frame such issues not in terms of the duty – which is a matter for the Conclusion – but in terms of the effects on the person(s) with the protected characteristic(s). The framing of the main issue should enable the Inspector to properly identify and ascribe weight to those effects with regard to

### ‘Character and Appearance’

291. It is not unusual to receive objections to development on ‘character and appearance’ grounds. Inspectors should bear in mind that the people who occupy an area are part and parcel of its character. It is important to be respectful to those who live, work or play in the locality, and to avoid sweeping, ambiguous, stereotyping or pejorative statements in relation to ‘character’.

292. For example, Inspectors should take care in how they describe a street with inharmonious house extensions, since such developments may be a sign that this is an area where families want to stay. If an external wheelchair ramp would fail to preserve or enhance the appearance of a listed building, that should not slip into a suggestion of harm to ‘character’ when the purpose of the ramp is to increase the use of the property by disabled people.

293. In reasoning on ‘character and appearance’, pinpoint what is in dispute (such as the use, scale, form or landscaping), explain the alleged harm in objective terms and consider the acceptability of the development in terms of relevant policies and guidance. An SPD or Area Action Plan may provide information to help an Inspector apply design policies in the context of the community.

### Unlawfulness and IUD

294. Much the same approach should be taken here as with Article 8. Planning permission should never be refused or approved just because it is sought retrospectively for (potentially) unlawful development. However, in accordance with NPPF Policy DM8, and whether or not the PSED is engaged, if it is concluded based on evidence that unauthorised development was intentional, that fact should be given substantial weight in considering whether to grant planning permission.

295. Any finding that the development is ‘intentionally unauthorised’ cannot affect the weight given to other considerations. Inspectors should not, for example, give reduced weight to the appellant’s personal circumstances because they carried out IUD. Whether harm caused by the development outweighs or is outweighed by the benefits for persons with protected characteristics is a question to address in the final balance.

### The Conclusion and Decision

300. Where the PSED is engaged, the decision must be shown to be proportionate.

301. Ss13, 17 and 19 of the EA10 provide that specified forms of discrimination are not unlawful if a ‘proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim’. That phrase does not appear in s149, but the concept of proportionality is nonetheless crucial. ‘Legitimate aims’ that are engaged in casework include those set out in the relevant planning (or other) policy and legislation, such as the protection of the Green Belt, and the three aims set out in the PSED.

302. The ‘Bingham checklist’ and ‘proportionality assessment’ apply strictly to Article 8 and should not be applied to any conclusion on the PSED. But there are similarities in approach and, if Article 8 is engaged as well as the PSED, the conclusion on the latter would sensibly follow that on the former.

303. In Coleman v Barnet LBC & Another [2012] EWHC 3725 (Admin), it was claimed that the Council failed to discharge the PSED when deciding to grant

68 Tower Hamlets LBC application ref: PA/17/00250

<!-- Part2 p.506 -->

planning permission for the redevelopment of a garden centre where the café was used as meeting place by elderly and disabled people.

304. Lindblom J (as he then was) noted that the Council officer had described consultation responses and objections to the development accurately and at length in their report. They had accepted, in reasoning on the issues and in discussion on the PSED, that elderly and disabled people relied on the facility, there was no viable alternative place for many users, and the proposed development would not replace what was lost.

305. In their conclusion to the report, the officer factored in the effect of the development ‘on the identified protected groups’ but found that effect would be outweighed by other considerations on balance. It was held that the Council had properly discharged the PSED ‘not merely in form…but also in substance’.

306. That the weight ascribed to different factors is a matter for the decision-maker was reinforced in Patel v SSCLG & Others [2016] EWHC 3354 (Admin). Mr Justice Ouseley noted that:

‘There is no duty to give particular weight to the needs of [persons with protected characteristics], no duty to achieve the outcome which advantages them most or disadvantages them the least…[the Inspector] is not obliged by s149 to find some countervailing public benefit to set against the greater disadvantage…’

307. On the facts in Patel, it was also held that the Inspector, in deciding a prior approval appeal, had applied their mind to the needs of the elderly and disabled, despite not referring specifically to the s149 duty. Given the Addison case discussed above, the Brown principle that the PSED is a duty to be met in substance remains good law.

308. That said, it will in some cases be necessary to refer expressly to the PSED and, where findings on the main issues point different ways, to set out a full balancing exercise in the Conclusion to a decision or report. The Conclusion should then cover:

- The findings on the main issues and the weight ascribed to those findings, with regard where appropriate to conditions.

- The balance, and what decision that points to.

- The implications of that decision for persons with protected characteristics in terms of the three aims of the PSED.

- Whether meeting the three aims justifies or adds weight to a decision that is consistent with the PSED.

- Why a decision that is not consistent with the three aims of the PSED is nonetheless proportionate.

<!-- Part2 p.507 -->

309. Thus, as with human rights, any failure to secure the three aims will be lawful, and a proportionate means to achieve a legitimate aim, so long as there is no error in the reasoning; the Inspector has had, in accordance with Brown, conscious ‘due regard’ as part of the decision-making process, and the Inspector has exercised the duty in substance, with rigour and an open mind.

310. To assist Inspectors, example wording for conclusions is set out in Annex C. It should be adapted to the case and not treated as a template or standard.

### Enforcement Appeals: Grounds (a) and (g)

311. As set out above in relation to human rights, effects on persons with protected characteristics may need to be addressed when deciding whether to grant planning permission pursuant to ground (a) and when deciding whether to extend the period for compliance with the notice pursuant to ground (g). The PSED may be engaged in both of these grounds, even if relevant evidence is only submitted in respect of one.

### Evidence and Procedure

313. Again, much advice here is similar to that given in relation to Article 8:

- It is not unusual for the PSED to be engaged in cases proceeding by written representations.

- Inspectors should be alert to any claims or evidence which point to implications for persons with protected characteristics, whether or not they refer expressly to equality or the PSED.

- Assertion or anecdote is evidence that should be accepted unless it is contradicted and even if it carries little weight.

- Inspectors should request information as necessary to make a sound decision, while being alert to data protection requirements.

- It will usually be straightforward in written representation cases for the Inspector to properly identify the main issues and make a demonstrably ‘proportionate’ decision.

- If there is a prospect of a serious harm to persons with protected characteristics – or their needs otherwise being a decisive factor – it may be appropriate to determine the case though the hearing or inquiry procedure, so that the evidence is properly tested and the implications of the PSED are fully aired.

<!-- Part2 p.508 -->

- Inspectors must be pro-active and inquisitorial at hearings or inquiries, to ensure that all the main issues are discussed, all relevant evidence is brought forward and there is enough information to have due regard to the three aims.

314. Occasionally, it may be unclear as to whether a person’s characteristics are ‘protected’ or not. For example, the evidence may be ambiguous as to whether a health condition represents a disability, or a belief is protected. As a rule, Inspectors should avoid making any determination that a person does or does not have a protected characteristic; that is a legal question and the focus in any case should be on the link between the characteristic(s) and development.

315. If an issue related to the person’s characteristics is material but the evidence is insufficient for the Inspector to make a reasoned decision, sensitive enquiries should be made, with questions focussed on the issue rather than person. For example, if it is proposed to carry out [harmful] works to a listed building in order to meet the health needs of an occupier, the Inspector will need to understand what those needs are and why the development is required to meet them. It should not be necessary to interrogate the health condition which gives rise to those needs; doing so would be intrusive and probably unhelpful.

316. It would only be appropriate for an Inspector to reach a view as to whether a person’s characteristic is protected or not (on the balance of probabilities) if that person is relying on the characteristic for their case, the issue is critical, and the parties have properly discussed the matter. Even then, the safest and most respectful approach will normally be for the Inspector to either give the person the benefit of the doubt and assume that their characteristic is protected or reach no view. The protection in itself merely means the PSED is engaged, it would not make that person’s case for or against the appeal.

317. In Local Plan, Infrastructure and major planning appeals, the information before the Inspector may include an Equality Impact Assessment (EqiA). An EqIA should be an evidence-based document which assesses the likely impacts of whatever is subject to the case on persons with protected characteristics69. It is not for the Inspector to judge whether the EqIA is robust or the party who prepared it discharged the PSED. The Inspector should rather use the EqIA in the same way as other evidence: to inform your reasoning that will lead to your conclusion and decision.

318. An EqIA may carry significant weight, but the Inspector is not bound to agree with any or all of its findings especially – but not only – if the parties do not agree either. If other evidence indicates, for example, that the EqIA underestimates the impact of a proposal on the elderly, the Inspector should canvas the matter and make their own finding as to what the impact would be.

Given the second aim of the PSED and wording of s149(3)(c), concern may be expressed that other organisations did not encourage persons who share a relevant protected characteristic to participate in proceedings. For example, questions may be raised in examination as to whether the Romany Gypsy community was properly consulted on a Local Plan. Objectors to a planning appeal may question the public consultation on the application.

320. It will be for the authority as to whether they properly ‘encouraged’ participation by persons with relevant protected characteristics. It is not the role of the Inspector to decide whether any other body complied with the PSED in the exercise of their functions. However, whether the consultation was adequate in the circumstances may bear on the soundness of the Local Plan or robustness of the Council’s case at appeal.

321. Inspectors must comply with the PSED70 in procedural decisions of all kinds, ranging from whether to accept late representations to whether to adjourn an inquiry. It should be borne in mind that people with protected characteristics may be involved in any casework as a party or on a professional basis.

Adjustments may also be needed, for example, when considering what the procedure is, deadlines for or formats of representations, how much time is allowed for the site visit and the frequency of breaks or length of sitting days71.
