# 22 Flood Risk: Assessment of flood risk

<!-- Part1 p.1070 -->

## Assessment of flood risk

### Establish if the site is at risk of flooding

9. According to section F4 of the NPPF, an application must be accompanied by a site-specific Flood Risk Assessment (FRA) if the development is:

- in Flood Zones 2 and 3a and 3b

- in Flood Zone 1 where the site is of 1 hectare or more

- land identified by the EA as having critical drainage problems

- land identified in an SFRA as being at increased flood risk in the future

- land that may be subject to other sources of flooding, where the development would introduce a more vulnerable use

10. Even if the site is not at risk of flooding, section F8 of the NPPF requires that all development that could affect drainage on or around a site should incorporate SuDS to reduce wider flood risk. Proposals for major development should take into account advice from the LLFA, and have minimum standards and maintenance arrangements.

### Site-specific Flood Risk Assessment

11. In most cases, an application will be accompanied by an FRA carried out by an appropriately qualified specialist. An exception to this may be if it is for a simple, low risk development, such as a house extension.

<!-- Part1 p.1071 -->

12. Para 020 of the PPG states that the objectives of a site-specific FRA are to establish:

- whether a proposed development is likely to be affected by current or future flooding from any source;

- whether it will increase flood risk elsewhere;

- whether the measures proposed to deal with these effects and risks are appropriate;

- the evidence for the local planning authority to apply (if necessary) the Sequential Test, and;

- whether the development will be safe and pass the Exception Test, if applicable.

13. The level of detail needs to be credible and fit for purpose. The FRA should be proportionate to the anticipated degree of flood risk and the scale, nature and location of the development. It should make use of the SFRA, the Flood Map for Flooding, and the surface water and groundwater flood risk information on the ‘long term flood risk’ site.

14. As a minimum, an FRA should contain the information set out in the flood risk assessment checklist in para 080 of the PPG.

15. The following sections describe the most common sources of information and how these should be used.

16. You will need to be satisfied that you have sufficient information before you to understand the risk from flooding. Where it is missing, you may conclude that the proposal fails to demonstrate that it avoids flood risk, in which case a Sequential Test may be needed.

17. The absence of an FRA, or the fact that a submitted FRA does not contain sufficient or adequate evidence on which to base a decision can sometimes be a reason for refusal.

### Risk management authorities

18. Responsibility for managing flooding and responding to planning applications falls to several bodies, with different remits, which can sometimes appear to overlap. These include the EA, LLFAs, District Councils, Internal Drainage Boards, Highways Authorities, and Water and Sewerage Companies. The main bodies are described below to help understand how they relate to each other, their responsibilities and what you should expect to receive from them.

<!-- Part1 p.1072 -->

EA | The EA supervises and works with other organisations to manage the risk of flooding and coastal erosion in England. It also directly manages flood risk from main rivers, the sea and reservoirs.

There is a statutory requirement for local planning authorities to consult the EA for developments in certain areas of flood risk (as defined in Schedule 4 of the Town and Country Planning (Development Management Procedure) (England) Order 2015 before granting planning permission. For lower risk sites the EA may refer to standing advice.

The EA provides flood risk guidance for local planning authorities and developers on what to include in a flood risk assessment, when to consult the Environment Agency and how to address flood risk using standing advice.

The EA produces the Flood Map for Planning and the ‘Long term flood risk’ data and mapping.

Applicants can also buy additional data from the EA to help complete the flood risk assessment. This includes information about risk management structures, climate change and residual flood risk, in addition to data for additional modelling. These are referred to as ‘Product 1’ to ’Product 8’ information.

All local planning authorities should notify the Environment Agency of the decision on any planning application where the Agency has objected on flood risk grounds.

For any planning application for major development where the local planning authority is minded to grant permission and the proposal:

1. Is within Flood Zones 2 or 3, or on land within Flood Zone 1 which has been notified to the local planning authority as having critical drainage problems; and

2. Is the subject of a sustained objection by the Environment Agency on flood-risk grounds,

the local planning authority must follow the procedure provided for in the Town and Country Planning (Consultation) (England) Direction 2021. This can result in a referral to the Secretary of State for possible call-in.

LLFAs | LLFAs have the lead operational role in managing the risk of flooding from surface water and groundwater. In areas with no district or borough council, they also have the lead role in managing flood risk from ‘ordinary watercourses’, for example any watercourse that isn’t a main river.

They can be either unitary authorities or county councils.

They prepare and maintain a preliminary flood risk assessment, flood hazard maps, flood risk maps and flood risk management plans

<!-- Part1 p.1073 -->

When considering proposals for major development the local planning authority will need to consult the lead local flood authority on surface water drainage.

District | Manage flood risk from ordinary watercourses, operate and maintain Councils | existing sea defences and carry out other work to manage flood risk from the sea.

They take flood risk into account when making decisions on development in their area

Internal | These are independent public bodies responsible for water level Drainage | management in low lying areas. They work in partnership with Boards | other authorities to actively manage and reduce the risk of flooding.

### Strategic Flood Risk Assessments

19. A Strategic Flood Risk Assessment (SFRA) is a study carried out by one or more local planning authorities to assess the risk to an area from flooding from all sources. An SFRA takes into account the impacts of climate change and assesses the cumulative impact that land use changes and development in the area will have on flood risk. It is developed in consultation with the EA, the LLFA and local stakeholders.

20. The SFRA is used to inform strategic policies including, among other things, the allocation of land in the local plan. The policies should consider cumulative impacts in, or affecting, local areas susceptible to flooding. The SFRA and policies should take account of advice from the EA and other relevant flood RMAs, such as lead local flood authorities and internal drainage boards.

21. At paragraph F5.3, the NPPF explicitly states that it is the SFRA that will provide the basis for applying the Sequential Test for both the plan and individual developments.

22. There are two levels of SFRA, which reflect the likely risk of flooding from all sources and development pressures. They are:

- Level 1 SFRAs represent the first stage in assessing strategic flood risk and identify where more detailed assessment may be necessary.

- Level 2 SFRA, where development cannot be allocated outside areas at risk of flooding, or it anticipated that applications may come forward in areas at risk of flooding.

<!-- Part1 p.1074 -->

23. More guidance about strategic FRAs can be found in the EA’s ‘How to prepare a strategic flood risk assessment’.

### EA’s Flood Map for Planning and the long-term flood risk service

24. The data shown on the flood map for planning should be used alongside the SFRA.

### Flood map for planning: ‘flood zones plus climate change’

25. There are two datasets shown on the flood map for planning.

- The first shows the risk of flooding from rivers and the sea in Flood Zones, both for the current day and with climate change, represented by ‘2070 to 2125’.

- A second set of maps shows the extent of surface water flooding according to an annual likelihood. *Note: This was updated on 28 May 2026 to add climate change extents and banded depth information. ‘Banded depths’ are the depth of water during different severities of flash flooding. This is intended to give greater focus to surface water flooding and on resilience and recovery measures rather than relying on traditional flood control structures.

26. Flood defences are ignored because they can fail and water could spill over the top if the water is deep enough.

27. Flood Zone 1 sites that are within the ‘Flood Zones plus climate change’ extent should be treated as if they were in Flood Zone 2 according to the EA’s guidance. These are shown in orange on the Flood Map for Planning.

### Long-term flood risk service

28. The PPG states that the service is a source of information to inform a site-specific FRA. However, it is clarified in the EA’s ‘Flood risk assessments: applying for planning permission’ that it should only be used to check the high level risk from groundwater or reservoirs. This mapping should not be used in development planning to assess the risk from rivers, the sea or surface water (from May 2026).

29. This mapping also ignores flood defences.

### Climate change

30. All assessments of flood risk should include an allowance for climate change. Further information about how climate change allowances should be applied can be found here: Flood risk assessments: climate change allowances

31. This explains how predictions of changes to peak river flow, rainfall intensity, sea level rise and offshore wind speed and extreme wave height should be applied for each river catchment.

### Minor, major and non-major development in relation to flood risk

32. Para 051 of the PPG provides the following definitions for the purpose of flood risk assessment:

Minor development | minor non-residential extensions (industrial/commercial/leisure etc): extensions with a floorspace not in excess of 250 square metres.

alterations: development that does not increase the size of buildings, e.g. alterations to external appearance.

householder development: for example, sheds, garages, games rooms etc. within the curtilage of the existing dwelling, in addition to physical extensions to the existing dwelling itself.

This definition excludes any proposed development that would create a separate dwelling within the curtilage of the existing dwelling (e.g. subdivision of houses into flats) or any other development with a purpose not incidental to the enjoyment of the dwelling.

Major development | ‘Major development’ is as defined by the Town and Country Planning (Development Management Procedure) (England) Order 2015 (Article 2).

Non-major development ‘Non major development’ is any development falling below the above thresholds but excluding minor development. For example, a planning application for 8 dwellings an office building creating 750 square metres of floor space, or a development with a site area of 0.4 hectares.

### Changes of use

33. Para 052 also explains that changes of use can increase the vulnerability of the development or result in occupation or use by people who are more vulnerable than the previous occupants/users to risks from flooding.

34. Older existing properties may not previously have been subject to a flood risk assessment and appropriate mitigation measures, or the nature or severity of the flood risk may have changed over time, requiring more appropriate mitigation. Even if a development’s vulnerability is not increasing, change of use

<!-- Part1 p.1076 -->

can often present an opportunity to improve the flood resilience of existing development.

### Permitted development rights and prior approval

35. A number of permitted development rights are subject to prior approval by the local planning authority in respect of flooding. Such applications for prior approval in areas of flood risk must be accompanied by a site-specific FRA.

36. As per section F4 of the NPPF, flooding prior approvals require consultation with the EA where the development is in an area within Flood Zone 2 or Flood Zone 3, or in an area within Flood Zone 1 which has critical drainage problems.

37. Rights that provide for hard surfaces, for both householders or business, require that provision is made for a permeable or porous surface to ensure that such works do not increase levels of surface water runoff.

### Assessment of vulnerability

38. There are five classes of flood risk vulnerability set out in Annex F, Table 2 of the NPPF and this should be referred to for a full list. In summary, these are:

- Essential infrastructure, including some transport and energy projects

- Highly vulnerable, including emergency infrastructure required during flooding, basement dwellings and caravans for permanent use

- More vulnerable, including dwelling houses, hospitals, care homes, caravans for temporary use and some non-residential uses

- Less vulnerable, including works that do not need to remain operational during flooding, shops, car parks

- Water compatible development, including pumping stations, sand and gravel workings, water-based activities, open space and recreation.

### Lifetime of development

39. Para 006 of the PPG states that residential development can be assumed to have a lifetime of at least 100 years, unless there is a specific justification for considering a different period. For example, this could be a time-limited planning condition.

40. The lifetime of a non-residential development depends on the characteristics of that development, but a period of at least 75 years is likely to form a starting point for assessment.

<!-- Part1 p.1077 -->

41. Where development has an anticipated lifetime significantly beyond 100 years such as some major infrastructure projects, or where it would create significant land-use change such as a new settlement or substantial urban extension, it may be appropriate to consider a longer period for the lifetime of development

42. Para 005 of the PPG sets out the following considerations when determining whether a proposed development will be safe for its lifetime:

- the characteristics of a possible flood event, including residual risks. For example, the type and source of flooding and frequency, depth, velocity, speed of onset and duration.

- the safety of people within a building if it floods. The safety of the people around the building. Including people who are less mobile. This includes the ability of users to safely access and exit a building during a design flood and to evacuate before an extreme flood (0.1% annual probability with allowance for climate change).

- structural safety of the buildings

- the impact of a flood on the essential services provided to or from a development.

43. Further advice is provided in para 050 where development must be time-limited and potentially relocated, for example because of coastal erosion or increased risk because of climate change.

### Reliance on flood risk management infrastructure

44. Where proposals are located in areas of flood risk which are protected by existing flood defences that seek to protect the site and/or the wider area, para 005 of the PPG states that FRAs should, where appropriate:

- identify how the infrastructure will be operated, funded and maintained

- ensure there is space for maintenance. This means that the FRA should demonstrate that the development would not impede or curtail the operation of the flood defence and ensure adequate space for maintenance of this.

- consider the consequences of it failing or the design standard being exceeded

- consider the likelihood of defences keeping pace with climate change

### Common issues with assessment of flood risk

### FRA is out of date

<!-- Part1 p.1078 -->

45. The FRA can become out of date because new information emerges, included updated EA flood risk maps. Where this has been raised and where flooding is a key determining issue in the appeal, it is recommended that the views of the parties (including the EA) on the implications of this are sought.’

### FRA uses the wrong modelling data

46. If one of the parties raises concerns that the wrong modelling data have been used, then it will be necessary to consult all parties to gather views on the significance of this.

47. If the consequences of erroneous modelling are likely to cause the outcome of the risk assessment to be worse and/or unacceptable then this would be a matter of considerable adverse weight, and it may be appropriate to dismiss the case on the basis that an adequate FRA must be submitted in areas at risk of flooding.

### FRA doesn’t include surface water risk

48. If the surface water risk has not been included and the omission not explained, then it may be appropriate to contact the parties to gather their views on this.

49. If such an assessment should have been provided then the lack of it would be a matter of considerable adverse weight, and it may be appropriate to dismiss the case on the basis that an adequate FRA must be submitted in areas at risk of flooding.

### The water level would be really low

50. The risk is a consequence of both the height and velocity of flood water. According to the Government’s guidance on flash flooding, shallow flood water (around 15cm or 6 inches) that does not look dangerous can knock a person over or conceal hazards. It only takes around 60cm (2 feet) of water to carry away a vehicle of any size.

51. There is nothing in the policy or guidance that absolves applicants from undertaking an FRA or sequential test because the water level would be low. However, the characteristics of a flood are relevant when assessing mitigation measures, including escape routes, under paragraph F7.1 and 2 of the NPPF.

### There would be an escape route

52. There is nothing in the policy or guidance that absolves applicants from undertaking an FRA or sequential test because there is an escape route.

53. However, the efficacy of an escape route is taken into account when assessing the safety of the site over its lifetime.

<!-- Part1 p.1079 -->

### Only the road floods

54. A road is built development. An FRA and sequential test are still necessary in these circumstances according to paragraph F5.2 of the NPPF.

55. The degree to which the site floods and the use of the road as an escape route are matters when assessing the safety of the site over its lifetime.

### The building is already there

56. Changes of use are not subject to the sequential test.

57. However, changes of use can increase the vulnerability of the development or result in occupation or use by people who are more vulnerable than the previous occupants/users to risks from flooding.

58. Older existing properties may not previously have been subject to a flood risk assessment and appropriate mitigation measures, or the nature or severity of the flood risk may have changed over time, requiring more appropriate mitigation.

59. Even if a development’s vulnerability is not increasing, change of use can often present an opportunity to improve the flood resilience of existing development.

### Extensions

60. Small extensions (less than 250 sqm) are not subject to the sequential test.

61. However, an FRA is still required, proportional to the degree of risk and sufficient to demonstrate that the requirements of paragraph F7.1 and 2 of the NPPF have been satisfied.
