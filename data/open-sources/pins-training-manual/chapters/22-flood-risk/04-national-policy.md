# 22 Flood Risk: National policy

<!-- Part1 p.1080 -->

## National policy

62. The outline of the approach for individual developments in the NPPF is illustrated in the flowchart in Annex 1.

63. The general approach to decision-taking to flood risk is to:

- Assess flood risk

- Avoid flood risk by applying the Sequential and Exception Tests as appropriate, then

- Manage and mitigate any unavoidable risk, including by ensuring development is flood resilient and resistant, safe and will not increase flood risk overall

64. The PPG states that ‘even where a flood risk assessment shows the

### development can be made safe throughout its lifetime without increasing

risk elsewhere, the Sequential Test still needs to be satisfied’. No matter how much detail an appellant may provide to prove that a proposed development would be safe from flooding for its lifetime, if it is at flood risk you must apply the Sequential Test first.

### Outline of the Sequential Test

65. Paragraph F5.1 of the NPPF states that the aim of the Sequential Test is to steer new development to areas with the lowest risk of flooding from any source.

66. Para 024 of the PPG explains that the Sequential Test ensures that a sequential, risk-based approach is followed to steer new development to areas with the lowest risk of flooding, taking all sources of flood risk and climate change into account. Where it is not possible to locate development in low-risk areas, the Sequential Test should go on to compare reasonably available sites:

- within medium risk areas; and

- then, only where there are no reasonably available sites in low and medium risk areas, within high-risk areas.

67. Initially, the presence of existing flood risk management infrastructure should be ignored, as the long-term funding, maintenance and renewal of this infrastructure is uncertain. Climate change will also impact upon the level of protection infrastructure will offer throughout the lifetime of development.

68. The Sequential Test should then consider the spatial variation of risk within medium and then high flood risk areas to identify the lowest risk sites in these areas, ignoring the presence of flood risk management infrastructure.

<!-- Part1 p.1081 -->

69. It may then be appropriate to consider the role of flood risk management infrastructure in the variation of risk within high and medium flood risk areas. In doing so, information such as flood depth, velocity, hazard and speed-of-onset in the event of flood risk management infrastructure exceedance and/or failure, should be considered as appropriate. Information on the probability of flood defence failure is unsuitable for planning purposes given the substantial uncertainties involved in such long-term predictions.

70. Paragraph F5.3 of the NPPF states that the strategic FRA will provide the basis for applying this test. The guidance does not suggest augmenting this with more up-to-date datasets. In these circumstances, para 180 of the NPPF says that the exception test may need to be reapplied for allocated sites.

71. Para 174 of the NPPF also states that development should not be permitted if there are reasonably available sites appropriate for the proposed development in areas with a lower risk of flooding.

### Why is a Sequential Test required?

72. PPG (para 023) states that this means ‘avoiding, so far as possible, development in current and future medium and high risk areas considering all sources of flooding including areas at risk of surface water flooding’.

73. The reason given for this is that it is the most effective way of addressing flood risk because it places the least reliance on measures like flood defences, flood warnings and property level resilience features.

74. In addition, application of the sequential approach in the plan-making and decision-making process will help to ensure that development is steered to the lowest risk areas, where it is compatible with sustainable development objectives to do so, and developers do not waste resources promoting proposals which would fail to satisfy the test (PPG para 023).

75. The PPG (paras 023 and 029) explain that application of this approach means that developers do not waste resources promoting proposals which would fail to satisfy the test. It emphasises that early engagement on the Sequential Test should avoid other work being undertaken unnecessarily.

76. This is not merely an administrative exercise. The costs and consequences of flooding are significant. This policy recognises the uncertainty associated with flood risk modelling and climate change, including over long-periods, and concludes that, if possible, such risks should be avoided. The Chapter 18 objective of the NPPF states that such development is ‘inappropriate’. It is only when it has been demonstrated that avoidance is not possible that flood management and mitigation are considered.

<!-- Part1 p.1082 -->

### When is a Sequential Test required?

77. Paragraph F5.2 of the NPPF states that a sequential risk-based approach should be taken to individual applications in areas known to be at risk now or in the future from any form of flooding. PPG para 027 states that the Sequential Test should be applied to ‘Major’ and ‘Non-major’ development proposed in areas at risk of flooding. Paragraph F5.2 of the NPPF sets out exemptions from the Sequential Test, which includes most minor development.

78. Paragraph F5.2(b) of the NPPF states that a Sequential Test is not required where a site-specific FRA demonstrates that no built development within the site boundary, including access or escape routes, land raising or other potentially vulnerable elements, would be located on an area that would be at risk of flooding from any source, now and in the future.

79. PPG paragraph 027 states that a ‘proportional approach’ should be taken to application of paragraph F5.2(b) when considering surface water flood risk. Where a site-specific FRA demonstrates clearly that the proposed layout, design, and mitigation measures would ensure that occupiers and users would remain safe from current and future surface water flood risk for the lifetime of the development, without increasing flood risk elsewhere, then the Sequential Test need not be applied. Note that this applies to surface water flood risk only; it does not apply to other forms of flooding, including rivers and the sea.

80. Under paragraph F5.2(c) of the NPPF, certain types of development that are listed are also exempt from a Sequential Test (and an Exception Test). Don’t forget that these sites must still meet the requirements for site-specific FRAs though. These are:

- Householder development

- Small, non-residential extensions (with a footprint of less than 250 m2)

- Changes of use, except to a caravan, camping or chalet site, or to a mobile home or park. There is further information on this category in para 014 of the PPG.

81. Paragraph F5.2(a) of the NPPF also states that where sites have already been allocated in the development plan through a Sequential Test, applicants need not apply the test again. However, the Exception Test may need to be re-applied if more recent information about flood risk has come to light.

<!-- Part1 p.1083 -->

82. Developers may refer to earlier judgments, including ‘Wathen-Faye v SoS’1 in which the judge accepted that a Sequential Test was not necessary for an area at risk of groundwater flooding. However, these cases rest on previous versions of the guidance and the findings no longer apply. The guidance is now explicit that the Sequential Test applies to all sources of flood risk.

83. Although it is not explicitly stated in the PPG, the ST should be applied even when only part of a site is identified at risk of flooding, except where a site-specific FRA demonstrates that no built development within the site would be located on an area.

### Area of the Sequential Test

84. According to the PPG (para 027a), the area of the test will be governed by local circumstances relating to the catchment area for the type of development and the needs it is proposing to address. For example, for a non-major housing development it would not usually be appropriate for the area of search to extend beyond the specific area of a town or city in which the proposal is located, or beyond an individual village and its immediate neighbouring settlements. Overall, the sequential test should be applied proportionately, focusing on realistic alternatives in areas of lower flood risk that could meet the same development need.

85. If there is disagreement about the area of the test the guidance in the PPG will be highly relevant. This should not be constrained by land ownership and should be proportionate to the scale of development proposed. For major housing development this may extend across a town, housing market area or District. Arguments that development is needed to sustain the existing community should be carefully scrutinised before accepting that sites outside them would not provide reasonable alternatives

86. Para 029 of the PPG states that the planning authority will need to determine an appropriate area of search, based on the development type and relevant spatial policies. The applicant will then need to identify whether there are any other ‘reasonably available’ sites within the area of search, that have not already been identified by the planning authority in site allocations or relevant housing and/or economic land availability assessments, such as sites currently available on the open market. The applicant may also need to check on the current status of relevant sites to determine if they can be considered ‘reasonably available’.

[^1]: Wathen-Fayed v Secretary of State for Levelling Up, Housing and Communities Case No: CO/3847/2021 & Substation Action Save East Suffolk Ltd v Secretary of State for Energy Security and Net Zero: CA-2023- 000087

<!-- Part1 p.1084 -->

87. A pragmatic approach should be taken to the availability of alternatives. The examples given in the PPG are:

- a small extension to existing premises where it may be impractical accommodate the additional space in an alternative location.

- where there are large areas in Flood Zones 2 and 3 (e.g. coastal towns and settlements on major rivers) and development is needed in those areas to sustain the existing community, sites outside them are unlikely to provide reasonable alternatives

- It may also, in some cases, be relevant to consider whether large scale development could be split across a number of alternative sites at lower risk of flooding, but only where those alternative sites would be capable of accommodating the development in a way which would still serve its intended market(s) as effectively.

88. Sites should be considered ‘reasonably available’ for the purposes of the Sequential Test if their location is suitable for the type of development proposed, they are able to meet the same development needs and they have a reasonable prospect of being developed at the same time as the proposal.

89. The Mead Realisations Ltd judgment [2024] EWHC 279 (Admin) (paragraph 100) rejected a highly specific approach whereby alternative sites would need to accommodate the proposed development in its various particulars, commenting that such an approach would render the Sequential Test ineffective.

90. Mead (paragraph 110) also clarified that a ‘series of smaller sites’ connotes a relationship between sites appropriate for accommodating the type of development which the decision-maker judges should form the basis for the sequential assessment. This addresses the concern that a proposal should not automatically fail the sequential test because of the availability of multiple, disconnected sites across a local authority’s area. The issue is whether they have a relationship which makes them suitable in combination to accommodate any need or demand to which the decision-maker decides to attach weight.

91. ‘Point in time’ was also covered in Mead. The Court commented that comparison of availability between two sites involves matters of degree. It does not require precise alignment (paragraph 121 of the judgment).

92. An appellant may apply a capacity threshold to a long list of candidate sites – for example rejecting sites that would be more than 25% smaller than the appeal site. A ‘series of smaller sites’ is considered in Mead, which says that this wording connotes a relationship between sites appropriate for accommodating the type of development which the decision-maker judges should form the basis for the sequential assessment.

<!-- Part1 p.1085 -->

93. In considering whether alternative lower-risk sites would be capable of accommodating the proposed development, such alternative sites do not need to be owned by the applicant to be considered ‘reasonably available’.

94. The EA provides guidance on searching for alternative sites, and the provision of information about potential alternative sites, but not on the extent of the search area.

95. For the schedule of sites, a good tip is to ask for a summary table with the appellant’s comments and the Council’s comments in adjoining columns, much as you might do for HLS sites.

### Lack of Sequential Test and paragraph S1 and S3 of the NPPF

96. The PPG (para 004) states that ‘where the Sequential and the Exception Tests have been applied as necessary and not met, development should not be allowed’. In this eventuality, those findings should be balanced against other considerations in your planning balance.

97. Paragraph F5.1 of the NPPF states that development should not be permitted if there are reasonably available sites in areas with a lower risk of flooding. If there is no Sequential Test, it is highly unlikely that it can be concluded that there would be no alternative sites. It follows from this that the lack of a Sequential Test is potentially a reason for refusal.

98. The alternative could be perverse; a developer not undertaking the test could be at an advantage. In addition, the lack of Sequential Test could lead to avoidance of the Exception Test, which is predicated on the Sequential Test. If the Exception Test is failed, then the PPG is clear that development should be refused (para 063), which must be weighed against the other considerations in the planning balance.

99. The PPG states that ‘flood risk’ is key to the application of the presumption in favour of sustainable development in paragraph S1 and footnote 24 of the National Planning Policy Framework.

100.Para 027 of the PPG specifically states that the absence of a 5-year housing land supply is not a relevant consideration in applying the Sequential Test for individual applications. However, housing considerations, including housing land supply, may be relevant in the overall planning balance, alongside the outcome of the Sequential Test.

The need for the Exception Test will depend on the potential vulnerability of the site and of the development proposed, in line with the Flood Risk Vulnerability Classification set out in Annex F Table 2.

106.The NPPF and PPG (Notes to Table 2) confirm that the Exception Test does not need to be applied for the types of development set out in paragraph F5.2(c) of the NPPF.

107.Para 035 of the PPG states that where a development proposal is in accordance with an allocation made in a Plan following the application of the Sequential and Exception Tests, it should not be necessary to repeat aspects of the Exception Test unless:

- elements of the development that were key to it satisfying the Exception Test at the plan-making stage (such as wider sustainability benefits to the community or measures to reduce flood risk overall) have changed or are not included in the proposed development; or

- the understanding of current or future flood risk has changed significantly.

### Application of the Exception Test

108.The Exception Test only applies to fluvial and tidal flooding.

109.There are three parts to the Exception Test, as set out in paragraph F6.1(b) of the NPPF, all of which must be satisfied for the development to be permitted:

- Provide wider sustainability benefits to the community that outweigh the flood risk;

- Be safe for its anticipated lifetime in accordance with policy F7; and

- Not increase flood risk elsewhere and, where possible, reduce flood risk overall.

110.For further information on how to demonstrate a reduction in the causes and impacts of flooding, see paras 062 to 067 of the PPG.

111.Para 037 states that to satisfy the Exception Test without an overall reduction in flood risk, it would need to be demonstrated that such measures cannot be identified or are unfeasible.

112.For essential infrastructure in Flood Zone 3a, Table 2 of the PPG additionally requires that it is designed and constructed to remain operational and safe in times of flood.

113.For essential infrastructure in Flood Zone 3b, Table 2 of the PPG additionally requires that it is designed and constructed to remain operational and safe in times of flood, does not result in a net loss of floodplain storage, and does not impede water flows and increase flood risk elsewhere.

### Wider sustainability objectives

114.Para 031 of the PPG says that it may be possible to move onto the Exception Test when the Sequential Test has failed (so there are sites that would be at lower risk) where, accounting for wider sustainable development objectives, application of relevant local and national policies would provide a clear reason for refusing development in any alternative locations identified.

115.The PPG para 036 advises that Local Planning Authorities should set their own criteria for assessing wider sustainability objectives, based on the sustainability appraisal in the Local Plan. Such appraisals are typically wide ranging and may not be up to date. Furthermore, the examples given in the PPG do not form a closed list. Accordingly, you will need to reach a judgement on whether the stated sustainability benefits are relevant in this case and the weight to be attributed to them. The PPG advises that the impacts of flood risk on social, economic and environmental factors should be considered. Therefore, arguments based solely on housing numbers without wider benefits should be carefully scrutinised, and are unlikely, on their own, to outweigh the flood risk.

116.The examples given include re-use of brownfield land as part of a regeneration scheme, an overall reduction in flood risk to the wider community, or the provision of SUDS that significantly exceed the NPPF policy requirements.

117.The benefits still need to be balanced against the flood risks, informed by the site-specific risk assessment.

118.If benefits are absent or outweighed by the flood risk, the Exception Test will not have been satisfied, and the development is contrary to national policy. The NPPF is clear that this is a strong reason for refusal which should be weighed in the planning balance.

### Not increasing the flood risk elsewhere

119. The requirements of paragraph F7.1 and F7.2 of the NPPF must be applied to all sites, including those exempt from undertaking the Sequential Test.

120. Paragraph F7.2 of the NPPF requires that developers demonstrate that:

a) Within the site, the most vulnerable development is located in areas of lowest flood risk, unless there are overriding reasons which justify a different arrangement;

b) The development will be safe throughout its lifetime taking account of the vulnerability of its users;

c) Any residual risk can be safely managed, and safe access and escape routes are included where appropriate, as part of an agreed emergency plan;

<!-- Part1 p.1089 -->

d) The development is appropriately flood resistant and resilient such that, in the event of a flood, it could be quickly brought back into use without significant refurbishment; and

e) It can be demonstrated that flood risk will not be increased elsewhere.

121. Para 004 specifically states that ‘tensions between flood risk mitigation measures and other planning matters, do not justify unsafe development’.

### Resistant and resilient construction

122. The PPG references the British Standard as describing suitable measures that can be taken to avoid flood risk. However, this was withdrawn in 2025 and the PPG has not been updated. You may get reference to the 2015 standard or its replacement, which only references resilience. Inspectors should be alert to this when assessing the measures proposed’

123. Inspectors should consider whether proposals for flood resistant or resilient development meets the test of being able to quickly bring the development back into use without significant refurbishment (Paragraph F7.2(d) of the NPPF).

124. The PPG (para 068) says that passive measures should be prioritised over active measures as they are likely to be more effective and more reliable.

### Residual risk

125. Residual risk comes in two main forms:

- from flood risk management infrastructure. Examples given in the PPG include breach of a flood defence, failure of pumped drainage, failure of a reservoir or a flood event that exceeds the design standard

- risk to a development once site-specific flood mitigation measures are taken into account, including safe access and escape routes. Examples given are the depth of internal flooding predicted after raising of floor levels, the hazard to which people would be exposed on escape routes after they have been raised, and or failure in flood warning.

126. Para 041 of the PPG states that where flood risk management infrastructure is likely to be improved to keep pace with climate change, the potential consequences of flooding resulting from breach or failure of that improved infrastructure is likely to be the main driver for mitigation. Para 005 of the PPG suggests that, if relying on flood defences, the likelihood of them keeping pace with climate change will also inform the nature of the residual risk. Where infrastructure is unlikely to be improved, the potential consequences of flooding resulting from overtopping or a breach will also be an important consideration.

<!-- Part1 p.1090 -->

127. Para 042 says that is not appropriate to rely solely on emergency plans to mitigate residual risk. More information on this is provided in para 048.

### Emergency plans

128. Para 043 of the PPG states that emergency plans are needed wherever emergency flood response is an important component of making a development safe. Emergency plans will be essential for sites at risk of flooding used for holiday or short-let caravans and camping and for any site with transient occupancy (e.g. hostels and hotels). Further details of the requirements for emergency plans can be found in Para 044 to 047 of the PPG, including for reservoir failure.

129. Paragraph F7.2(c) of the NPPF specifically requires that safe access and escape routes are included as part of ‘an agreed emergency plan’. It is not clear from the wording in the NPPF whether this means such plans should be provided as part of the application, rather than something that can be left to a condition, as has previously been done. In absence of further detail in the PPG, Inspectors should consider any submissions on this point and should assess whether in principle a plan could be agreed.

### Flood resistance and flood resilience

130. Flood resilience measures (also called recoverability measures, or wet-proofing), accept that water will enter the building, but through careful design and changes to the construction will minimise damage and allow faster cleaning, drying, repairing and re-occupancy of the building after a flood.

131. Flood resilience measures can use a combination of flood resistance and recovery measures. The industry-developed CIRIA Property Flood Resilience Code of Practice provides advice for both new-build and retrofit.

132. Flood resistance measures, or dry-proofing, stop water entering a building up to a safe structural limit. Resistance measures can be passive, such as flood doors which are normally closed; or active, such as air brick covers or removable flood barriers.

133. Further information on flood resistance and resilience can be found in PPG paras 068 to 069.

### Sustainable drainage systems

134. Sustainable drainage systems (SuDS) are an explicit requirement for major development and development in flood risk areas, unless there is clear evidence that this would be inappropriate (Paragraph F8 of the NPPF).

<!-- Part1 p.1091 -->

135. SuDS are designed to control surface water run off close to where it falls, combining a mixture of built and nature-based techniques to mimic natural drainage as closely as possible, and accounting for the predicted impacts of climate change. Many types of sustainable drainage systems are possible, contributing to reducing the causes and impacts of flooding.

136. The layout and function of drainage systems needs to be considered at the start of the design process for new development, as integration with road networks and other infrastructure can maximise the availability of developable land.

137. You should be satisfied that the minimum standards of operation for the proposed sustainable drainage system are appropriate, and that there are clear maintenance and adoption arrangements in place for the lifetime of the development.

138. Further information on SuDS, including the information required, can be found in PPG para 055 to 061.
