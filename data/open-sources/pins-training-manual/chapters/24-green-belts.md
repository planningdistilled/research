## Introduction

1. All the legal cases referred to pre-date the National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF; “the Framework”) of August 2026. However, they have been included because they remain relevant.

## National policy

2. Be aware that in order to help show that national policy has been correctly applied, you should always use the terminology in the NPPF in your decisions and reports. Do not substitute alternative words or phrases. In particular, avoid using the term ‘appropriate development’. It is best to describe proposals as being ‘inappropriate’ or ‘not inappropriate’.

3. Further advice is also given in the government’s Planning Practice Guidance.

## The NPPF, the development plan and Metropolitan Open Land

4. In dealing with Green Belt casework the policies in the NPPF are a material consideration (NPPF Introduction paragraph 3). However, the starting point is that appeals should be determined in accordance with the development plan unless other material considerations indicate otherwise1. Where development plan policies dealing with the Green Belt significantly pre-date the NPPF they might be based on Planning Policy Guidance 2 Green Belts (DETR, 1995) (PPG2) or the original (2012) NPPF.

5. You may need to consider whether the relevant development plan policies are different from those in the NPPF and, if so, what weight should be given to them. This will depend on the degree of consistency with the NPPF. Policies which are materially inconsistent with the NPPF should be given very limited weight (NPPF Annex A Paragraph 2).

6. This might be especially important in deciding the basis on which you will consider whether a proposal is inappropriate development. You should, however, bear in mind that PM6 of the NPPF indicates that local plans should avoid unnecessary duplication including of the policies within the NPPF. However, through the examination process there may have been particular circumstances that justified a local policy that was not the same as national policy. The position will vary depending on the age of the policy and any supporting evidence provided such as the examining Inspector’s report. Policies that follow the broad approach in the NPPF should not be regarded in the same way as those that are directly contrary to it. Policies might not be the same as the NPPF but still consistent with it.

[^1]: Section 38(6) Planning and Compulsory Purchase Act 2004 as amended (“the PCPA”)

## General approach

7. In order to structure your decision in a logical way and to comply with the NPPF, the following general approach is recommended.

1. Is the development inappropriate? Is it grey belt land? How should the effects on openness and Green Belt purposes be considered?

2. Would there be any other harm (this refers to non-Green Belt factors, for example to character and appearance), that weigh against the development or any other main issues that need to be addressed?

3. If the development is inappropriate, are there any other considerations advanced in support of it? How much weight in favour of it do they attract?

4. If any positive other considerations exist, do they clearly outweigh the harm to the Green Belt, and any other harm? (this is the ‘Green Belt balancing exercise’).

5. If other considerations clearly outweigh the harm, do very special circumstances exist?

6. Reach clear conclusions on your main issues with reference to relevant development plan policies and the NPPF as necessary and make a final conclusion under S38(6).

8. These steps are set out in the flow diagram in Annex 1 and are considered in more detail below.

## Defining main issues

9. Your definition of the main issues should reflect the general approach set out above and described in more detail below. For example (although these can be adjusted depending on the circumstances of the appeal):

1. Whether the proposal would be inappropriate development in the Green Belt [having regard to the National Planning Policy Framework and relevant development plan policies].

2. The effect of the proposal on the openness and purposes of the Green Belt.

3. The effect of the proposal on [insert any main issues relating to non-Green Belt matters]].

4. Whether any harm by reason of inappropriateness, and any other harm, would be clearly outweighed by other considerations, so as to amount to the very special circumstances required to justify the proposal OR Whether other considerations clearly outweigh the harm to the Green Belt and any other harm so as to amount to very special circumstances.

## Step 1a: Is the development inappropriate?

10. You will firstly need to decide what type of development you are dealing with and assess it against relevant development plan policies, and the NPPF (GB6, GB7 and

<!-- Part2 p.86 -->

GB8). Are the development plan policies consistent with the NPPF? (see above) If the inconsistency is significant, the critical judgement is likely to be whether the proposal falls into one of the exceptions to inappropriate development in the Green Belt set out in GB7 of the NPPF.

11. Further advice about particular development types is provided later in this chapter.

12. If the proposed development would fall within any of those exceptions and is therefore not inappropriate development, there is no need to consider it against any of the other exceptions – even if they might be applicable and have been raised by the parties. However, where there are arguments about which exception should apply and you are finding that the proposal would be inappropriate development it is likely to be necessary to consider all of those cited or which are clearly relevant.

13. In some cases, the proposal may contain different elements which do not precisely fall into any of the exception categories. For example, the re-use of an existing building and the redevelopment of previously developed land. In those instances, it is advisable to consider the proposal against all relevant exceptions that apply rather than to find that it would be inappropriate development when judged against a single category. This will particularly be the case if the various elements of the proposal could, in theory, be proposed separately. Therefore, in the example above, you would assess the proposal against both GB7(1)(b) and GB7(1)(e).

14. If you consider that the development is not inappropriate:

- Deal with the proposal as you would for any other s78 or s174 ground (a) appeal.

- Do not carry out steps 3 (other considerations), 4 (the Green Belt balancing exercise) or 5 (‘very special circumstances’). See below for advice on dealing with ‘openness’ (step 1b).

- Address any other alleged non-Green Belt harm (for example, to character and appearance) in the usual way. A finding that a development is not inappropriate does not automatically mean that it is acceptable in terms of other planning issues (step 2).

### Inappropriateness by reason of effects on openness

15. The NPPF (chapter 13) states that the objective of Green Belt policy is to “prevent urban sprawl by keeping land permanently open. The government attaches great importance to Green Belts, the essential features of which are their openness and permanence.”

16. Openness may be a consideration in identifying exceptions to inappropriate development. Certain exceptions within GB7 of the NPPF require the decision maker to first assess the effect of the development on openness. It may also be a matter that requires consideration for proposals that are found to be inappropriate development but do not require this initial assessment.

<!-- Part2 p.87 -->

### What is openness?

17. The PPG on Green Belt sets out factors that can be taken into account when considering the potential impact of development on the openness of the Green Belt (Reference ID: 64-013-20250225). These include but are not limited to visual and spatial aspects, duration and remediability and degree of activity. Any such assessment requires a judgment based on the circumstances of the case and the courts have identified a number of such matters.

Spatial and visual aspects

18. The Court of Appeal in Turner v SSCLG & East Dorset Council [2016] EWCA Civ 466 confirmed that the openness of the Green Belt has a spatial aspect as well as a visual aspect. This means that the absence of visual intrusion does not in itself mean that there is no impact on the openness of the Green Belt as a result. But equally this does not mean that the openness of the Green Belt has no visual dimension (paragraph 25).

19. The Supreme Court in R (on the application of Samuel Smith Old Brewery (Tadcaster) and others) (Respondents) v North Yorkshire County Council (Appellant) [2020] UKSC 3 endorsed paragraph 14 of Turner to the effect that the word openness is open textured and a number of factors are capable of being relevant when it comes to applying it to the particular facts of a specific case. However, how to take account of the visual effects is a matter of planning judgement rather than one of legal principle (paragraph 26). In this case it was concluded that there was no error of law in the officer report as there is no express or implied requirement to refer to visual impact. The Supreme Court also highlighted that openness is the counterpart of urban sprawl and that it does not imply freedom from any [emphasis added] form of development. Furthermore, the visual qualities of the land may be an aspect of the planning judgement in applying this broad policy concept (paragraph 22).

20. In conclusion the Supreme Court confirmed that “the matters relevant to openness in any particular case are a matter of planning judgement, not law” (paragraph 39). So whilst visual impact can be relevant to openness it is not necessarily relevant in every case. Nevertheless, Inspectors are best advised to have regard to potential visual impacts rather than simply to ignore or not refer to them at all.

21. The Turner judgment also clarified that “The visual dimension of the openness of the Green Belt does not exhaust all relevant planning factors relating to visual impact when a proposal for development in the Green Belt comes up for consideration” (paragraph 16). This means that it is possible that a development which would harm openness could be acceptable visually and vice versa. Therefore, it is advisable that you clearly separate out your assessment of any effects on openness from any assessment of effects on character and appearance.

Duration and activity

22. The effect on openness might not be confined solely to permanent physical works. For example, cars in a car park, boats in a marina and play equipment in a garden might all have some effect on openness. The extent of the effect on openness may vary depending on the extent of any car parking or mooring of boats and the frequency.

<!-- Part2 p.88 -->

### The effect on openness and purposes

23. Paragraphs GB7(1)(e) and GB7(1)(f), contain distinct tests relating to openness, in determining whether the proposal should be categorised as inappropriate development. Paragraph GB7(1)(e) refers to not causing substantial harm to openness. Paragraph GB7(1)(f) requires the impact on the openness of the Green Belt to be minimised and there to be no significant conflict with the Green Belt purposes. Paragraph GB7(1)(g)(i) refers to development undermining the purposes (taken together) of the remaining Green Belt land across the area of the plan, where the development would utilise grey belt land. These tests need to be applied to determine whether a proposal should be categorised as inappropriate development. In so doing, regard should be had to the aspects of openness outlined above. Further guidance on assessing development under GB7(1)(g) – Grey Belt – is set out below.

24. Whilst in reference to the tests in the previous NPPF, the concepts of the following are helpful in assessing the effect on openness. In Samuel Smith Old Brewery (Tadcaster) & Oxton Farm v North Yorkshire CC & Darrington Quarries Ltd [2018] EWCA Civ 489 it was acknowledged that some forms of development, including mineral extraction, may in principle be appropriate, and compatible with the concept of openness. Similarly in Euro Garages Ltd v SSCLG & Anor [2018] EWHC 1753 (Admin) the judge indicated at paragraph 42 that rather than treating any change as having a greater impact on openness of the Green Belt, the correct approach is to consider the impact or harm, if any, wrought by the change. Whether or not any change will have an adverse impact, and so cause harm to openness, might depend on factors such as the scale of the development, its locational context, and its spatial and/or visual implications (paragraph 32).

## Step 1b: Should effects on openness or purposes be considered further?

### If the development is ‘not inappropriate’

25. In Lee Valley Regional Park Authority, R (on the application of) v Epping Forest District Council & Anor (Rev 1) [2016] EWCA Civ 404, the Court of Appeal endorsed the conclusion of Dove J in the High Court. Where development is found to be ‘not inappropriate’, applying paragraphs 89 or 90 of the original NPPF, it should not be regarded as harmful either to the openness of the Green Belt or to the purposes of including land in the Green Belt (see para 17 of judgment).

“Development that is not, in principle, "inappropriate" in the Green Belt is, as Dove

J. said in paragraph 62 of his judgment, development "appropriate to the Green Belt". On a sensible contextual reading of the policies in paragraphs 79 to 92 of the NPPF, development appropriate in – and to – the Green Belt is regarded by the Government as not inimical to the "fundamental aim" of Green Belt policy "to prevent urban sprawl by keeping land permanently open", or to "the essential characteristics of Green Belts", namely "their openness and their permanence" (paragraph 79 of the NPPF), or to the "five purposes" served by the Green Belt (paragraph 80). This is the real significance of a development being appropriate in the Green Belt, and the reason why it does not have to be justified by "very special circumstances".” (Paragraph 24)

26. Impact on openness is therefore implicitly taken into account in the exceptions unless there is a specific requirement to consider the actual effect on openness. As a result, for those exceptions within GB7 where the effect of the development on

<!-- Part2 p.89 -->

openness is not expressly stated as a determinative factor in gauging inappropriateness, there is no requirement to assess the impact of the development on the openness of the Green Belt if the proposal would not be inappropriate development.

27. Indeed, the judgment makes it clear that there is no place for a subsequent assessment of the effect of the development on Green Belt openness if it has been found to be not inappropriate.

“….. the fact that an assessment of openness is "a gateway in some cases to identification of appropriateness" in NPPF policy indicates that "once a particular development is found to be, in principle, appropriate, the question of the impact of the building on openness is no longer an issue" ” (Paragraph 20)

28. Likewise, the effect of the development on Green Belt purposes is implicitly taken into account unless there is a specific requirement to consider it in the exceptions. Consequently, unless expressly stated as a as a determinative factor in gauging inappropriateness, there is no requirement to assess the impact of the development on Green Belt purposes if the proposal would not be inappropriate development.

29. However, a finding that a development is ‘not inappropriate’ does not automatically mean that it is acceptable in terms of other planning issues.

“That is not to say, of course, that proposals for the erection of agricultural buildings in the Green Belt will escape other policies in the NPPF, and in the development plan, including policies directed to the visual effects of development and the protection of the countryside or the character of the landscape. Policies of this kind will bear not only on proposals for development that is inappropriate in the Green Belt but also on proposals for development that is appropriate.” (Paragraph 26)

30. Furthermore, Policy S5(5) of the NPPF states that “[…] where development would not be inappropriate in these locations (through the application of policies HC8 and GB7), proposals should be approved unless the benefits of doing so would be substantially outweighed by any adverse effects, when assessed against the national decision-making policies in this Framework, and applying paragraph 2 of this policy.” This essentially applies a titled balance in favour of granting permission.

### If the development is ‘inappropriate’

31. If the development is ‘inappropriate’ you should go on to explain what the effect would be on openness (if not explicitly considered already because the effect on openness is an integral part of considering whether a development type is inappropriate), and if necessary, what the effect would be on the purposes.

32. In many, but not necessarily all, cases the effect on openness and/or purposes could be harmful. GB6 of the NPPF indicates that substantial weight should be given to the harm to the Green Belt (by reason of inappropriateness) which would be caused, including harm to its openness (it therefore distinguishes between weight and harm). The Court of Appeal judgment in SSCLG & Others v Redhill Aerodrome Ltd [2014] EWCA Civ 1386 confirmed that the interpretation given to “any other harm” in

<!-- Part2 p.90 -->

paragraph 88 of the original NPPF (now GB6(2)) is such that it is not restricted to harm to the Green Belt.

33. Consequently, if you find that there would be harm to the Green Belt, it will inevitably carry (at least) substantial weight. However, it is good practice to quantify the degree of any harm to openness and the purposes of including land in the Green Belt (where relevant). But in doing so, avoid attributing weight individually to these factors – instead your finding about weight should relate to the totality of any Green Belt harm. A finding of ‘no harm’ or ‘no effect’ would be a neutral factor.

## Step 2: Would there be any non-Green Belt harm?

34. If there would be an adverse effect, it is helpful to explain the degree of harm – for example ‘significant’ or ‘moderate’. This will help demonstrate that you have carried out the Green Belt balancing exercise correctly.

35. A finding of ‘limited’ harm would not weigh very heavily against a proposal. But in assessing the totality of harm in Step 4 there will be a balancing exercise that takes into account all such harms.2 A finding of ‘no harm’ would be a neutral factor which would not weigh for or against the proposal.

36. However, if the proposal involves consideration of a heritage asset, then even if you were to find that any harm to the heritage asset has been outweighed by other benefits, that does not constitute a finding of ‘no harm’ (and thus a neutral factor) for Green Belt purposes. Instead, it would remain a harmful impact which has to be weighed in the balance when applying the very special circumstances test below.

## Step 3: If the development is inappropriate, are there any other considerations which weigh in favour of it?

37. Even though they may also be ‘material considerations’, it is best to use the terminology given in the NPPF and so referred to as ‘other considerations’. There is no restriction on what might be considered as an ‘other consideration’.

38. Paragraph 68 of Brentwood BC v SSE [1996] 72 P&CR 61 stated that, “the decision maker is required to look for factors having the character or quality that they lie in the balance against harm. …Those factors can vary widely. They can be green belt factors as such; for example, that the development may preserve or increase openness or contribute to green belt functions. They can be other planning factors, such as, perhaps, a building of exceptional architectural quality. They can be factors derived from national or other economic needs. They can be factors relating to personal circumstances. The list is endless and it would not be for the court to restrict it.”

39. Arguments which you might encounter include:

- personal circumstances (eg relating to accommodation, health, education, family life)

[^2]: The Court of Appeal judgment in SSCLG & Others v Redhill Aerodrome Ltd [2014] EWCA Civ 1386 confirmed that harms, even if less than the thresholds for refusal set out in the original NPPF, are “material considerations” for the purposes of deciding whether to grant planning permission. This position is the same both outside the Green Belt and within the Green Belt, save that the very special circumstances test applies if the proposal is for inappropriate development in the Green Belt (paragraph 32).

<!-- Part2 p.91 -->

- the existence of a fallback position - for example, permitted development rights or an extant planning permission3 (see ‘Approach to Decision-Making’ for advice)

- visual or environmental improvements - for example, the removal of existing buildings might be argued to improve appearance and/or increase openness (see below for further advice on how to deal with arguments relating to openness)

- economic benefits (for example sustaining or expanding an existing business or creating jobs)

- meeting a need for a particular type of development (for example, a rural worker’s dwelling, tourist accommodation, housing, telecommunications equipment etc)

- the lack of a suitable site for the development outside the Green Belt (if so, has it been demonstrated that the proposal needs to be located in the Green Belt or that it would not be feasible to find a suitable site elsewhere?)

- enhancing the beneficial use of the Green Belt, for example by improving access, providing opportunities for outdoor sport and recreation etc4

- enabling the restoration of a listed building

41. If benefits have been advanced, you might need to consider whether the scale of the proposed development is the minimum necessary to achieve the benefit. This might affect the weight you can attach to a benefit.5

42. It is helpful to explain what weight or importance you attach to these ‘other considerations.’ This is a matter of planning judgement. Terms you could use include: ‘minimal’, ‘limited’, ‘significant’ or ‘considerable’. This will help with the balancing exercise although such terminology does not have to be used each and every time. Consideration may also need to be given to the weight indicated to certain matters in the NPPF which should generally be reflected in the decision.

43. It is also vital that other considerations are treated separately and discretely and are not referred to as very special circumstances in themselves. There is also no requirement for them to be ‘very special’ or to compare them to the harm identified by means of a mini-balance as you go through them. Rather deal with each one in turn and make clear the importance you attach to each individual consideration.

An absence of harm or a reduced level of harm should be treated as such and should not be counted as positive considerations in support of the scheme. ## Step 4: If other considerations exist, do they clearly outweigh the harm to the Green Belt, and any other harm?

46. Carry out the Green Belt balancing exercise. Balance the combined weight of any other considerations against the totality of the harm (both Green Belt and other). Does the weight of the other considerations clearly outweigh the totality of the harm by reason of inappropriateness and any other harm resulting from the proposed development? There is no ‘formula’ for doing this. The balancing is one of judgement but ensure that the decision properly reflects the policy test that other considerations should “clearly outweigh”.

## Step 5: Do very special circumstances exist?

47. If the other considerations do not clearly outweigh the totality of the harm, very special circumstances cannot exist (GB6 of NPPF) and the appeal should be dismissed.

48. If the weight of the other considerations clearly outweighs the totality of the harm, it is likely that very special circumstances exist. This would be likely to lead to the appeal being allowed.

49. Before reaching this conclusion, do a common sense check. Do the factors in support of the proposal clearly outweigh the harm? It is not sufficient for them to merely outweigh. In other words, for the appeal to succeed, the overall balance would have to favour the appellants’ case, not just marginally, but decisively. The NPPF states that substantial weight should be given to any harm to the Green Belt and the Government attaches great importance to Green Belts. Does the reasoning clearly and logically lead to a conclusion?

50. Other considerations do not have to be rare or uncommon to be special. However, rarity may be a relevant consideration. In Wychavon v SSCLG & Butler [2008] EWCA Civ 692 the Court of Appeal found that the High Court judge was wrong:

“…. to treat the words "very special" in the paragraph 3.2 of the guidance as simply the converse of "commonplace". Rarity may of course contribute to the "special" quality of a particular factor, but it is not essential, as a matter of ordinary language or policy. The word "special" in the guidance connotes not a quantitative test, but a qualitative judgment as to the weight to be given to the particular factor for planning purposes. Thus, for example, respect for the home is in one sense a "commonplace", in that it reflects an aspiration shared by most of humanity. But it is at the same time sufficiently

The circumstances do not have to be unique, and the possibility that similar circumstances might arise elsewhere does not prevent a finding of very special circumstances in any particular case.

52. In Sefton Metropolitan Borough Council v SSHCLG & Jerry Doherty [2021] EWHC 1082 (Admin) it was alleged that the Inspector should have applied substantial weight to each of the Green Belt harms identified but it was found that there was no error in not attaching separate substantial weight to each element of harm (paragraph 61). This might include harm by reason of inappropriate development or the effect on Green Belt openness or purposes. There is therefore no need to attach substantial weight to each of those harms individually. The judge also said (paragraph 34) that:

“When paragraphs 143 and 144 are read together they can be seen as explaining that very special circumstances are needed before inappropriate development in the Green Belt can be permitted. In setting out that explanation they emphasise the seriousness of harm to the Green Belt in order to ensure that the decision maker understands and has in mind the nature of the very special circumstances requirement. They require the decision maker to have real regard to the importance of the Green Belt and the seriousness of any harm to it. They do not, however, require a particular mathematical exercise nor do they require substantial weight to be allocated to each element of harm as a mathematical exercise with each tranche of substantial weight then to be added to a balance. The exercise of planning judgement is not to be an artificially sequenced two stage process but a single exercise of judgement to assess whether there are very special circumstances which justify the grant of permission notwithstanding the particular importance of the Green Belt.”

53. In undertaking the Green Belt balancing exercise, it is important to show that all the harms and benefits have been considered. The weight or importance attached to them should be consistent with that identified within the main body of the decision. Where the amount of harm is significant and the other considerations in favour of the proposal are also significant, it will make the decision more convincing and credible if this is acknowledged. It will assist the losing party to understand which factors in particular caused the outcome to go against them. However, it is not necessary to undertake a hypothetical balancing exercise assuming that the considerations at play were different in some way. This should generally be avoided.

54. A possible outcome of the balancing exercise is that you find that there are very special circumstances. It is a conclusion you reach after the balancing exercise and so should only feature towards the end of your reasoning.

55. Do not return to any other matters or other considerations after this conclusion. It is possible to have separate sections of your decision dealing with other considerations and other matters. The former would include considerations advanced in support of the proposal. The latter would typically include any alleged harm which you have not

- state that it is the very special circumstances that outweigh/don’t outweigh the harm (it is the other considerations)

## What if the parties have agreed that the proposed development would be inappropriate?

61. Sometimes the main parties will agree that a proposal would be inappropriate development. If you reach the same conclusion, you will not need to deal with this as a separate main issue, subject to dealing with any third party views to the contrary. However, you should briefly explain the position early in your decision before setting out the main issues. For example:

The proposal would be inappropriate development in the Green Belt according to the National Planning Policy Framework [and the development plan]. Consequently, the main issues are: […]

## What if the parties have not raised the question of inappropriateness?

62. Sometimes, although the site is in the Green Belt, the question of inappropriateness may not have been raised. If it is an important issue (perhaps because it is clear that the proposal might be inappropriate development), you should seek the views of the parties.

63. Alternatively, it might be clear to you that the proposal would not be inappropriate or that the location in the Green Belt is immaterial to your consideration of the appeal (for example, this might be the case where the appeal is against a condition which would not have any implications for openness). If those circumstances it might be prudent to confirm this but it is not essential if the location of the site in the Green Belt is essentially a non-issue.

### Reuse, extension, alteration or replacement of buildings – GB7(1)(b)

69. Proposals relating to the reuse of a building may include any associated uses of land of minor operations such as external storage, garden areas, hardstanding or car parking boundary walling or fencing. 70. In Smith v SSCLG [2017] EWHC 2562 (Admin) the judgment confirmed that there was no legal error by the Inspector in concluding that proposed fencing, bin storage, car parking space and domestic paraphernalia would fail to preserve openness.

The Judge also found that there is no need to identify a particular large built-up area in deciding whether there would be urban sprawl.

72. The extension or alteration of a building is not inappropriate development provided that it does not result in a disproportionate increase in size compared to the original building. In settling this it will be important to firstly establish the size of the original building before deciding whether the proposal would, when taken in combination with any previous additions to the original building, result in a disproportionate increase in terms of its size. In other words, when taken together, would the sum total of existing and proposed extensions to the original building be disproportionate in size? It may therefore follow that a small extension could represent a cumulative disproportionate addition if the building has previously been extended.

73. There are different ways of assessing and measuring proportionality. Development plans and SPDs may contain specific limits in terms of floorspace and/or volume. However, the NPPF refers to size. Consequently, you should primarily look at the overall increase in terms of volume, floorspace, footprint and external dimensions. This exercise should not consider any effect on openness. 75. NPPF Footnote 40 defines the term ‘original building’ as:

‘The building as it existed on 1 July 1948 or, if constructed after 1 July 1948, as it was built originally’.

76. Therefore, extensions which were added to a building before 1 July 1948 should be regarded as part of the ‘original building’. Where an extension to a building constructed after 1 July 1948 is proposed, the comparison will be between the building as first built and the building as proposed to be extended, together with any existing extensions constructed since the building was first built.

77. The proposal may be to extend a building which replaced a previous building (most commonly a replacement dwelling). In relation to buildings constructed after 1 July 1948, the definition of ‘original building’ in footnote 40 of the NPPF does not expressly deal with replacements. For the purposes of the NPPF it would be reasonable to conclude that the ‘original building’ in such a case would be the replacement dwelling itself, as originally built, and that should form the baseline against which subsequent extensions and alterations should be measured. However, the development plan may contain more detailed policies relating to replacement dwellings in the Green Belt, which can be given weight depending on their degree of consistency with the NPPF.

### Limited infilling in villages lying within the Green Belt - GB7(1)(c)

90. Relevant considerations are:

- Is the site previously developed land? (see the definition in Annex B to the NPPF)

In addition to minerals extraction and waste disposal by landfill, the exclusion relating to land subject to restoration now also encompasses renewable and low carbon energy development where provision for restoration exists. There is also a reference here and at the start of the definition to land which has been lawfully developed. ## Other forms of development - GB7(1)(f)

94. Other forms of development are not inappropriate provided the impact on the openness of the Green Belt is minimised and there would not be a significant conflict with the Green Belt purposes. These are:

ii. Development brought forward under a Community Right to Build Order or Neighbourhood Development Order

101. All of these criteria would need to be met in order for the proposal to qualify as not inappropriate development:

- Criterion i. is a matter of judgement. In reaching that judgement, Inspectors should have regard to the Planning Practice Guidance on Green Belt. The assessment relating to - whether development on grey belt land would fundamentally undermine the purposes (taken together) of the remaining Green Belt across the area of the plan - is not confined to the effect of development on the site or its immediate surroundings, nor to a single Green Belt purpose in isolation, but requires an evaluative judgement of whether the development would affect the ability of the remaining Green Belt across the plan area to serve all five Green Belt purposes in a meaningful way.

- Criterion ii. should be established on the basis of the available evidence. The NPPF provides information on what evidenced unmet need may look like in different circumstances. For housing, this is including, but not limited to, development proposals involving the provision of housing where the LPA cannot demonstrate a 5 year supply of deliverable housing sites or scores below 75% in the most recent Housing Delivery Test. Note that Annex D paragraph 9 requires an appropriate buffer to be added to the housing requirement in every case. For economic development, Policy E2: Paragraph 2 sets out considerations relating to a) market signals and b) specific locational requirements – note that parts a) and b) have ‘or’ between, so meeting only one can demonstrate evidenced unmet need. Note that Annex D paragraph 9 requires an appropriate buffer to be added to the housing requirement in every case. Proposals for replacement dwellings will often raise particular issues in relation to criterion ii, as they do not ordinarily contribute to housing supply and may not be driven by an identified unmet need. In such cases, a shortfall in five-year housing land supply will not, of itself, demonstrate compliance with GB7(1)(g) where no net increase in dwellings is proposed.

<!-- Part2 p.103 -->

- Criterion iii. should be addressed in a straightforward way and a judgement made as to whether the development would be in a sustainable location, with particular reference to Policy TR3 - Locating development in sustainable locations. A broader review of whether the proposal would be sustainable development would not be appropriate, and the focus of this assessment should be its location. Additionally, not all the content of that policy will be relevant and neither does “particular reference” imply that other considerations should be omitted.

- Criterion iv. only applies to “major development involving the provision of housing”. Major development is defined in Annex B of the NPPF.

102. None of the criteria relating to grey belt land are concerned with openness. Therefore, this should not be a consideration in determining whether proposals are inappropriate development or not.

## Proximity to a well-connected station - GB7(1)(h)

103. The 2026 Framework has introduced this exception for residential or mixed-use development which would:

i. Be within reasonable walking distance of a well-connected station

ii. Be physically well-related to the station or the settlement within which the station is located;

iii. Be of a scale which can be accommodated taking into account the existing or proposed availability of infrastructure

iv. Not prejudice any proposals for long-term comprehensive development in the same location; and

v. In the case of proposals for major development, comply with policy GB8.

- A well-connected station is defined in Annex B as:

‘Railway stations and underground, tram and light rail stops located within a top 80 Travel to Work Area located partially or fully within England by Gross Value Added (GVA) and which, in the normal weekday timetable, are served (or have a reasonable prospect of being served due to planned upgrades or through agreement with the rail operator) throughout the daytime by at least four trains or trams per hour overall, or at least two trains or trams per hour in any one direction’.

- Footnote 72 to the NPPF explains that, for the purposes of determining whether a location falls within a qualifying Travel to Work Area, the 2023 Gross Value Added (GVA) dataset published by the Office for National Statistics should be used. This dataset remains fixed until the publication of the 2028 GVA data, after which the applicable dataset will be updated on a five-yearly basis.

104. All of these criteria would need to be met in order for the proposal to qualify as not inappropriate development.

105. Criterion i. requires an assessment of reasonable walking distance. Reasonable walking distance is defined in the Glossary in Annex B of the NPPF around 800 metres, or around 10 minutes’ walk time if topography, route availability and quality or physical barriers would prevent or discourage walking from up to 800 metres away […] Where […] only part of the site falls within the reasonable walking distance, GB7(1)(h)(i) only apply to the area of the site within that reasonable walking distance.

<!-- Part2 p.104 -->

106. You will need to take account of the distance, not just a circle on a plan, accounting for the elements that may prevent or discourage walking. It will be a common sense approach, taking account of the specific site circumstances and your own observations on site. Ultimately, it is a matter of planning judgement applying the 800m / 10 minutes’ walk as the baseline.

107. Criterion ii. requires the development to be physically well-related to the station or the settlement within which the station is located. This will be a matter of planning judgment, considering the evidence before you. However, note that there is an ‘or’ between station and settlement, so the development could be physically well-related to the station, but not the settlement, and this would meet this criterion test, and vice versa.

108. Criterion iii. requires the development to be of a scale which can be accommodated taking into account the existing or proposed availability of infrastructure. This would require an assessment of the scale in relation to existing or proposed infrastructure, rather than an assessment of the scale of the proposal as a character and appearance consideration, which would be a separate main issue if necessary.

109. Criterion iv. requires the development to not prejudice any proposals for long-term comprehensive development in the same location. This assessment would rely on the evidence before you, in terms of development allocations in existing or emerging development plans, and the weight to which you can give any emerging policies.

110. Criterion v. requires compliance with The Golden Rules (GB8) where it is for major development.

## Golden Rules – Policy GB8

111. The Golden Rules apply to all “major development” (as defined in the NPPF) involving the provision of housing on sites in the Green Belt. This includes all development, not just those meeting the exceptions in Policy GB7(1)(g) or (h). It will be a matter to assess based on the circumstances of the case as to whether all the contributions by Policy GB8(1)(a), (b) and (c) have been met.

112. As set out above, if the proposal is being considered under GB7(1)(g) or (h), and the Golden Rules are not met, then the proposal would be inappropriate development. However, if the proposal is being considered under one of the other criteria under GB7 (for example, it is previously developed land and meets GB7(1)(e), then a failure to meet the Golden Rules under GB8 would weigh against the proposal as a material consideration, but it would not mean the proposal reverted to being inappropriate development. The weight to be given to this failure would be a matter for the Inspector.

113. Policy GB8(2) states that substantial weight should be given to the importance of complying with the Golden Rules. This only relates to development within the Green Belt. Adherence to them will be a material consideration to be taken into account in any final balance in cases where the proposal is either inappropriate development or not inappropriate development.

114. Policy GB8(3) introduces 3 circumstances in which a viability assessment demonstrating that it would not be possible for a development to comply with the Golden Rules may be submitted as part of a development proposal. Policy GB8(4) then seeks to ensure that development proposals should still make the maximum

<!-- Part2 p.105 -->

possible contribution to affordable housing and other infrastructure requirements, and where contributions are significantly below those set out at GB8(1)(a)(i) or (a)(ii), they will only be acceptable in exceptional cases where fully evidenced and justified. This will be a planning judgement on the evidence before you.

115. Footnote 43 to GB8 also specifies that the Golden Rules do not apply to:

(a) developments brought forward on land released from the Green Belt through plans adopted prior to 12 December 2024;

(b) developments that were granted planning permission on Green Belt land prior to 12 December 2024;

or (c) Traveller sites.

## Curtilage buildings

116. The NPPF does not make any specific reference to ancillary outbuildings within the curtilage of a dwelling or other buildings. Therefore, if a new curtilage building is proposed, you will need to decide if it would fall within any of the exceptions in GB7. If not, it would be inappropriate development.

117. Given that outbuildings are buildings, GB7(1)(b) logically applies to any proposal to extend an outbuilding (i.e. an extension or alteration of a building would be not inappropriate provided that it does not result in a disproportionate addition). Furthermore, providing that the new building would be in the same use, proposals to replace an existing curtilage building could reasonably be considered under this exception criteria and the test of whether it would be “materially larger” applied.

## Removal of existing buildings

118. If it is argued that the demolition of existing buildings would either increase openness or would balance any loss of openness caused by the proposal, then it will generally be preferable to consider the overall consequences for openness in Step 1. Should it be concluded that the proposal would bring about a positive outcome in terms of openness, this should also be weighed in the balance within Steps 3-5. Whether the removal of existing buildings would lead to a visual improvement is a separate matter to openness and is therefore best covered as an ‘other consideration’.

119. If the demolition of existing buildings is necessary to enable permission to be granted, a condition must be imposed that requires the buildings to be demolished within a reasonable time frame. You may also need to consider whether a building could be erected in any event under permitted development rights and the likelihood of this occurring.

## Dwellings for rural workers

120. Dwellings for rural workers in agriculture or forestry are primarily intended for residential use. Consequently, they are not buildings for agriculture or forestry (even though they are intended to support such a use). Unless a proposed rural worker’s dwelling specifically falls within one of the exceptions in the NPPF (for example, because it is the re-use of a building) it would be inappropriate development. If you conclude that there is an ‘essential need’ for a rural worker’s dwelling you will need to

<!-- Part2 p.106 -->

consider whether this would be an ‘other consideration’ which would clearly outweigh any harm to the Green Belt and any other harm and so amounting to ‘very special circumstances’.

121. Issues relating to the Green Belt may arise in proposals seeking to remove an agricultural occupancy condition. However, the dwelling will already exist and a potential change in occupancy from an agricultural to a non-agricultural worker would not be a material change of use or an act of development. Accordingly, it would be reasonable to conclude that the question of inappropriateness is not relevant to such proposals.

## Renewable energy

122. Policy GB6(3) specifically identifies that in the case of proposals for renewable and low carbon energy development, very special circumstances may include the wider environmental benefits associated with increased production of energy from renewable sources.

## Advertisements

123. Advertisements are subject to control only in the interests of amenity and public safety.16 As a result, development plan and NPPF policies relating to the Green Belt are not relevant.

## Temporary permissions

124. In some cases, permission will be sought for a temporary period after which the development would cease17. In Europa Oil and Gas Limited v SSCLG, Surrey County Council, Leath Hill Action Group [2013] EWHC 2643 (Admin) the judge confirmed that it is plain that temporary development can be inappropriate (paragraph 56). Consequently, if a development is inappropriate, the harm to the Green Belt would still be substantial (GB6 of the NPPF). However, the degree of any other harm could potentially be reduced.

## Removal of permitted development rights

125. There are appeal cases where the LPA suggests conditions which would remove permitted development rights on new buildings in the Green Belt or where permission is sought for extensions. There will also be appeals against conditions cases where conditions removing permitted development rights are in dispute.

126. However, permitted developments rights have not been withdrawn (in total or in part) in the Green Belt in the GPDO. The NPPF states that planning conditions should not be used to restrict national permitted development rights unless there is a clear justification to do so (DM6). In addition, the PPG (21a-017-20190723) says that conditions restricting the future use of permitted development rights may not pass the tests of reasonableness or necessity. These provisions should be borne in mind when considering proposals where such conditions are at issue.
