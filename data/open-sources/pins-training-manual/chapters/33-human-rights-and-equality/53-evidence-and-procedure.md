# 33 Human Rights and Equality: Evidence and Procedure

<!-- Part2 p.507 -->

### Evidence and Procedure

313. Again, much advice here is similar to that given in relation to Article 8:

- It is not unusual for the PSED to be engaged in cases proceeding by written representations.

- Inspectors should be alert to any claims or evidence which point to implications for persons with protected characteristics, whether or not they refer expressly to equality or the PSED.

- Assertion or anecdote is evidence that should be accepted unless it is contradicted and even if it carries little weight.

- Inspectors should request information as necessary to make a sound decision, while being alert to data protection requirements.

- It will usually be straightforward in written representation cases for the Inspector to properly identify the main issues and make a demonstrably ‘proportionate’ decision.

- If there is a prospect of a serious harm to persons with protected characteristics – or their needs otherwise being a decisive factor – it may be appropriate to determine the case though the hearing or inquiry procedure, so that the evidence is properly tested and the implications of the PSED are fully aired.

<!-- Part2 p.508 -->

- Inspectors must be pro-active and inquisitorial at hearings or inquiries, to ensure that all the main issues are discussed, all relevant evidence is brought forward and there is enough information to have due regard to the three aims.

314. Occasionally, it may be unclear as to whether a person’s characteristics are ‘protected’ or not. For example, the evidence may be ambiguous as to whether a health condition represents a disability, or a belief is protected. As a rule, Inspectors should avoid making any determination that a person does or does not have a protected characteristic; that is a legal question and the focus in any case should be on the link between the characteristic(s) and development.

315. If an issue related to the person’s characteristics is material but the evidence is insufficient for the Inspector to make a reasoned decision, sensitive enquiries should be made, with questions focussed on the issue rather than person. For example, if it is proposed to carry out [harmful] works to a listed building in order to meet the health needs of an occupier, the Inspector will need to understand what those needs are and why the development is required to meet them. It should not be necessary to interrogate the health condition which gives rise to those needs; doing so would be intrusive and probably unhelpful.

316. It would only be appropriate for an Inspector to reach a view as to whether a person’s characteristic is protected or not (on the balance of probabilities) if that person is relying on the characteristic for their case, the issue is critical, and the parties have properly discussed the matter. Even then, the safest and most respectful approach will normally be for the Inspector to either give the person the benefit of the doubt and assume that their characteristic is protected or reach no view. The protection in itself merely means the PSED is engaged, it would not make that person’s case for or against the appeal.

317. In Local Plan, Infrastructure and major planning appeals, the information before the Inspector may include an Equality Impact Assessment (EqiA). An EqIA should be an evidence-based document which assesses the likely impacts of whatever is subject to the case on persons with protected characteristics69. It is not for the Inspector to judge whether the EqIA is robust or the party who prepared it discharged the PSED. The Inspector should rather use the EqIA in the same way as other evidence: to inform your reasoning that will lead to your conclusion and decision.

318. An EqIA may carry significant weight, but the Inspector is not bound to agree with any or all of its findings especially – but not only – if the parties do not agree either. If other evidence indicates, for example, that the EqIA underestimates the impact of a proposal on the elderly, the Inspector should canvas the matter and make their own finding as to what the impact would be.

319. Given the second aim of the PSED and wording of s149(3)(c), concern may be expressed that other organisations did not encourage persons who share a relevant protected characteristic to participate in proceedings. For example, questions may be raised in examination as to whether the Romany Gypsy community was properly consulted on a Local Plan. Objectors to a planning appeal may question the public consultation on the application.

320. It will be for the authority as to whether they properly ‘encouraged’ participation by persons with relevant protected characteristics. It is not the role of the Inspector to decide whether any other body complied with the PSED in the exercise of their functions. However, whether the consultation was adequate in the circumstances may bear on the soundness of the Local Plan or robustness of the Council’s case at appeal.

321. Inspectors must comply with the PSED70 in procedural decisions of all kinds, ranging from whether to accept late representations to whether to adjourn an inquiry. It should be borne in mind that people with protected characteristics may be involved in any casework as a party or on a professional basis.

322. The duty set out in the EA10 to make reasonable adjustments for disabled persons applies to proceedings; the Inspector is accountable for the accessibility of hearing and inquiry venues. Adjustments may also be needed, for example, when considering what the procedure is, deadlines for or formats of representations, how much time is allowed for the site visit and the frequency of breaks or length of sitting days71. The Judicial College’s Equal Treatment Bench Book provides advice on reasonable adjustments that may be made in a court or tribunal setting.

323. Inspectors should take appropriate steps to meet the needs of any participants who are pregnant, have recently given birth or are breast-feeding. To avoid making intrusive inquiries, it may be safest to act as though any baby was born in the last 26 weeks. Inspectors should also take steps to meet the needs of older and disabled participants, which should be assessed on a case-by-case or person-by-person basis.

324. Unlike in the courts, it is for any party to Inspectorate proceedings to secure the service of a translator or interpreter as required. However, the Inspector should ensure that they can do so and accommodate the translator or interpreter as discussed in the Enforcement ITM Chapter.
