# 33 Human Rights and Equality: Reasoning

<!-- Part2 p.501 -->

### Reasoning

275. The advice given here is similar to that pertaining to Article 8. The Inspector should identify the issue(s) whereby protected characteristics are engaged, make reasoned findings on those issues, and taken account of them, with or without reference to the PSED, in their overall Conclusion and decision.

276. The PSED is likely to be engaged in casework where the development would be liable to have specific impacts upon a person, group or community with one or more protected characteristics because of those protected characteristics. Whether or not any party refers to the PSED, any such real-life impacts (and relevant policies) must be addressed in the reasoning before an assessment is made in the overall conclusion as to the equality implications of the decision.

277. Given that the PSED ‘must be exercised in substance, with rigour and an open mind’, Inspectors should scrutinise the evidence before them so as to identify whether any persons with protected characteristics are party to the case, and whether those characteristics could be relevant to the decision because of the implications – positive or negative – for those persons.

278. If the evidence suggests that an issue related to protected characteristics is significant for those persons and/or potentially decisive, it should be a main issue or consideration in the decision letter or report. This advice particularly applies if there is a prospect of the decision being at odds with the PSED.

279. The Inspector should frame such issues not in terms of the duty – which is a matter for the Conclusion – but in terms of the effects on the person(s) with the protected characteristic(s). The framing of the main issue should enable the Inspector to properly identify and ascribe weight to those effects with regard to

any less harmful alternative outcome’64 or potential conditions which might lessen the impacts on those persons or alter the balance in their favour.

280. The PSED may be engaged where any of the following is a main issue for one or more of the parties. The list contains examples and is not exhaustive or intended to be read prescriptively:

- The supply/availability of [accessible] [x bedroom plus] [sheltered] [other specialist] housing.

- The supply/availability of suitable, affordable and acceptable housing within [the catchment area] [commuting distance] of X [School] [Hospital]

- The impact of the loss of the [community centre] [sports facility] [nightclub] on [disabled people] [young people] [the LGBTQ+ community].

- The impact of the relocation of the [centre/services] on [children with SEN] [women] [disabled people] [people of Asian heritage] in the area

- The need for the [proposed] [development] to serve the local [[Charedi] Jewish] [Black British] [LGBTQ+] community.

- The benefits of the [proposed] [development] to [children] [in the area.

- The [appellant’s] [occupiers’] personal circumstances and need for the [proposed] [development] [with regard to…]

- The consequences of a refusal of permission for the [appellant’s] family

- The effect of the [proposed] [development] on the [living conditions] [reasonable enjoyment of the home] [health] of nearby occupiers…

281. Focussing on the real-world effects on persons with protected characteristics is key to substantive compliance with the PSED. Webb-Harnden v Waltham Forest LBC [2023] EWCA Civ 992 concerned an appeal against the Council’s decision to offer a homeless family accommodation in another part of England. The duty on the Council under the Housing Act 1996 was to secure ‘suitable’ accommodation with regard to prescribed matters.

282. The Council discharged the PSED not only because the reviewing officer set out and referred to the terms of s149, but also because they ‘considered all the matters that the appellant and the solicitors relied upon’. They considered the impact of moving and ‘the effect of separation from family and support networks on the physical and mental health of the appellant and her children’.

283. R (oao Devonhurst Investments Ltd) v Luton BC [2023] EWHC 978 (Admin) concerned whether the Council had due regard to the PSED when issuing an enforcement notice against blocks of flats. In their report, the Council had ‘not teased out the potential impacts by reference to…the [occupiers’] relevant protected characteristics, and ordinarily it would be better to do so’.

284. However, the Council had assessed ‘what was required by way of enquiry to enable it to assess the impact [of enforcement action] on [occupiers] with relevant characteristics’. The report acknowledged that requiring occupiers to move out would cause them disruption and distress, and impact upon their financial well-being, health and schooling as well as housing needs. The Council had ‘shown a proper appreciation of the potential impact of the decision on equality objectives and the desirability of promoting [them]’.

285. In other recent cases concerning the PSED, local authorities have successfully claimed that, even if there was a breach of s149, the claimant should be denied relief on the basis that the outcome would have been the same anyway65. Again, this implies that the decision-maker understood the substantive issues.

286. R (oao Addison) v Southwark LBC & Others [2022] EWHC 3211 (Admin) concerned a grant of permission for the redevelopment of a stadium and Astroturf pitch. The Council had received objections that ‘local people including many BAME people, use the Astroturf for informal sport and recreation and for them it is important that it is free and does not require booking’.

287. The Council’s report considered the PSED in relation to the protected characteristics of age, disability and sex but not that of race. Elsewhere in the report, however, the Council had ‘expressly considered’ the issues raised by objectors and found that there would be appropriate mitigation. It was held that, ‘even if there was some substance to the…PSED related complaints…it is highly likely that the outcome would not be substantially different’.

288. Thus, it again follows that the main issues should reflect what is crucial for the parties or those directly affected. For example, if a planning appeal concerns a proposed mosque or madrasa66, the main issues need not be restricted to the Council’s reasons for refusal; others might be the need of the local Islamic community for that facility and whether there are any suitable alternative sites.

289. In some cases, one of the main issues may relate to the interplay between the personal circumstances and relevant protected characteristic(s) of one or more individuals. It could be, for example: ‘the appellant’s need for a house extension, given their personal circumstances and Haredi Jewish faith’.
