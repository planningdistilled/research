# 33 Human Rights and Equality: International Human Rights Law

<!-- Part2 p.450 -->

## The Legal Framework

### International Human Rights Law

6. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) was adopted by the United Nations (UN) General Assembly on 10 December 1948. It recognises the ‘inherent dignity and…equal and inalienable rights of all members of the human family’ and sets out those ‘universal’ rights in thirty articles. The principle of universalism underlies the concept of human rights. The UDHR is not binding on member states, but its contents are reflected in treaties which are.

7. The European Convention on Human Rights (the Convention) came into force on 3 September 1953. It drew on the UDHR as well as national documents such as the 1689 Bill of Rights (England) and 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen (France). Section I of the Convention sets out the main rights and freedoms; Section II establishes the European Court of Human Rights (Strasbourg Court or ECHR).

<!-- Part2 p.451 -->

8. Article 1 of Section I of the Convention binds the signatory parties to secure the rights under Articles 2-18. Protocol 1 to the Convention sets out rights that the signatories could not agree to place in the Convention itself.

9. In deciding cases concerning Convention rights, the Strasbourg Court has developed and applies two legal doctrines:

- The Convention is a ‘living instrument’ and human rights are interpreted so that they are relevant to contemporary circumstances. Human rights may be engaged in respect of issues such as climate change or new technologies which were unforeseeable when the Convention was signed.

- Convention rights are subject to a ‘margin of appreciation’, respecting that the different signatory nations have different legal and cultural traditions and should therefore have scope to decide on how the rights apply in practice. The ‘margin of appreciation’ is linked to the concept of ‘subsidiarity’, the principle that social and political issues should be dealt with at the lowest or most local level consistent with their resolution.

10. All Council of Europe member states are party to the Convention and bound by judgments of the Strasbourg Court. The Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement 1998 refers to the Convention as a safeguard and includes a commitment by the UK Government to incorporate the Convention into the law of Northern Ireland.

11. The HRA98 was passed to ‘give further effect’ in UK law of the ‘rights and freedoms guaranteed’ under the Convention. It came into force in October 2000. Article 2 of the ‘Northern Ireland Protocol’ to the Brexit Withdrawal Agreement provides that there shall be no diminution of rights, safeguards or equality of opportunity as set out in the Good Friday Agreement.

12. The amended protocol, set out in the Windsor Framework of 23 February 2023, recognises ‘the need…to protect all dimensions of the Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement’. It states that ‘the Protocol as amended will be subject to the general principles of public international law, as set out under the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties…the fundamental underpinning of this arrangement is in international law, not EU law and the EU institutions’.

13. The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) came into force on 2 September 1990. Article 3(1) provides that ‘in all actions concerning children… undertaken by public or private social welfare institutions, courts of law, administrative authorities or legislative bodies, the best interests of the child shall be a primary consideration’. The Equality and Human Rights Commission (the Commission) provides guidance on the UNCRC.

14. Article 3(1) is not incorporated into the Convention or HRA98, but the Supreme Court considered its application in a public law context in ZH (Tanzania) v SSHD [2011] UKSC 4. It was held that ‘in making the proportionality assessment under Article 8 [of the Convention], the best interests of the child

The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD) came into force on 3 May 2008 to promote, protect and ensure the full and equal enjoyment of all human rights and fundamental freedoms by all persons with disabilities, and to promote respect for their inherent dignity. The UK agreed to follow the UNCRPD in 2009 as described in Commission guidance.

16. Since the HRA98 is UK law, Inspectors should make decisions in accordance with it rather than the Convention. However, it may be appropriate to describe, for example, ‘…rights under Article 8 of the [Convention] as set out in Schedule 1 of the [HRA98]’.

17. Section 6(1) of the HRA98 provides that it is unlawful for a public authority to act in a way which is incompatible with a Convention right. The word ‘act’ in that context, under s6(6), includes a failure to act.

18. S6(3) provides that a ‘public authority’ includes (a) a court or tribunal and (b) any person certain of whose functions are functions of a public nature. Inspectors work in that capacity3, with ‘functions of a public nature’ including procedural as well as appeal and other casework decisions.

19. The rights as set out in Schedule 1 of the HRA98 are, in summary:

- Article 2 – right to life

- Article 3 – prohibition of torture

- Article 4 – prohibition of slavery and forced labour

- Article 5 – right to liberty and security

- Article 6 – right to a fair trial

- Article 7 – no punishment without law

- Article 8 – right for respect for private and family life

- Article 9 – freedom of thought, conscience and religion

20. The rights fall into three broad categories:

- Absolute rights may not be violated. Articles 3, 4 and 7 are absolute rights. The right to a fair trial is also absolute but certain specific minimum rights set out in Article 6 apply to criminal and not civil cases such as appeal proceedings.

- Limited rights are rights that may be limited or curtailed in certain circumstances. Articles 2, 5 and 12 are limited rights.

- Qualified rights are rights which may be ‘interfered’ with or ‘infringed’ in order to secure an aim set out in the relevant article. Articles 8, 9, 10 and 11 and Article 1 of the First Protocol (A1FP) are qualified rights. Dealing with these involves balancing the fundamental rights of individuals against the legitimate interests of others and the wider public interest4.

21. Articles 1 and 13 of the Convention do not feature in the HRA98. The Act itself fulfils the rights set out under Article 1 that signatory states must secure the Convention rights in their own jurisdiction. S7 of the HRA98 fulfils the Article 13 right for any ‘victim of an unlawful act’ to bring proceedings against the authority in the appropriate court or tribunal and to rely on their Convention right(s) in any legal proceedings.

22. For the purposes of s7, a person is a victim of an unlawful act only if they would be a victim for the purposes of Article 34 of the Convention, if proceedings were brought in the [Strasbourg Court] in respect of that act. Article 34 provides that ‘the court may receive applications from any person, non-governmental organisation or group of individuals claiming to be the victim of a violation…’

25. However, the Court found that the applicant association, given its membership, its representativeness, and its purpose to ensure its members could exercise their rights regarding the effects of climate change on them was 'a vehicle of collective recourse aimed at defending the rights and interests of individuals against the threats of climate change in the respondent State’. Although the association ‘cannot itself claim to be the victim of a violation of the Convention’, it was granted standing ‘in the interests of the proper administration of justice’.

26. In the context of UK planning law, a claimant company did not have standing to bring a human rights claim in Devonhurst Investments Ltd v Luton BC [2023] EWHC 978 (Admin). The company argued that, when deciding to take enforcement action against the residential use of flats, the Council had not had proper regard to the best interests of the children living on the site. The Court held that the company was not a victim for the purposes of s7 of the HRA98 because it made no allegation that its own Article 8 rights had been breached, and it did not represent the flat occupiers who could speak for themselves.

27. Again, however, as in VKSS, the Court in Devonshire Investments proceeded to consider the merits of the Article 8 claim. Inspectors should not speculate in any decision or report as to whether any party would have ‘victim status’ in HRA98 terms, were they to make a statutory challenge or application for judicial review on human rights grounds. Our advice to be mindful of any human rights implications for persons who are not parties but affected by the decision, such as tenants in the appeal building, also still stands.
