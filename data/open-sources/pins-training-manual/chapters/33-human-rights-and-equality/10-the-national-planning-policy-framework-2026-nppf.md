# 33 Human Rights and Equality: The National Planning Policy Framework 2026 (NPPF)

<!-- Part2 p.458 -->

### The National Planning Policy Framework 2026 (NPPF)

51. The NPPF provides that the purpose of the planning system is to contribute to the achievement of sustainable development. That means the planning system has three overarching objectives, economic, social and environmental, which are interdependent and need to be pursued in mutually supportive ways (so that opportunities can be taken to secure net gains across all three).

52. The social objective is set out in NPPF paragraph 17b:

‘To support strong, vibrant and healthy communities, by ensuring that a sufficient number and range of homes can be provided to meet the needs of present and future generations; and by fostering well-designed, beautiful and

<!-- Part2 p.459 -->

safe spaces, with accessible community facilities, public service infrastructure and open spaces that reflect current and future needs and support communities’ health, social and cultural well-being.’

53. The NPPF indicates, in paragraph 18, that development plans and decisions should play an active role in guiding development towards sustainable solutions but, in doing so, should take local circumstances into account, to reflect the character, needs and opportunities of each area.

54. NPPF Policy S4, provides that development proposals within settlements should be approved unless the benefits of doing so would be substantially outweighed by any adverse effects such as in relation to recreational land and facilities or Local Green Space (Policies HC7 and HC8).

55. Conversely, the limited forms of development which should be approved outside of settlements, under Policy S5, include an exception site, development brought forward under a Community Right to Build or Neighbourhood Development Order, or development which would meet an evidenced unmet need for Gypsy, Traveller or Travelling Showpeople’s accommodation.

56. Chapter 6 of the NPPF, ‘Delivering a Sufficient Supply of Homes’, is prefaced with a statement that “the objective of the policies in this chapter is to support the delivery of a substantial increase in the supply of homes and traveller sites, by ensuring that a sufficient amount and variety of land can come forward where needed. This includes planning for an appropriate mix of accommodation for the local community and ensuring that land with permission is developed without unnecessary delay.”

57. Policy HO1 therefore includes:

2. At the most appropriate level, development plans should also take into account an assessment of the size, type and tenure of housing or other accommodation needed for different groups. These groups include, but are not limited to:

a. Those who require affordable housing (including Social Rent) as defined in the glossary at Annex B;

b. Older people (including those who require retirement housing, housing-with-care and care homes);

c. Disabled people;

d. People who rent their homes;

e. Families with children;

f. Looked after children (evidence of need for which can be found in the relevant local authority’s Children’s Social Care Sufficiency Strategy) and those who require other specialist community-based accommodation (where evidence of need is available for the relevant local authority);

<!-- Part2 p.460 -->

g. Students;

h. Travellers; and

i. People wishing to commission or build their own homes.

58. Policy HO12 is specifically concerned with the provision of Gypsy and Traveller sites and so the NPPF supersedes Planning Policy for Traveller Sites (PPTS). The definitions of ‘Gypsy and Traveller’ and ‘Travelling Showperson’ contained in PPTS Annex A are set out in NPPF Annex B and remain consistent with the judgment in Smith v SSLUHC & Others [2022] EWCA Civ 1391, as explained in the Gypsy, Traveller and Travelling Showpeople’s Casework ITM.

59. The NPPF also supports community-led development (HO10 and W3) and the provision, improvement and/or protection of community facilities (CC2, HO4, TC2, L1, GB5, HC1-HC8, P1, P4 and N3). Policy P4 sets out continued support for application of the “agent of change” principle to ensure new development does not result in unreasonable restrictions placed on the current or permitted operations of existing businesses, community facilities, public services or defence operations.

60. The NPPF also supports effective community engagement in planning processes (DM1 and DP2) and the consideration of “wider sustainability benefits to the community” where the exception test is applied (F6). Policy HE1 supports local heritage lists to identify non-designated heritage assets that are important to the local community.

61. The term “community facilities” is defined in NPPF Annex B as including meeting places, public houses, places of worship, cultural venues, youth centres, allotments, cemeteries and burial grounds, and facilities for play, sport and informal recreation.

62. “Community-led development” is defined as: “a development taken forward by, or with, a not-for-profit organisation, that is primarily for the purpose of meeting the needs of its members or the wider local community, rather than being a primarily commercial enterprise…The benefits of the development to the community should be clearly defined and consideration given to how those benefits can be protected over time...”

63. Notwithstanding the words “wider local”, the term “community” should be read as capable of encompassing [local] communities of interest, such as those with specific protected characteristics such as disability or religion.

64. Thus, while the NPPF does not refer expressly to human rights or equality, it shows how the social impacts of development may be relevant in any planning appeal. Social impacts may be positive and/or negative, and either way, they may be experienced by individuals and/or groups so that human rights and/or the PSED are engaged.
