## Introduction

1. This chapter sets out legal, policy and practical considerations for appeals casework involving Gypsies, Travellers and Travelling Showpeople in England. Inspectors make decisions on the evidence before them and the law and planning policy as they stand, which may sometimes justify departure from advice given here.

2. The terms ‘Traveller sites’ and ‘Traveller appeals’ are used in this chapter, except where more precision is required, as shorthand for Gypsy, Traveller or Travelling Showpeople’s sites and appeals.

3. The purpose of this chapter is to provide advice on matters typically raised in planning and enforcement appeals pertaining to the use of land as a Gypsy, Traveller or Travelling Showpeople’s site:

- Harms typically alleged to be caused by such sites, and Temporary and Personal Conditions which, if necessary, may be imposed to make the development acceptable.

- The need for sites in the relevant area, plus current and likely future levels of provision.

- Whether the Council has a five-year supply of specific, deliverable sites against their locally set targets.

- The accommodation needs of the appellant and alternative accommodation options realistically available to them.

- Personal circumstances which may be relevant to the decision.

- If necessary, whether the intended occupants are ‘Gypsies or Travellers’ or ‘Travelling Showpeople’ for planning purposes.

- Whether, or to what extent, the development complies with the development plan and national policy set out in Planning Policy for Traveller Sites (PPTS), the National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) and the Planning Practice Guidance (PPG).

- Whether to make a split decision and/or grant a temporary and/or personal permission, and the length of any temporary permission.

- The planning balance, including assessment of the implications of the decision in terms of human rights (including the best interests of the child) and the public sector equality duty (PSED).

4. This chapter does not duplicate advice pertaining to Traveller site policies or allocations set out in the Local Plan Examinations ITM chapter.

## Who are Gypsies, Travellers & Travelling Showpeople?

### Traveller Groups and ‘PPTS Status’

5. “Gypsies and travellers”1 are defined in Annex 1 of PPTS 2024 as “Persons of nomadic habit of life whatever their race or origin, including such persons who on grounds only of their own or their family’s or dependants’ educational or health needs or old age have ceased to travel temporarily or permanently, and all other persons with a cultural tradition of nomadism or of living in a caravan, but excluding members of an organised group of travelling showpeople or circus people travelling together as such.”

- “Whatever their race or origin” means that person who is not an ethnic Gypsy or Traveller (such as a “New Age” traveller) may fall within the Annex 1 definition, while a person who is a member of an ethnic Gypsy or Traveller group may be excluded.

- “On grounds only of…have ceased to travel temporarily or permanently”2 mean that persons who pursued a nomadic way of life but ceased to do so fall within the Annex 1 definition, so long as their reason(s) for ceasing to travel for work are related to their own or a dependant’s educational or health need or old age.

- “All other persons with a cultural tradition of nomadism or living in a caravan” indicates that ethnic Gypsies or Travellers who have a ‘cultural aversion to bricks and mortar’ and need to live or continue living in a caravan but have never travelled for work or ceased to do so for a reason not related to education, health or old age, may fall within the Annex 1 definition. Such persons may include those who took on caring duties as soon as they left school, whose nomadic work dried up, or who at some point were accommodated in a bricks and mortar house.

6. In practice, most Traveller casework relates to sites intended for occupation by Romani or other ethnic Gypsies, or Irish or other ethnic Travellers3, that is, for persons who have the “protected characteristic” of race for the purposes of the PSED. When referring to any ethnic group or someone as a member of such, the initial letters should always be capitalised, as in ‘Romani Gypsy’.

7. “Travelling showpeople are defined in Annex 1 of PPTS 2024 as “Members of a group organised for the purposes of holding fairs, circuses or shows (whether

8. Travelling showpeople are typically self-employed; they travel the country to hold circuses or amusement or entertainment fairs, and/or to run rides or kiosks at shows, festivals, markets, fetes or even shopping centres. Their community is not an ethnic group, but it is both small and tightknit.

9. Historically, travelling showpeople worked seasonally; to this day, their sites may be referred to as ‘winter quarters’. They are now likely to work at any time of year, but also to find work precarious, as reflected in the provision for them to cease travelling on grounds of ‘more localised pattern of trading’.

### Travellers, Caravans and Travellers’ Culture

10. Gypsies, Travellers and travelling showpeople usually live in caravans as an integral and necessary part of their nomadic lifestyle; living in a caravan facilitates travel for work4. However, being nomadic does not preclude having a base upon which a caravan may be stationed for the occupier(s) to return to and live in at any time. PPTS is thus concerned with sites rather than caravans.

11. While Gypsies and Travellers with different families or family backgrounds do not often wish to share the same site, they have many needs, traditions and cultures in common5. Romani Gypsy and Irish Traveller communities, for example, share a tradition of nomadism and living in caravans. Whether or not they move every day is immaterial; their ethnic and cultural identity is to always be able to move. Living in a building with a sense of enclosure may adversely affect the health and wellbeing of people used to freer and more outdoor living6.

12. The dominant position of the family is integral to Gypsy and Traveller culture. Where possible, they live and travel in extended family groups for mutual support and care. Their strongly held belief and practice is to care for elderly, sick or disabled members within the family. Gypsies and Travellers take their caring responsibilities seriously and may experience profound isolation if separated from their families.

13. Another key element of Gypsy and Traveller culture, especially for Romani Gypsies, is a high emphasis on maintaining cleanliness through customs such

It is intrinsically linked to their nomadic traditions and cultures, and it underlines why their housing need, for the purposes of planning policy, is considered in terms of caravan sites.

15. Many Romani Gypsies and Irish Travellers do live in conventional housing, but not always by choice; some were accommodated there by their local authority when homeless8. Travellers have had varying degrees of success in adapting to life in bricks and mortar9, and some wish to return to living in caravans. Others have never lived in a house and would find it harmful, in accordance with their traditions and culture, to do so.

16. Inspectors should not assume that these aspects of Gypsy and Traveller culture and identity apply to all Gypsies or Travellers or would be relevant in any given case. It is for the parties to set out in their evidence the facts and considerations that are material to the appeal.

## The Use and Occupation of Land

17. Planning permission is required for ‘development’ as defined by s55 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (TCPA90). ‘Development’ includes the carrying out of operations or a material change of use. It does not include, and planning permission is not required for the ‘occupation’ of land. Any control over occupation must be secured by way of a planning condition.

### Gypsy and Traveller Sites and Pitches

20. Gypsies and Travellers generally live on residential ‘pitches’10, each of which is typically occupied by one household, meaning a single adult or two+ adults living together as a family or household, with or without children, who have both a static and a touring caravan. Some private sites contain two+ pitches for extended family groups to live together.

21. Travellers may seek to develop pitches next to existing sites, in order to live closer to family or friends. They may seek to develop pitches next to land that can be used for the keeping of horses, as is traditional in Traveller culture, and/or some other land use(s) related to their nomadic work.

22. Operational development may be required to facilitate the use of land as a Traveller site; this may include the laying of hardstanding for access, parking vehicles and/or the stationing of caravan(s), the erection of buildings such as utility blocks or dayrooms, the erection of fences or walls and the installation of incidental domestic structures such as sewerage, play and/or lighting facilities.

23. Subject to the description and the approved plans, a grant of permission for the use of land as a [residential] caravan site would not necessarily be construed as a grant of permission for associated operational development.

24. Thus, the majority of Gypsy or Traveller appeals concern:

- A change of use of the land to residential use [for Gypsies or Travellers] facilitated by the siting of [x number of] caravans.

- A change of use to a mix of uses comprising residential use as above plus (for example) the keeping of horses and/or [specified] business use(s).

- Operational development – on its own or with the change of use.

### Transit Sites, Temporary Stopping Places and Negotiated Stopping

27. Transit sites are sites that are in permanent residential use but only for temporary occupiers, normally being Gypsies and Travellers rather than travelling showpeople. Transit sites are required in most planning authority areas to meet the needs of Travellers who resort to the district.

28. Transit pitches may be provided on sites that are otherwise used as the permanent base of one or more families. The owner may wish to reserve the pitches for relatives, friends and/or colleagues, or rent them out on a commercial basis to other Travellers.

29. Some transit sites have individual plots of tarmac hardstanding and a utility shed with bathroom and toilet facilities. Others are more basic but still by definition remain in situ permanently.

30. The length of stay on a transit site or pitch may vary but is usually between 28 days and three months. The requirements may be more relaxed where transit pitches are provided on private family sites but, even then, there must be some limitation so they are not used as permanent bases for individual households.

31. When permission is granted for a transit site or pitch(es), and as set out in PPTS paragraph 28c), conditions must be imposed to specify the length of time

## Planning Issues Arising in Traveller Casework

### Traveller sites in the Green Belt

56. Green Belt policy set out in paragraphs 142-160 of the NPPF and advice in the Green Belts ITM Chapter applies to Traveller casework. Crucially, under NPPF paragraph 153, inappropriate development is, by definition, harmful to the Green Belt and should not be approved except in very special circumstances.

57. Any finding that development would be inappropriate in the Green Belt does not in itself mean that there is conflict with the NPPF, PPTS or, necessarily, any relevant development plan policy. Substantial weight should be given to any harm to the Green Belt, including harm to openness. But since inappropriate development may be permitted in very special circumstances, whether there would be conflict with Green Belt policy is usually for the planning balance.

Inappropriate Development: PPTS paragraph 16 and NPPF paragraph 154h)v)

58. PPTS paragraph 16 states that: “Traveller sites (temporary or permanent) in the Green Belt are inappropriate development unless the exceptions set out in Chapter 13 of the [NPPF] apply”. Although this paragraph is located in the ‘Plan-making’ section of PPTS, it should be applied to Traveller site appeals relating to land in the Green Belt.

59. NPPF paragraph 154h)v) – previously 150e) – provides that a material change of use of land is not inappropriate development so long as it preserves the openness of the Green Belt and does not conflict with the purposes of including land within the Green Belt. This paragraph does not apply to Traveller site development or indeed to any proposed change of use to a caravan site.

60. It was held in Royal Borough of Kingston Upon Thames Council v SSLUHC [2023] EWHC 2055 (Admin) that an Inspector erred in deciding whether a Traveller site would be inappropriate development in the Green Belt on the

<!-- Part2 p.126 -->

basis of the then NPPF paragraph 150(e). Lieven J’s reasons for that judgment were:

- Paragraph 4 of NPPF 2021 ‘state[d] in terms that it must be read with PPTS’; this remains true of NPPF 2024.

- “…residential uses do not fall within paragraph 150(e)” because they are not of the same “flavour or extent” as the “such as” sports, recreation, cemeteries and burial grounds uses listed. “If [150e)] was simply an open ended category then it is not clear why examples would be given at all.”

61. Notwithstanding the 2024 revisions, PPTS paragraph 16 remains the starting point for consideration of Green Belt Traveller site appeals. It does not specify which ‘exceptions’ to inappropriate development apply to Traveller sites but they are unlikely to include NNPF paragraph 154h)v), because that does not encompass any change to residential use.

62. Even if it is argued that the change of use to create a Traveller site would preserve or enhance the openness of the Green Belt, perhaps through the removal of pre-existing structures on the land, Inspectors should not consider whether the development is inappropriate under NPPF paragraph 154h)v). The correct approach would be to find that PPTS paragraph 16 applies and then:

- Consider whether the exception set out in NPPF paragraph 154g) or 155 applies, as discussed below.

- If the answer to the above question is ‘no’, Inspectors should conclude that the development is inappropriate in the Green Belt under PPTS paragraph 16, attach substantial weight to that definitional harm, but go on to consider the impact of the development on openness, and attach weight to any benefits in that regard in accordance with PPTS paragraph 27b).

Inappropriate Development: ‘Exceptions’

63. The first ‘exception’ set out in the NPPF that is potentially relevant to Traveller site development in the Green Belt is that set out under paragraph 154g): “the limited infilling or the partial or complete redevelopment of previously developed land (including a material change of use to residential or mixed use including residential), whether redundant or in continuing use (excluding temporary buildings), which would not cause substantial harm to the openness of the Green Belt, is not inappropriate development”.

64. When considering this exception, regard should be had to the definition of previously developed land (PDL) set out in NPPF Annex 2 which was modified in December 2024 to include large areas of fixed surface infrastructure such as large areas of hardstanding but also exclude any unlawful development.

<!-- Part2 p.127 -->

65. Advice from MHCLG is that paragraph 154g) would normally apply to sites which entirely or substantially comprise PDL. The redevelopment of land containing redundant barns, for example, would not fall within the exception, since PDL excludes land that is or was occupied by agricultural buildings.

66. The second potentially relevant exception is that set out under NPPF paragraph 15518: the development of homes, commercial and other development in the Green Belt should not be regarded as inappropriate where criteria apply. Wherever caravans are to be stationed for permanent residential use, whether for occupation by Travellers or otherwise, the development would provide homes for the purposes of paragraph 155 – which should also be read as allowing for a mixed use Traveller or Travelling Showperson’s site.

67. The first criterion set out under NPPF paragraph 155 is a): The development would utilise grey belt land and not fundamentally undermine the purposes (taken together) of the remaining Green Belt across the area of the plan. The term ‘grey belt’ is defined in NPPF Annex 2 and subject to advice in the Green Belt ITM. Crucially, grey belt comprises PDL and any other land that, in either case, does not “strongly contribute” to any of the purposes of including land in the Green Belt as set out under (a), b) or d) of the NPPF at paragraph 143.

68. NPPF paragraph 155b) requires that there is a demonstrable unmet need for the type of development proposed. NPPF footnote 56 explains that, in the case of Traveller sites [for occupation by persons with PPTS status], “demonstrable unmet need” means the lack of a five year supply of deliverable traveller sites assessed in line with PPTS.

69. NPPF paragraph 155c) requires that the development would be in a sustainable location, with particular reference to NPPF paragraphs 110 and

115. NPPF footnote 57 adds that, for development to provide Traveller sites, particular reference should also be made to PPTS paragraph 13.

70. The final criterion set out under NPPF paragraph 155 is d): Where applicable the development proposed meets the ‘Golden Rules’ requirements set out in [NPPF] paragraphs 156-157. PPTS paragraph 18 is explicit that the Golden Rules do not apply to Traveller sites. Only criterion (a), (b) and (c) of NPPF paragraph 155 therefore need to be met.

Other Considerations and ‘Very Special Circumstances’

76. In accordance with NPPF paragraph 153, inappropriate development in the Green Belt should not be approved except in very special circumstances, which will not exist unless the potential harm to the Green Belt by reason of inappropriateness and any other harm resulting from the proposal is clearly outweighed by other considerations.

77. Other considerations raised in favour of Traveller sites in the Green Belt may include: a shortage or unmet need for Traveller sites in the local authority area; lack of a five year supply of Traveller sites in the local authority area; failure of the local authority to provide Traveller sites through their planning policies; and the personal need for a site and personal circumstances of the appellant or other site occupiers. An appellant may rely on site-specific factors, such as the removal of historic structures to improve openness or ‘sustainability’ benefits.

78. The decision maker must decide where the balance lies in each case, with regard to the substantial weight that must be attached to Green Belt harm (in accordance with the NPPF) and whatever weight is attached to any other harm(s), unmet need, any personal circumstances and/or other considerations. As of December 2024, the planning balance in Green Belt cases is entirely for the decision-maker and not guided by the PPTS.

79. In accordance with the Green Belts ITM Chapter, each ‘other consideration’ must be treated separately and discretely. Each consideration in favour of the appeal should be weighted but not referred to as ‘very special circumstances’ in themselves or compared individually to the identified harm. In the final balance,

it should also be remembered that ‘other considerations’ do not have to be unique, rare or uncommon to amount to very special circumstances19.

80. The hierarchy of weight has often been described as: ‘little’/‘limited’, ‘moderate’, ‘material’/‘significant’ and ‘considerable’ up to ‘substantial’. The Courts have accepted that “the word ‘substantial’ does not denote a greater quantum of weight than significant’” albeit subject to the context20. However, Inspectors should still take care to avoid ambiguity or uncertainty.

81. If the Inspector considers that different factors should carry the same weight, the most straightforward approach is to denote that by using the same words. If different descriptors of weight are to be used, perhaps where ‘significant’ weight is attached to personal circumstances, after ‘substantial weight’ is given (as required) to Green Belt harm, the Inspector should tell the parties clearly if they actually intend ‘significant’ to be read as equal to ‘substantial’.

82. PPTS Policy C, set out in paragraph 14, states that “When assessing the suitability of sites in rural or semi-rural settings, local planning authorities should ensure that the scale of such sites does not dominate the nearest settled community.”

### Character and Appearance

87. PPTS paragraph 4k) expects authorities and, by extension, Inspectors to have due regard to the protection of local amenity and the environment. It is not uncommon for authorities to object to Traveller sites on the basis of conflict with development plan policies which particularly seek to protect the character and appearance of the countryside.

88. However, as discussed above, PPTS paragraphs 14 and 26 implicitly accept that Traveller sites – with all that they include – may be located in rural areas. If and where the use is acceptable in principle, that must encompass the stationing of caravans which are not uncommonly seen across the countryside on farms and holiday caravan sites as well as established Traveller sites.

89. Thus, the Inspector should decide whether any detraction from or harm to the character and appearance of a rural area would be unacceptable. That finding should be based on an assessment of the scale, characteristics and visual impact of the development in its context, rather than some generalised objection to caravans urbanising the countryside.

A literal reading of the policy would:

‘…render it unworkable because it is difficult to conceive in practice and reality that there would be any kind of development with regard to Gypsies which would not, at least in some way, detract either from the character, or from the appearance, or from both, of the countryside…it is reasonable to construe the policy as embracing detractions which are perhaps significant or material. That would give the policy real purpose and bite and…make it workable’.

### Flood Risk and Drainage

93. NPPF Annex 3 classifies caravans, mobile homes and park homes intended for permanent residential use ‘highly vulnerable’ to flood risk. PPTS paragraph 13g) expects that local plan policies – and appeal decisions – ensure that Traveller sites are sustainable economically, socially and environmentally by, amongst other matters, not locating such sites in areas at high risk of flooding, including functional floodplains, given the particular vulnerability of caravans.

94. Any flood risk issue raised in a Traveller or travelling showpeople’s appeal should be determined with regard to the NPPF, the Planning Practice Guidance (PPG) chapter on Flood Risk and Coastal Change and the Flood Risk ITM. NPPF paragraph 181 and footnote 63 provide that a site-specific flood risk assessment (FRA) should support all applications relating to land in Flood Zones 2 and 3, and certain applications relating to Flood Zone 1, including those relating to land that may be subject to other sources of flooding, where its development would introduce a more vulnerable use.

95. NPPF paragraph 176 and footnote 62 expect that the sequential and exception tests are applied as appropriate to any application for a change of use to a caravan, camping, chalet, mobile home or park home site.

96. NPPF paragraph 175 requires use of the sequential test when determining applications relating to areas known to be at risk now or in the future from any form of flooding, except where the site-specific FRA demonstrates that no built development within the site boundary, including access or escape routes, land raising or other potentially vulnerable elements, would be located on the area at risk of flooding from any source, now or in the future.

97. The purpose of the sequential test is to consider whether appropriate sites are reasonably available in areas with a lower risk of flooding. This assessment should be entirely consistent with the assessments of the general need for,

<!-- Part2 p.133 -->

supply of and availability of Traveller sites. It may be that the site-specific FRA does not need to include a detailed sequential assessment if – for example – it is undisputed that there is a shortfall of Traveller sites and none are available.

98. Moreover, in considering future flood risk, regard should be had to whether permission may be granted on a permanent, personal or time-limited basis. It may be appropriate, given the circumstances and the period of the permission, to set aside (rather than ignore) any allowance for climate change in the site specific FRA. The planning balance could change on that basis.

99. The exception test should be applied where, having applied the sequential test, it is not possible for development to be located in an area at lower risk of flooding. However, the need for the exception test will depend on the potential vulnerability of the site and development – and PPG Table 2 indicates that the exception test is not required for highly vulnerable uses in Flood Zone 3.

100. However, if the site is in Flood Zone 2 and the sequential test is passed, a Traveller site would be subject to the exception test. It should be shown that:

a) the development would provide wider sustainability benefits to the community that outweigh the flood risk; and

b) the development will be safe for its lifetime taking account of the vulnerability of its users, without increasing flood risk elsewhere, and, where possible, will reduce flood risk overall.

101. If the exception test is not applicable, regard should still be had to NPPF paragraph 170 which requires that any development which is ‘necessary’ in an area at risk of flooding should ‘be made safe for its lifetime without increasing flood risk elsewhere’. To be clear, paragraph 170 allows for Traveller sites in Flood Zone 3 although PPG Table 2 advises that ‘highly vulnerable development’ should not be permitted there.

102. The Inspector’s findings on whether there are ‘wider sustainability benefits’ – or whether the development is ‘necessary’ – should again be integrated with their findings on the need for, supply of and availability of Traveller sites, plus the PPTS paragraph 13 ‘sustainability criteria’ and, where applicable, the personal need or circumstances of those intending to occupy the site.

103. In considering whether the development would be safe for its lifetime and users, and not increase flood risk elsewhere, regard should be had to all the criteria set out in NPPF paragraph 181.

104. The development of a Traveller site will often involve the laying of hard surfacing which could increase surface water run-off. Traveller sites also often lack connection to mains sewers and require the installation of a septic tank or cesspit. The Inspector should establish the intended foul and surface water

<!-- Part2 p.134 -->

drainage arrangements and whether the development would or could incorporate a sustainable drainage system.

105. NPPF paragraph 182 requires that applications which could affect drainage on or around the site to incorporate sustainable drainage systems, in order to control flow rates and reduce volumes of runoff. The system should be proportionate to the nature and scale of the development and, where possible, provide multifunctional benefits for water quality, biodiversity and amenity.

### Highway Safety

106. Where an issue is raised as to the actual or potential effect of the use of land as a Traveller site on highway safety, regard may need to be had to:

- Characteristics of the (rural) road network including the presence or otherwise of footways and street lighting.

- Local traffic volumes and patterns.

- Visibility splays and other safety issues pertaining to the site access.

- The proposed mix of uses, number of parking spaces and the nature and size of vehicles that would be moved on and off the land.

- Whether or not the number of residential pitches would generate similar trips per day than the equivalent number of dwellings, being in mind that Travellers tend to rely on their private vehicles, but do not commute daily for work, and there may be potential for shared trips on multi-pitch sites.

107. Travelling showpeople’s sites typically require access from a main road for large and slow moving vehicles. The necessary junction design, visibility splays and impacts of projected vehicle movements from proposed Travelling Showpeople’s sites should be assessed on an individual basis.

### Access to Services and Facilities

108. It is not unusual for local authorities to suggest that proposed Traveller sites would lack adequate access to shops or services by foot, bicycle or public transport. Any such objection should be assessed and concluded upon with regard to relevant policies in development plans as well as the evidence relating to the location of nearby services, the provision of footways and cycle paths, the nature of local roads generally and of local public transport services.

109. However, as noted above, Travellers have a variety of reasons for preferring sites in rural or semi-rural areas, and such developments may be permitted under PPTS paragraphs 14 and 26. It is therefore relevant that NPPF paragraph 110 recognises that opportunities to maximise sustainable travel solutions will vary between urban and rural areas. Inspectors should consider

<!-- Part2 p.135 -->

whether any rural Traveller site in the authority area could be reasonably and realistically expected to facilitate access on foot or by bike or public transport.

110. It should also be borne in mind that persons with a nomadic way of life will travel by definition and not work in employment areas that were planned along public transport routes. Wherever they live, Travellers rely on private vehicles for work and often for access to shops and services too. If there is a dispute over accessibility, it may be appropriate to consider the work catchment area and access to the motorway network as well as to local amenities.

111. Inspectors should also have regard PPTS paragraph 13 which is concerned with social, economic and environmental sustainability for Traveller sites. Only two of the criteria set out under paragraph 13 mention travel – and even they do not mention distance or means of transport to local shops and services. Rather, they expect that Traveller sites should:

13d) provide a settled base that reduces both the need for long-distance travelling and possible environmental damage caused by unauthorised encampment.

13h) reflect the extent to which traditional lifestyles (whereby some travellers live and work from the same location thereby omitting many travel to work journeys) can contribute to sustainability.

112. It follows from PPTS paragraph 13d) that, when addressing questions of accessibility or sustainable travel, consideration should be given to whether – on the evidence – a refusal of permission would be liable to generate more or longer journeys by private vehicle due the site occupiers being forced to live by the roadside, potentially further away from shops and services.

113. PPTS paragraph 13 is otherwise concerned with non-travel sustainability issues. Criteria b) and c), which promote ‘access’ to appropriate health services and ensuring that children can attend school on a regular basis, are concerned with ‘access’ in the sense of ability to secure health care and education. Anyone without a fixed address will find it harder to register for and/or sustain health care or education.

114. Appellants often argue that even a remote site will improve access to medical or educational facilities. Subject to the evidence, Inspectors may need to treat such an issue as relevant to sustainability (as well as personal circumstances). PPTS paragraph 4j) aims to enable the provision of suitable accommodation from which Travellers can access education, health, welfare and employment – and there are social benefits to everyone having proper health care, and all children attending school regularly. It may be necessary to address whether such benefits could be expected arise if any Travellers occupied the site.

115. Accordingly, any question as to whether the site would be suitable in ‘access’ terms should be considered in the light of not only the relevant development

<!-- Part2 p.136 -->

plan policies but also material considerations including PPTS paragraph 13. It may be necessary to acknowledge pros and cons to the development before concluding as to whether ‘accessibility’ or ‘sustainability’ in the round is a consideration for or against the appeal, or neutral in the planning balance.

### Living Conditions and Community Integration

116. PPTS paragraph 4k) expects authorities to have due regard to the protection of local amenity and the environment, while paragraph 13e) requires proper consideration of the effect of local environmental quality (such as noise and air quality) on the health and well-being of any Travellers that may locate there or on others as a result of new development.

117. Matters relating to living conditions, such as effect of the development on outlook, light and privacy for occupiers of the site and of adjacent properties, should be considered in the same way as for any residential development, including whether any harm can be overcome by imposing conditions.

118. The same usually applies to noise, although on mixed use Traveller or Travelling Showpeople’s sites, regard should be had to the potential for noise and disturbance from vehicle movements on and off the site, vehicular parking on the site, and any other on-site business activities.

119. It is not unusual for Travellers to enclose their sites with high hedges or walls that may prompt objections on the grounds of visual harm or overshadowing. Conversely, there may be objections that Traveller sites are insufficiently screened from adjoining properties.

120. The acceptability of the boundary treatment should be assessed with regard to PPTS paragraph 27d) which expects that weight is attached to Traveller sites not being enclosed with so much hard landscaping or high walls or fences that the impression may be given that the site and its occupants are deliberately isolated from the rest of the community.

121. PPTS paragraph 27 is also relevant to living conditions insofar as criterion c) expects weight to be attached to the promotion of opportunities for healthy lifestyles, such as ensuring adequate landscaping and play areas for children.

122. PPTS paragraph 13a) seeks to promote peaceful and integrated co-existence between Traveller sites and the local [settled] community. While not all Traveller sites are subject to objections, and there may indeed be letters of support, it is not usual for such appeals to attract considerable complaints from interested parties, including groups and/or politicians.

123. In dealing with concerns as to the impact of a Traveller site on a settled community, regard should be had to:

- The fact that a Traveller site is a residential use.

<!-- Part2 p.137 -->

- Any relevant findings and/or lack of objections on living conditions.

- The Government’s aims, set out in PPTS paragraphs 4e), h) and i), to promote more private traveller site provision, and increase the number of traveller sites (generally) in appropriate locations with planning permission, while reducing tensions between settled and Traveller communities.

- Whether the site, if it is or would be in a rural and semi-rural location, would respect the scale of and not dominate the nearest settled community in accordance with PPTS paragraphs 14 and 26.

- In accordance with PPTS paragraph 13f), whether the development would place undue pressure on (or, conversely, help to sustain) local infrastructure and services.

- Peaceful and integrated co-existence depends, by definition, on Travellers living in the same area as members of the settled community, so that they can interact and have shared interests in the use of shops, schools and community facilities such as churches.

### Intentional Unauthorised Development

124. A Written Ministerial Statement (WMS) made on 27 December 2015 confirms changes to national planning policy, initially made by a “Dear Chief Planning Officer letter”, to make ‘intentional unauthorised development’ a material consideration to be weighed in the determination of planning applications and appeals received since 31 August 2015.

125. In deciding whether there has been, and the weight to be attached to any ‘intentional unauthorised development’, it may be useful to have regard to:

- Whether the appellant did ‘intend’ that the site be unauthorised, given that they have sought to regularise the development by applying and paying the fee for a grant of retrospective planning permission.

- The appellant’s reasons for developing the land without waiting to obtain planning permission, for example, if they had nowhere else to live.

- Whether, or the extent to which, the works carried out exceeded the minimum necessary to site caravans and create a habitable environment.

- The WMS states that, where development is undertaken in advance of obtaining planning permission, there is no opportunity to appropriately limit or mitigate the harm that has already taken place. However, it may be – and so Inspectors should consider whether – any harm caused can in fact be limited or mitigated via planning condition or obligation.

<!-- Part2 p.138 -->

126. It should also be borne in mind, when attributing weight to intentional unauthorised development, that the TCPA90 provides through s73A and s177 for grants of retrospective planning permission. The enforcement code as contained in Part VII of the TCPA90 is intended to be ‘remedial not punitive’.

### Other Issues

127. PPTS does not say that Travellers must occupy a caravan and it has been held by the Court of Appeal that they do not22. However, Gypsies and Travellers have long lived in caravans to facilitate their nomadic lifestyle and tradition, and so PPTS is concerned with the development of caravan sites.

128. In most cases, success at appeal would mean a grant of permission with the word ‘caravan’ or ‘caravans’ somewhere in the description of development, and a condition imposed to restrict the numbers and/or types of ‘caravan’ on the land. Accordingly, it will be necessary to deal with any concerns raised that a relevant structure on the land may not be a caravan23. Regard should be had to the statutory criteria and relevant case law as well as factual evidence from the site visit and/or hearing or inquiry24.

129. Where an appeal is made for a bricks and mortar house for a Traveller family, it should normally be considered in accordance with the development plan and the NPPF as an appeal for general housing. It would rarely be reasonable to restrict occupation of a house to Gypsies and Travellers since they are nomadic by definition or tradition. Permanent buildings should also not be subject to personal conditions as advised by the PPG 'Use of Planning Conditions' and the Conditions ITM.

130. PPTS would probably be of limited relevance even if the new dwelling would be built on or close to an existing Traveller site for the purposes of occupation by a site manager or family member. However, regard should be had to PPTS, as well as personal circumstances, if it is proposed to develop a new Traveller site close to an existing house for family reasons.

131. It is not unusual for local authorities or residents to raise fear that allowing an appeal would set an undesirable precedent and thus limit the ability of the authority to control development on other sites, particularly in the Green Belt. As in any casework, it is necessary to show that any decision to allow the appeal is made strictly on the merits of the case25.

### Loss of a Traveller site

134. Where planning applications are made for a change of use from a Traveller site, it is not unusual for the authority to refuse permission on the basis of land supply harm through the loss of pitches or plots. Such development may conflict with any local plan policy that seeks to safeguard either existing Gypsy and Traveller and/or travelling showpeople’s sites specifically, or residential land more generally.

135. Even if there is no such policy or conflict, regard should be had to evidence of the need for and supply of the relevant kind of Traveller site – and then weigh in the balance the benefits of the proposed development against the loss of the pitches or plots. If the proposal is to construct bricks and mortar housing on the land, it may be necessary to compare and contrast the five year supply of land for general housing and Traveller sites.

136. If a major development proposal would require the relocation of a Traveller site, whether permanently or temporarily, PPTS paragraph 22 expects local authorities to work with the applicant and affected Traveller community to identify a suitable site or sites. If and where the development to be displaced was lawful, the applicant should identify and provide an alternative site.

## The Need for and Supply of Traveller Sites

137. The need for and supply of Traveller sites is a main issue or consideration in almost all appeals concerning the change of use of the land to introduce new pitches or plots. It is necessary to distinguish between and deal separately with the ‘general’ need for sites by the authority, and the ‘personal’ need of the appellant(s) and/or site occupier(s)26.

138. With respect to general need, the key matters to test at hearing or inquiry and address in the appeal decision are:

[^26]: Hedges v SSE & East Cambridgeshire DC [1996] EWHC Admin 240

<!-- Part2 p.140 -->

- The Council’s ‘locally set targets’ for the provision of Gypsy and Traveller pitches or Travelling Showperson plots in the development plan or a Gypsy and Traveller Accommodation Assessment (GTAA).

- Any other evidence of a need for pitches or plots over the relevant period.

- The supply of land for pitches or plots against the locally set targets or other assessment of need.

- Whether there is a shortfall of sites to meet existing needs, and the broad extent of the shortfall. This is what is known as ‘unmet need’.

- Whether the authority has a five year supply of specific deliverable sites for pitches and plots against their locally set targets. Whether the authority has a five year supply is not the same question as to whether they have an unmet need and it should therefore be assessed separately.

- Any proposals from the authority to redress any shortage of sites or lack of five-year supply through the development plan process or other means.

139. The ‘relevant’ area in terms of need for a site may extend beyond than the local authority boundaries27. Some authorities co-operate when carrying out GTAAs as described below.

141. Prior to PPTS, local authorities were required to undertake GTAAs to inform core strategies and allocations in development plan documents; see Annex A. The duty on authorities now is to consider the needs of people residing in or resorting to their district with respect to the provision of sites on which caravans can be stationed; s124 of the Housing and Planning Act 2016 (HPA16) repealed s225 and amended s8 to this effect.

142. PPTS paragraph 7 expects local authorities to use a robust evidence base to establish accommodation needs to inform the preparation of local plans and planning decisions. The need for Traveller sites should be assessed (in a GTAA or otherwise) as a sub-set of needs for caravan sites generally, which in

### Other Evidence and ‘Need on the Ground’

149. Other evidence pertaining to the need for or supply of Traveller sites may be given at appeal. The Traveller Caravan Count has been undertaken every year in January and July since 197932. It is carried out for MHCLG, usually by local authority Gypsy and Traveller Liaison, Housing or Environmental Health Officers. It provides a record of the number of caravans on authorised public and private sites and on unauthorised developments and encampments.

150. The accuracy and consistency of the count varies between local authorities, and it is in any event only a record of occupation; it is best regarded as a snapshot of the number of caravans present in that area on those dates. In that regard, however, the counts may indicate general (patterns of) need over time, and whether there is likely to be any ‘need on the ground’.

151. Information may be submitted with an appeal relating to changes in the area that have occurred since the GTAA base date:

- Any new planning permissions granted, and whether any such additional sites would be available to Travellers not known to the landowner.

- The progress of a site allocations development plan document, the prospects of draft allocations being permitted and the likely date(s), if known, at which such new sites may come forward.

- Evidence of need arising from unauthorised developments or encampments, or the loss of Traveller sites to redevelopment.

152. The appeal must be decided on the basis of current circumstances, which may not reflect the estimates of need and supply on the GTAA base date. Any evidence of changes in the area since the base date and/or of further ‘need on the ground’ may reduce the weight of even the most accurate GTAA for the particular appeal decision. That said, outside of the GTAA process, counts of travelling showpeople tend to be carried out on an irregular basis.

### Unmet Need

153. The Council’s assessment and/or other evidence may show that the local authority does not have sites available to meet the current needs of Travellers residing in or resorting to the district. This situation may be variously described as a ‘backlog of need’ or ‘unmet need’ or ‘shortfall of sites’.

[^32]: The counts typically show fewer caravans in July than in January, since Travellers are more likely to be on the road in the summer months.

<!-- Part2 p.144 -->

154. It is normally necessary to make a finding in an appeal decision as to whether there is an outstanding need for pitches/plots and, if so, the broad scale of the unmet need relative to the Traveller population.

155. Inspectors should be aware that unmet need can indicate an immediate and pressing need for Traveller sites – which in turn can be expected to result in unauthorised encampments, environmental damage, lack of access to health care, education and even fresh water, sanitation and washing facilities, and to community tensions. As with any material consideration, however, the weight attached to unmet need for the decision-maker.

156. Even if there is not an unmet need, it does not follow that there will be a five year supply of deliverable sites. That question must be assessed separately as set out in the next section.

### Five Year Land Supply Issues

157. PPTS paragraph 10a) requires local authorities, in producing their local plan, to identify and update annually a supply of specific deliverable sites sufficient to provide five years’ worth of sites [five years’ worth of pitches or plots] against their locally set targets. If the authority cannot demonstrate an up to date five year supply of Traveller sites, PPTS paragraph 28, states that the provisions of NPPF paragraph 11d) apply33.

158. Consideration should be given not only to whether the authority has a five year supply in quantitative terms, but also to matters such as:

- Whether the supply includes a mix of tenures given PPTS paragraph 4e) and a mix of site sizes (including the size proposed), and it would meet the identified needs for different Traveller groups.

- Whether the supply includes sites subject to a temporary or personal permission which will in fact expire.

- Whether the Council intends to allocate existing unauthorised sites or sites with temporary permissions – which would ensure deliverability but only address the needs of the existing occupiers.

- Evidence of the deliverability of new sites or sites which the authority rely on as part of its five-year supply. The reasoning should clearly resolve any arguments about deliverability for the purposes of the decision.

- The anticipated provider of, funding source for and delivery timescale for any proposed affordable pitches – and where pitches or plots would be provided within mixed allocations.

- Constraints to delivery, such as the cost of environmental mitigation work.

159. As with unmet need, it is not necessary to describe the Council’s supply of sites with arithmetical precision. It was held in Swale BC v SSHCLG [2018] EWHC 3402 (Admin) that an Inspector did not err in granting temporary permission for a Traveller site partly on the basis of a ‘substantial shortfall’ of pitches.

### Emerging Plans

160. If there is an emerging local plan and/or DPD, the Inspector should establish the stage(s) it is at, and whether it contains any policies and/or allocations in order to bring forward Traveller sites. Other questions to address may include:

- Whether there is or will be a new accommodation needs assessment.

- Whether the Council accepts that there is a need for more Traveller sites and, if so, for what Traveller groups and of what tenure(s) and sizes etc.

- The likelihood of and timescale for adoption of the plan in its current form.

- The prospects of and timescales for any proposed allocations being granted planning permission and made available for occupation.

161. Weight should be attached to emerging local plans and their policies or allocations with regard to the factors set out in NPPF paragraph 49. The Inspector should also consider the prospects of an emerging plan being adopted within the period of any temporary permission that may be granted.

### ‘Large Scale Unauthorised Site[s]’

162. PPTS paragraph 12 states that: ‘In exceptional cases, where a local planning authority is burdened by a large-scale unauthorised site that has significantly increased their need, and their area is subject to strict and special planning constraints, then there is no assumption that [they are] required to plan to meet their Traveller site needs in full.’

163. At the time of introduction in August 2015, the Government stated that ‘…there is only one local authority caught in this position (Basildon District Council in respect of Dale Farm)’34. Since Dale Farm was an exceptional site with some 80 unauthorised pitches, Inspectors should take it that there is a high threshold for a ‘large scale unauthorised site’ to be a material consideration.

164. Moreover, the implications of any large scale unauthorised site for meeting Traveller site needs should be addressed at the local plan examination. If that

## Alternative Sites

165. Whether an appellant relies on general need, personal need or both, there is no requirement for them to prove a need to live specifically on the appeal site, or that no other site is available. The Court of Appeal held in South Cambridgeshire DC v SSCLG & Brown [2008] EWCA Civ 1010 that:

‘In seeking to determine the availability of alternative sites for residential Gypsy use, there is no requirement in planning policy, or case law, for an applicant to prove that no other sites are available or that particular needs could not be met from another site…such a level of proof would be practically impossible…’

166. Nonetheless, the appellant may have conducted an unsuccessful search, and there may be other evidence of the availability (or otherwise) of alternative sites, perhaps from the Council’s planning or housing records, a Strategic or site-specific FRA, Gypsy and Traveller liaison officers, site managers, estate agents, landowners or other Travellers. Any such evidence may assist in understanding the actuality of any need as well as availability on the ground.

167. Where there is some evidence of alternative pitches or sites, the Inspector will need to reach reasoned conclusions as to whether:

- The proposed occupiers could live on the alternative site lawfully, meaning that the residential use is subject to an extant planning permission or lawful development certificate, and the occupation of or numbers/types of caravans on the site would not be in breach of any condition(s).

- The alternative site is a genuine or realistic alternative, meaning suitable, affordable, available and acceptable as discussed below35 or whether dismissing the appeal would be likely to make the intended occupiers homeless, and lead to camping on unauthorised sites.

168. While the parties should have the opportunity to put forward their views, the subject of alternative sites will rarely need to be discussed in any depth. There are rarely any realistic alternative sites and that is not usually disputed.

### Suitability

169. To be considered realistic, alternative sites should be capable – in principle – of being used for residential purposes without causing unacceptable harm to the environment or community in conflict with the development plan, the NPPF or

[^35]: Doncaster MBC v FSS & Smith [2007] EWHC 1034 (Admin)

<!-- Part2 p.147 -->

PPTS. Suitability will also depend on whether any alternative site will meet the occupiers’ requirements in terms of size, characteristics and/or location.

170. The suitability of an alternative site will need to be compared to that of the site under appeal. Where the proposed site would include more than one pitch or plot to be occupied by named individuals – probably relying on personal circumstances – facts to be established should include:

- The relationships between the households, including any shared working or mutual care arrangements and how long they have lived/travelled together.

- How important it is for them to remain together and the consequences for them of living apart.

- Whether they could live apart on smaller sites close to each other36.

171. Similarly, it may be necessary to address whether and why the appellant requires a site that is large enough and includes suitable space for other uses, for example, the stabling of horses or storage of business equipment.

172. In terms of location, PPTS paragraph 25e) is clear that authorities should determine applications for sites from any Travellers and not just those with local connections. However, any requirement from the appellant to live in a particular area should be factored in when considering the suitability of any alternative sites. Relevant needs may include:

- Work related reasons for living in the area, such as road links or proximity to sources of work.

- Education or health-related reasons, such as children attending a particular school, or any occupier being treated at a local hospital.

- Proximity to family and/or friends in the area.

173. Land in commercial areas may be acceptable for travelling showpeople’s sites, given their needs to use, store and/or move plant, machinery and heavy vehicles, and the risk of such activities causing unacceptable noise or other pollution in predominantly residential areas. However, showpeople and their families will themselves require adequate living conditions. Alternative sites would need to be suitable for carrying out all uses in the mix.

### Affordability

174. The importance of affordability in relation to human rights was addressed in Chapman v UK [2001] ECHR 43:

[^36]: Moss v FSS & South Cambridgeshire DC [2003] EWHC 2781 (Admin)

<!-- Part2 p.148 -->

“The cost of a site compared with the applicant’s assets, and its location compared with the applicant’s desires are clearly relevant. Since how much the applicant has by way of assets, what outgoings need to be met by her, what locational requirements are essential for her and why they are essential are factors exclusively within the knowledge of the applicant, it is for the applicant to adduce evidence on these matters.”

175. It may be reasonable to ask how much was paid for a site, but detailed questions about assets and the affordability of another site would normally be unduly intrusive. In any event, the answers may be unreliable or not recordable without compromising data protection regulations.. It is usually more appropriate to focus questions on land prices in the area generally and whether there is a reasonable prospect of the appellant being able to afford another site.

### Availability and Acceptability

176. It may be necessary to adjudge whether an alternative site that is not yet available will become so and, if so, when. For example, it may be that an allocated site does not yet have planning permission, but the Council may be aware that the landowner is preparing an application which is expected to be submitted and approved within the following 12 months.

177. Where mooted alternative sites do not have planning permission, it may be necessary to assess whether their use as a Traveller site would probably be more, less or equally harmful than the appeal development in planning terms (given the Council’s reasons for refusal). If the alternative site is unlawfully developed and occupied already, the Council should be asked as to whether they intend to take enforcement action or what stage such action is at.

178. It is reasonable to ask the Council whether they know of any vacancies on existing public or private sites, the length of any waiting lists in respect of public sites, and the prospects of any pitches being rented to the appellant. In turn, the appellant may be asked if they have considered joining a waiting list.

179. However, the appellant may be unwilling to live on a Council site. Private sites are encouraged by PPTS and preferred by many Travellers, just as members of the settled community prefer to buy rather than rent. Living on a public rented caravan site can give rise to privacy or security challenges.

180. Pitches on private rented sites may also be unavailable or unacceptable to the appellant. Indeed, Travellers who own private sites tend to keep ‘vacant’ pitches for friends and family members, just as occupiers of bricks and mortar homes may not let out spare bedrooms. That said, PPTS paragraph 28b) encourages consideration being given to conditions which allow for temporary increases in caravan numbers on sites, to permit visitors – and potentially increase the letting of ‘spare’ pitches.

Where the appellant seeks permission for what would be a strictly family-occupied site, a refusal to contemplate living with other people may carry limited weight if the appellant is homeless and alternative sites are available. Even then, however, the Inspector should be sensitive in dealing with the issue, bearing in mind that many Travellers have had distressing lived experience of being moved on by other landowners.

### Weighing the Options

182. It was held in Linfoot v SSCLG & Chorley BC [2012] EWHC 3514 (Admin) that the option of a temporary permission should not have been discounted on the basis that a change in planning circumstances would not occur when there was in fact a possibility of changes across the county. It may in some cases, depending on the evidence, be necessary to consider the likelihood of accommodation becoming available elsewhere.

183. However, Inspectors should not cast around for evidence of alternative sites across the country37. To repeat, the appellant does not need to demonstrate local connections, or that they must live on the appeal site, or that no other site is available. And their evidence does not have to be detailed or corroborated. The Inspector must adjudge the likelihood of homelessness if the appeal is dismissed and does not need to be ‘certain’ that there are no alternative sites38. The appellant’s case should be accepted if it is clear and there is nothing to suggest that it is probably wrong.

184. Those points are important because many Travellers have difficulties with reading and writing. Most land deals between Gypsies are by word of mouth and a handshake – which does not absolve them of the need to register details of the land transfer with the Land Registry but does mean they may well have less written evidence to provide.

185. Moreover, landowners and estate agents are unlikely to provide written statements of the non-availability of sites. Local authorities may not concede that there are problems on any public sites. There will rarely be documentary evidence of matters that might make it impossible for an appellant to move onto sites owned by other Travellers in the area.

- Staying on another site while the occupiers are travelling; this would be a temporary measure at best and potentially in breach of any ‘personal’ condition that the site is subject to.

### Dealing with Information: Data Protection

212. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), brought into UK law through the Data Protection Act 2018 (DPA18), prohibits (subject to exemptions), the processing (which includes the dissemination or otherwise making available) of special categories of personal data, being that which reveals

The racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs or trade union membership [of an identifiable living individual]; genetic or biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person; or data concerning a natural person’s sex life or sexual orientation57.

213. Personal data relating to health should be treated as subject to Article 9(1) even if it is not strictly ‘genetic or biometric’ in nature.

It will be for the Inspector to consider the extent to which there is a substantial public interest in disseminating or otherwise making available any personal data within the special categories – or indeed any other sensitive personal information.

Information relating to children, including their name, age, address or school is likely to be seen to be more intrusive than that relating to an adult. ## PPTS Status

218. It is not unknown for authorities to cite lack of PPTS status as a reason for refusing a Traveller site application. However, planning permission normally runs with the land and so the first question is whether the use of land as a residential caravan site for occupation by Gypsies or Traveller is acceptable in planning terms, with the identity of the occupiers liable to change.

[^57]: Article 10 of the UK GDPR does not prohibit but sets out strict limitations to the processing of personal data relating to criminal convictions or offences, or related security measures.

<!-- Part2 p.157 -->

219. It is not necessary for an individual to have PPTS status in order to seek or be granted planning permission for a Traveller site. The application – which may even be made by an organisation rather than person – should be considered on the basis of its description58. Where permission is granted for the use that complies with local plan policies relating to Traveller sites and/or PPTS, a condition should be imposed to restrict occupation to persons with PPTS status.

220. In some cases, imposing such a condition might mean that the appellant or intended occupiers could not lawfully live on the land, but that should not a reason to refuse the appeal. The appellant could, after all, sell the site with the benefit of the permission. In the event that the appellant or others without PPTS status do move onto the site, the local authority would be able to take enforcement action against a breach of the relevant condition.

221. Some appeals may relate to family groups including people with and without PPTS status. The development should be considered on the basis that PPTS is applicable because matters such as the need for and supply of Traveller sites are likely to be relevant. However, it may also be necessary to enquire as to the relationships between members of the group, including whether any occupiers are dependents and the need for the family to stay together59. If permission is granted, it may be subject to PPTS occupancy and personal conditions.

222. Generally, then, if the use is acceptable on its merits, the question of status is likely to be immaterial. The exception is where the case for permission rests in whole or part on the personal circumstances of an individual or whole family without PPTS status. Such persons cannot benefit from any policies aimed at providing for Travellers. The question would then be whether to grant planning permission for a residential caravan site on the basis of the development plan, with regard to the NPPF and the occupiers’ rights under Article 8.

223. Since Gypsies and Travellers are defined in PPTS Annex 1 as including persons with a cultural tradition of nomadism, ethnic Gypsies and Travellers intending to live on a proposed Traveller site can now be expected to have PPTS status, even if they have never travelled or ceased travelling for reasons unrelated to education, health or old age. Evidence from the appellants relating to their ethnicity should be accepted unless it is credibly disputed.

## Human Rights and Equality in Traveller Casework

235. Rights set out under the Human Rights Act 1998 (HRA98) and the Public Sector Equality Duty (PSED) set out under the Equality Act 2010 are frequently engaged in Traveller casework. Comprehensive advice on upholding human rights and complying with the PSED is set out in the Human Rights and Equality ITM Chapter and not duplicated here67. The only additional points to stress here are that:

- For the purposes of Article 8, the “private and family life” of a Traveller encompasses living in accordance with their Gypsy or Traveller heritage, including living in a caravan, and a Traveller pitch or plot is a “home”.

- The best interests of the child(ren) – being those under 18 years old, even if they have left education or started work – is relevant to Article 8.

- For the purposes of the PSED, Romani Gypsies68 and Irish Travellers69 are ethnic minorities and thus have the protected characteristic of race under s149(7) of the EA10, whether they have PPTS status or not.

- The appellant and/or occupiers may have other relevant protected characteristics, such as age, disability, pregnancy/maternity and/or sex.

- The Article 8 rights and/or protected characteristics of interested parties may also be engaged in a Traveller case.

## Temporary and Personal Conditions

240. When considering an appeal for a change of use of land for a Traveller or Travelling Showpeople’s site, appellants will often ask for a grant of permission on a permanent basis in the first instance or, failing that, a time-limited basis. Even if the appellant does not make that point, the Inspector should address the possibility of granting a temporary or personal permission for the site76.

241. As with any other casework, most Traveller appeals will be dismissed or allowed on a permanent basis. Where the latter outcome would be

242. The weight attached to all considerations should be revisited when the question is whether to allow the development on a time-limited basis. In Moore v SSCLG [2013] EWCA Civ 1194, the Inspector concluded against a grant of permanent and then temporary permission. In considering the latter, they gave increased weight to unmet need and reduced harm to the Green Belt, but did not reconsider the weight attached to visual harm or personal circumstances, or make clear findings as to what would happen to the appellant.

243. The Court of Appeal held that there was ample material before the Inspector on which to make a finding on the likelihood of roadside camping if permission was refused – and it was likely on the facts. That was important to the ‘harm’ side of the balance as well human rights considerations; the Inspector had recognised that roadside camping would probably be equally harmful to the Green Belt and potentially more harmful to the countryside than the appeal development.

244. The Court of Appeal thus held that:

“…the grant of temporary permission would still result in the harm identified by the inspector, and it may not be strictly accurate to describe that harm as being cancelled out or neutralised by the harm that would result from the refusal of temporary permission, but the overall balance would necessarily be affected if the harm resulting from the refusal of temporary permission would be equal to or greater than the harm resulting from…such permission.

“…it is far from inevitable that the Inspector would have reached the same conclusion if [they] had made a finding on the likelihood of roadside camping and followed through its implications…[the] refusal of temporary planning permission was [not] a reasonable reflection of the factors [the Inspector] was required to take into account in that context”.

245. In Ward v SSLUHC [2024] EWHC 676 (Admin), an Inspector’s decisions to refuse permanent and temporary planning permission for a Traveller site in the Green Belt were upheld; the Court found that the Inspector considered all the relevant factors and reached rational conclusions that were clearly open to them. However, that judgment again reinforces that, when considering whether to grant temporary (and/or personal) permission, Inspectors should consider the harms and benefits in the light of the duration of the development and a realistic assessment of what will happen to the site occupiers.

248. The period of the temporary condition will depend upon the circumstances of the case, but often depends on when alternative sites seem likely to become available. As indicated above, if a grant of permission is justified in whole or part because of personal need and circumstances, the permission should be subject to a personal condition which names the beneficiaries and refers to their dependants.

250. Those named in the condition need not be restricted to or even include the appellant. The condition should list the names of the leading members of each family or group per pitch; where the leading members are an adult couple, their names should be separated by an ‘and/or’ (eg, Henry and/or Mary Smith) to take account of possible family breakdown or death.

251. The condition should refer to the ‘dependants’ of the leading members of the family group – but not name them in case, for example, more children are born. The implication of using the term dependant is that when and if those people are no longer dependant on the named individuals, or when those named are no longer resident, the continued occupation of the site by the one-time dependants is in breach of that condition.

252. A personal condition should apply for the lifetime of the beneficiaries but may be adapted so that it can be imposed alongside a temporary condition.

This is so that the authority can enforce against any continued use of the land in breach of the temporary or personal condition.

255. When considering a grant temporary or personal permission, the Inspector will need to address what other conditions would be necessary and reasonable given the scale and nature of any works that might be required and the duration of the permission. For example:

- If highway safety concerns could only be overcome through significant alterations to the site access, consider whether it would be reasonable to impose the burden of the works on the appellant when the duration of the permission would be short – and if not, whether temporary permission should be granted at all.

- If harm to the character of the area would be mitigated but not overcome by landscaping, and it would not be reasonable to impose the burden of the works on the appellant when the duration of the permission would be short, consider whether the condition is necessary at all, bearing in mind that the shorter duration of the permission will also mitigate harm.

- Whether day or utility rooms could be provided in temporary structures.

- Whether hardstanding could be required to be removed.

- What drainage facilities and/or boundary treatments would be required.

## Other Conditions

257. PPTS paragraph 28 expects LPAs – and, by extension, Inspectors – to consider how they could overcome planning objections to particular proposal using planning conditions or obligations. However, this chapter does not duplicate advice in the Conditions ITM.

258. The PINS suite of suggested conditions includes model conditions for Traveller sites but, as in any other decision, the starting point should be the conditions suggested by the parties, and reasons should be given for imposing (or not) any condition.

259. In most cases, permission is granted for Traveller sites based on the special accommodation needs of Travellers. A condition should thus limit occupancy to Gypsies and Travellers (or Travelling Showpeople) as defined in PPTS 2024 or any subsequent replacement guidance. This condition may be imposed alongside a temporary or personal condition, with care taken to not exclude any relevant occupiers, including dependents, who have never travelled.

260. Since the grant of permission will generally be for the use of the land as a residential caravan site, it is usually necessary to impose a condition specifying the maximum number of caravans overall or the maximum number of pitches together with maximum number of caravans per pitch. The condition may also need to specify the types of caravan allowed; typically there will be a minimum of one static caravan and one touring caravan per pitch.

261. Since caravans may be moved by definition, a condition should be imposed to control the position of caravans within the site if that is necessary, perhaps for visual reasons. The condition should either tie the permission to the approved plans or require that details of the site layout are submitted to the Council for approval and then implemented as approved. Site licensing regulations require minimum distances between caravans for reasons of fire safety.

262. In accordance with PPTS paragraph 28b), however, another condition could specify a number of days per year when the site can be occupied by more than the allowed number of caravans, to allow for visitors or attendance at family or community events.

263. Amenity or toilet blocks, and day or utility rooms may also be required for site licensing reasons, as well as to meet the appellant’s own needs. If these are needed but not shown on submitted plans, a condition may again need to be imposed which requires the submission and approval of details.

264. Other matters which often need to be controlled by condition for Traveller sites, including through the submission of further details include:

- Hard and/or soft landscaping

- Boundary treatments

<!-- Part2 p.167 -->

- External illumination

- The means of access into the site

- The layout and surfacing of parking and turning areas

- Foul and surface water drainage, including sustainable drainage.

265. Travellers are less likely than in the past to need space for business activities and, if planning permission is sought strictly for residential use, bear in mind that conditions restricting business activities would not normally be considered reasonable or necessary in respect of any other residential development. The Council has powers in any event to enforce against any future unauthorised material change of use to put the site into commercial or mixed residential and commercial use.

266. However, where it is proposed to create a mixed use site – such as for Travelling Showpeople – or it is made clear that occupiers might need space for incidental (and acceptable) work-related activities, PPTS paragraph 28a) advises that conditions can limit which parts of a site are used for any business operation in order to minimise the visual impact and limit the effect on noise. As with any commercial use, conditions can also be imposed to control, for example, the height and/or nature of outdoor storage, hours of operation and/or harmful effects such as odour.

267. Travellers often own vehicles larger than domestic scale, for towing a caravan, transporting horses or working away. It is customary to impose a condition which limits the weight of vehicles and the number of large vehicles that may be parked or stored on a Traveller site. The usual upper weight limit is 3.5 tonnes, but sometimes a higher upper limit of 7.5 tonnes is accepted depending on the occupiers’ needs and any concerns regarding character and appearance, living conditions and/or highway safety.

268. When planning permission is to be granted for a transit site or transit pitches on a permanent site, conditions must be imposed to specify the length of time any occupier may reside on the site (as set out in PPTS paragraph 28c)) and the interval before which they may be permitted to return, and how such occupation is to be monitored by the local planning authority.
