# 25 Gypsy, Traveller and Travelling Showpeople Casework: Personal Need and Circumstances

<!-- Part2 p.151 -->

## Personal Need and Circumstances

### Facts to (Try to) Establish

194. Personal need and circumstances are often pleaded in aid of Traveller site appeals – and were a key factor in the House of Lords’ judgment in South Buckinghamshire DC v SSTLR & Porter (No. 2) [2004] UKHL 33 to uphold an Inspector’s decision to grant planning permission subject to a personal condition for a Traveller site in the Green Belt. Where the appellant relies on personal considerations, it will be necessary for the Inspector to record the names of and relationships between the intended site occupants42.

195. In terms of personal need, Travellers generally need a settled base whether they travel for work or not. Those who still have a nomadic lifestyle need a base to which they can return during periods between work and join family members who do not travel in accordance with PPTS. To establish personal need, it is important to try and ascertain the relationships between the intended occupiers, their individual and collective needs, and whether any of them have anywhere else to live lawfully given:

- Where they are living now, if not on the appeal site.

- Whether they have ever had a settled base and, if so, why they left it, with regard to issues set out under ‘Alternative Sites’ above.

[^42]: Married women in Traveller communities may use their birth and married surnames interchangeably. Men may also have two surnames and a family group may have a ‘clan’ name.

<!-- Part2 p.152 -->

- Whether they can lawfully return to any other site as a whole family group (where appropriate) and without ‘doubling up’, otherwise giving rise to overcrowding or causing other unacceptable harm to living conditions.

- Whether they could live in bricks and mortar housing43. This question should be asked with sensitivity and not discussed at length. The answer in the vast majority of cases will be ‘no’ given the PPTS definition and Traveller’s cultural tradition of nomadism.

196. It may also be necessary to look at any personal circumstances which would add weight to the case for a grant of permission for their residential use of the appeal site, again having regard to the significance of any individual’s particular situation on the appellant group as a whole44, and indeed the Traveller tradition of living in extended family groups for mutual care and support.

197. Where occupiers on the site have ceased travelling ‘on grounds only of their own or their family’s or dependants’ educational or health needs or old age’, as per PPTS Annex 1, the appellant does not need to show that such needs are in some way ‘special’ in order for them to carry weight in favour of the appeal. However, it will be necessary for the appellant to describe the facts and circumstances that they wish to be considered, such as:

- Which occupiers, if any, have parental and/or caring responsibilities and which are ‘dependants’.

- The number and ages of any children, noting particularly any who are under 5 or of ‘school age’.

- Any adults who need particular support and/or are aged 65+.

- In the case of an extended family group, how long they have lived together or why they need to do so now.

198. Turning to education, it is usually necessary to assess in Traveller cases firstly the benefits of the child(ren) continuing or starting education from the appeal site and secondly the likely educational ramifications of a refusal of permission. It will be necessary to establish:

- How many children are currently enrolled at school.

- Whether the children are enrolled at primary and/or secondary school(s).

- How the children have settled at the school and any reasons for low attendance.

- Whether any children are on a register of special educational needs (SEN) or receiving any other special/extra help at school.

- The location of the school(s) and any local authority support needed for travel between the school and site.

- The children’s educational history: when they were first enrolled at school, any previous schools attended, any previous or continuing home schooling.

- What local educational support services for Traveller children exist, and what provisions they make for home-based learning.

- The consequences for the children’s education if the appeal is dismissed, with regard to the prospects or stage of enforcement action, the availability of alternative sites and the likelihood of the family living on the roadside.

199. Many children successfully change schools when their parents move home, but it is difficult for Traveller children to sustain attendance, if not even enrol at school if they have no fixed address or need to move between a series of temporary and/or unauthorised sites45. Children are likely to have lower educational attainment and experience other harm from the disruption if they miss school regularly or have to move between different schools46.

200. Inspectors should make reasoned findings47 on whether dismissing the appeal would be likely to render any children homeless, and the likely effects on their access to and stability of education. The weight attached to this consideration may be affected by factors such as the likelihood and degree of disturbance to education, the number of children involved, the strength of connection with existing school(s), and the transferability of any special help to another school.

201. It has been accepted that educational needs can carry significant (or more) weight even when they are not special or unusual48, as well as when there are special educational needs49. But even then, and if there is no realistic alternative site, the balance may still be against the appellant if sufficient harm is or would be caused by the development50.

203. Traveller communities have worse health outcomes than the population as a whole51. In 2006, it was recorded that Traveller life expectancy is lower by ten years for men and 12 years for women compared to the settled population; 42% of Travellers had a limiting long-term illness compared to 18% for the settled population; 18% of Traveller mothers had experienced the death of a child, compared to less than 1% of settled mothers52 53.

204. Since sick, disabled or elderly Travellers are cared for by their families, it is not unusual for health matters to be raised in Traveller appeals. Since there is a public health interest in universal access to basic health care, it will be necessary to establish in each case:

- Whether the intended site occupants are registered with a GP, the location of the practice and how it is or would be accessed from the site.

- If applicable, why the occupiers are not registered with a [local] GP54.

- Whether any intended occupants have health problems and, if so, the effects or limitations of these conditions55.

- Whether any intended occupants are receiving regular treatment from a GP, clinic or hospital and the frequency and location of appointments.

- Whether any occupiers require full or part-time care from another occupier or relative living close to the site.

205. 206. If no alternative, available and affordable site has been identified, and it is clear that conventional housing would be unacceptable56, then it is likely that dismissing the appeal would render the occupiers homeless – and that may make it difficult for the occupiers to access routine and/or specialist healthcare on a consistent basis, to access fresh water, sanitation and washing facilities, and/or to stay together and sustain caring responsibilities. It may lead to frequent moves from various unauthorised sites, and thus a lifestyle which is inherently insecure and physically demanding.

207. As with education, health problems or caring needs do not have to be ‘special’ to be given significant weight, but that is not to say they will. Acute or unusual problems or needs may attract additional weight.

### Dealing with People: Issues when Hearing Evidence

208. Where personal circumstances are raised, it is helpful – though not always crucial – if documentary evidence is provided from appropriate professionals. The authority should be asked if they accept the contents of such material.

209. At hearings or inquiries, appellants and witnesses may agree to be cross examined or asked questions. As in any other casework, Inspectors should be alert to the inherent sensitivities in dealing with personal circumstances and consider whether, or the extent to which it is necessary for such details to be aired orally in public. Questions and discussion should be limited to the minimum needed in order to understand and assess the implications of their circumstances for the appeal decision.

210. Intrusive questioning of the appellant or others should be curtailed, whether in respect of personal matters or the Traveller way of life, including that Travellers live and work in family groups; care for the elderly, sick or disabled members within the family; and require particular sanitation facilities. If necessary, clarify these traditions for the benefit of settled persons who are interested parties.

211. The Inspector should ensure that any person with difficulties in reading and writing is able to fully participate in the hearing or inquiry, by giving clarifications throughout the event and by giving any agent time to talk them through documents. If appropriate, the Inspector can explain to the parties that giving the individual time and assistance is necessary to ensure that proceedings are fair and to obtain the evidence needed to make the decision.

### Dealing with Information: Data Protection

212. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), brought into UK law through the Data Protection Act 2018 (DPA18), prohibits (subject to exemptions), the processing (which includes the dissemination or otherwise making available) of special categories of personal data, being that which reveals

The racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs or trade union membership [of an identifiable living individual]; genetic or biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person; or data concerning a natural person’s sex life or sexual orientation57.

213. Personal data relating to health should be treated as subject to Article 9(1) even if it is not strictly ‘genetic or biometric’ in nature.

214. An exception to that prohibition applies where the processing of the personal data is necessary for reasons of substantial public interest. It will be for the Inspector to consider the extent to which there is a substantial public interest in disseminating or otherwise making available any personal data within the special categories – or indeed any other sensitive personal information.

215. It is accepted that some personal data is likely to be more sensitive, based on the potential harm or impact on the individual(s). Information relating to children, including their name, age, address or school is likely to be seen to be more intrusive than that relating to an adult. Inspectors must be alert to the risk of hate crime against Travellers, whether or not they are or are not perceived to be ethnic Romani Gypsies or Irish Travellers.

216. Hearings and inquiries, as public events, must be conducted to avoid the publication of special categories of personal data or other sensitive personal information. The Inspector may require that any filming or recording of a hearing or inquiry is paused when any personal matters are to be described in evidence or submissions.

217. Full advice on writing decisions to enable publication which does not contravene data protection regulations is set out in Annex 5 of the Approach to Decision Making ITM chapter.
