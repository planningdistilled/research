# 25 Gypsy, Traveller and Travelling Showpeople Casework: Planning Issues Arising in Traveller Casework

<!-- Part2 p.125 -->

## Planning Issues Arising in Traveller Casework

### Traveller sites in the Green Belt

56. Green Belt policy set out in paragraphs 142-160 of the NPPF and advice in the Green Belts ITM Chapter applies to Traveller casework. Crucially, under NPPF paragraph 153, inappropriate development is, by definition, harmful to the Green Belt and should not be approved except in very special circumstances.

57. Any finding that development would be inappropriate in the Green Belt does not in itself mean that there is conflict with the NPPF, PPTS or, necessarily, any relevant development plan policy. Substantial weight should be given to any harm to the Green Belt, including harm to openness. But since inappropriate development may be permitted in very special circumstances, whether there would be conflict with Green Belt policy is usually for the planning balance.

Inappropriate Development: PPTS paragraph 16 and NPPF paragraph 154h)v)

58. PPTS paragraph 16 states that: “Traveller sites (temporary or permanent) in the Green Belt are inappropriate development unless the exceptions set out in Chapter 13 of the [NPPF] apply”. Although this paragraph is located in the ‘Plan-making’ section of PPTS, it should be applied to Traveller site appeals relating to land in the Green Belt.

59. NPPF paragraph 154h)v) – previously 150e) – provides that a material change of use of land is not inappropriate development so long as it preserves the openness of the Green Belt and does not conflict with the purposes of including land within the Green Belt. This paragraph does not apply to Traveller site development or indeed to any proposed change of use to a caravan site.

60. It was held in Royal Borough of Kingston Upon Thames Council v SSLUHC [2023] EWHC 2055 (Admin) that an Inspector erred in deciding whether a Traveller site would be inappropriate development in the Green Belt on the

<!-- Part2 p.126 -->

basis of the then NPPF paragraph 150(e). Lieven J’s reasons for that judgment were:

- Paragraph 4 of NPPF 2021 ‘state[d] in terms that it must be read with PPTS’; this remains true of NPPF 2024.

- “…residential uses do not fall within paragraph 150(e)” because they are not of the same “flavour or extent” as the “such as” sports, recreation, cemeteries and burial grounds uses listed. “If [150e)] was simply an open ended category then it is not clear why examples would be given at all.”

61. Notwithstanding the 2024 revisions, PPTS paragraph 16 remains the starting point for consideration of Green Belt Traveller site appeals. It does not specify which ‘exceptions’ to inappropriate development apply to Traveller sites but they are unlikely to include NNPF paragraph 154h)v), because that does not encompass any change to residential use.

62. Even if it is argued that the change of use to create a Traveller site would preserve or enhance the openness of the Green Belt, perhaps through the removal of pre-existing structures on the land, Inspectors should not consider whether the development is inappropriate under NPPF paragraph 154h)v). The correct approach would be to find that PPTS paragraph 16 applies and then:

- Consider whether the exception set out in NPPF paragraph 154g) or 155 applies, as discussed below.

- If the answer to the above question is ‘no’, Inspectors should conclude that the development is inappropriate in the Green Belt under PPTS paragraph 16, attach substantial weight to that definitional harm, but go on to consider the impact of the development on openness, and attach weight to any benefits in that regard in accordance with PPTS paragraph 27b).

Inappropriate Development: ‘Exceptions’

63. The first ‘exception’ set out in the NPPF that is potentially relevant to Traveller site development in the Green Belt is that set out under paragraph 154g): “the limited infilling or the partial or complete redevelopment of previously developed land (including a material change of use to residential or mixed use including residential), whether redundant or in continuing use (excluding temporary buildings), which would not cause substantial harm to the openness of the Green Belt, is not inappropriate development”.

64. When considering this exception, regard should be had to the definition of previously developed land (PDL) set out in NPPF Annex 2 which was modified in December 2024 to include large areas of fixed surface infrastructure such as large areas of hardstanding but also exclude any unlawful development.

<!-- Part2 p.127 -->

65. Advice from MHCLG is that paragraph 154g) would normally apply to sites which entirely or substantially comprise PDL. The redevelopment of land containing redundant barns, for example, would not fall within the exception, since PDL excludes land that is or was occupied by agricultural buildings.

66. The second potentially relevant exception is that set out under NPPF paragraph 15518: the development of homes, commercial and other development in the Green Belt should not be regarded as inappropriate where criteria apply. Wherever caravans are to be stationed for permanent residential use, whether for occupation by Travellers or otherwise, the development would provide homes for the purposes of paragraph 155 – which should also be read as allowing for a mixed use Traveller or Travelling Showperson’s site.

67. The first criterion set out under NPPF paragraph 155 is a): The development would utilise grey belt land and not fundamentally undermine the purposes (taken together) of the remaining Green Belt across the area of the plan. The term ‘grey belt’ is defined in NPPF Annex 2 and subject to advice in the Green Belt ITM. Crucially, grey belt comprises PDL and any other land that, in either case, does not “strongly contribute” to any of the purposes of including land in the Green Belt as set out under (a), b) or d) of the NPPF at paragraph 143.

68. NPPF paragraph 155b) requires that there is a demonstrable unmet need for the type of development proposed. NPPF footnote 56 explains that, in the case of Traveller sites [for occupation by persons with PPTS status], “demonstrable unmet need” means the lack of a five year supply of deliverable traveller sites assessed in line with PPTS.

69. NPPF paragraph 155c) requires that the development would be in a sustainable location, with particular reference to NPPF paragraphs 110 and

115. NPPF footnote 57 adds that, for development to provide Traveller sites, particular reference should also be made to PPTS paragraph 13.

70. The final criterion set out under NPPF paragraph 155 is d): Where applicable the development proposed meets the ‘Golden Rules’ requirements set out in [NPPF] paragraphs 156-157. PPTS paragraph 18 is explicit that the Golden Rules do not apply to Traveller sites. Only criterion (a), (b) and (c) of NPPF paragraph 155 therefore need to be met.

- The appeal would not relate to a material change of use of land to residential use, but only to development associated with a Traveller site – such as the re-use of a building as a day room – which might fall within another exception set out in NPPF paragraph 154.

72. Where the development is inappropriate in the Green Belt by definition under PPTS paragraph 16, it will usually be necessary – in accordance with the Green Belts ITM – to carry out a separate subsequent assessment as whether there would be ‘any other harm’ to the Green Belt in terms of its openness and the purposes of including land in the Green Belt.

73. Any caravan site can be expected to cause a loss of openness in the Green Belt through the (sometime) presence of caravans (at least) on the land. However, the extent of any loss of openness or harm to the purposes of the Green Belt will vary according to the circumstances. The Inspector should identify and attribute weight to the actual (in addition to the definitional) harm for the purposes of the planning balance.

74. Indeed, it is possible that the development will cause no net loss or even a gain in the openness of the Green Belt, depending on the previous use of the site and factors such as:

- The number of caravans on the site and how many would be touring – and regularly taken away for work – and/or static caravans.

- Whether the site would be in a mixed use and, if so, the nature and impact of the non-residential use on Green Belt openness and purposes.

- The proposed or likely requirements for vehicular parking, with regard to the number of pitches and any other use(s).

- The nature and extent of any operational development, including but not limited to hardstanding, utility or day rooms and fencing or walls, that is proposed or likely to be needed.

<!-- Part2 p.129 -->

- The likelihood of domestic ‘paraphernalia’ such as children’s play equipment being used on the site.

- The previous use of the land; any buildings, structures or chattels associated with that use; and whether any existing buildings or structures which presently reduce the openness of the Green Belt would be removed.

- The duration of the use, and whether conditions controlling matters such as landscaping could be reasonably imposed on a temporary permission.

- The openness of the immediate surroundings, or the impact of the development on spatial openness in its context.

- The impact of the development on the visual openness of the Green Belt within the surrounding area.

75. PPTS paragraph 27b) states that weight should be attached to sites being well planned or soft landscaped in such a way as to positively enhance the environment and increase its openness. This applies to all Traveller sites, including but not limited to those in the Green Belt.

Other Considerations and ‘Very Special Circumstances’

76. In accordance with NPPF paragraph 153, inappropriate development in the Green Belt should not be approved except in very special circumstances, which will not exist unless the potential harm to the Green Belt by reason of inappropriateness and any other harm resulting from the proposal is clearly outweighed by other considerations.

77. Other considerations raised in favour of Traveller sites in the Green Belt may include: a shortage or unmet need for Traveller sites in the local authority area; lack of a five year supply of Traveller sites in the local authority area; failure of the local authority to provide Traveller sites through their planning policies; and the personal need for a site and personal circumstances of the appellant or other site occupiers. An appellant may rely on site-specific factors, such as the removal of historic structures to improve openness or ‘sustainability’ benefits.

78. The decision maker must decide where the balance lies in each case, with regard to the substantial weight that must be attached to Green Belt harm (in accordance with the NPPF) and whatever weight is attached to any other harm(s), unmet need, any personal circumstances and/or other considerations. As of December 2024, the planning balance in Green Belt cases is entirely for the decision-maker and not guided by the PPTS.

79. In accordance with the Green Belts ITM Chapter, each ‘other consideration’ must be treated separately and discretely. Each consideration in favour of the appeal should be weighted but not referred to as ‘very special circumstances’ in themselves or compared individually to the identified harm. In the final balance,

<!-- Part2 p.130 -->

it should also be remembered that ‘other considerations’ do not have to be unique, rare or uncommon to amount to very special circumstances19.

80. The hierarchy of weight has often been described as: ‘little’/‘limited’, ‘moderate’, ‘material’/‘significant’ and ‘considerable’ up to ‘substantial’. The Courts have accepted that “the word ‘substantial’ does not denote a greater quantum of weight than significant’” albeit subject to the context20. However, Inspectors should still take care to avoid ambiguity or uncertainty.

81. If the Inspector considers that different factors should carry the same weight, the most straightforward approach is to denote that by using the same words. If different descriptors of weight are to be used, perhaps where ‘significant’ weight is attached to personal circumstances, after ‘substantial weight’ is given (as required) to Green Belt harm, the Inspector should tell the parties clearly if they actually intend ‘significant’ to be read as equal to ‘substantial’.

### Traveller Sites in the Countryside

82. PPTS Policy C, set out in paragraph 14, states that “When assessing the suitability of sites in rural or semi-rural settings, local planning authorities should ensure that the scale of such sites does not dominate the nearest settled community.”

83. Similarly, PPTS Policy H, at paragraph 26, also states that:

“Local planning authorities should very strictly limit new traveller site development in open countryside that is away from existing settlements or outside areas allocated in the development plan21. Local planning authorities should ensure that sites in rural areas respect the scale of, and do not dominate, the nearest settled community, and avoid placing an undue pressure on the local infrastructure.”

84. That the PPTS allows for Traveller sites, in principle, in rural and semi-rural areas should be borne in mind when considering criteria-based local plan policies and matters such as character and appearance or accessibility. Travellers have long tended to live in or on the edge of the countryside, for access to work across a wide area and since they may be excluded from urban sites by reason of land prices and/or community objections.

85. Whether a site is in ‘open’ countryside should be considered in the round with regard to the characteristics of the locality and the position of the site in relation to any settlement boundary or allocated area. PPTS paragraph 26 does not

### Character and Appearance

87. PPTS paragraph 4k) expects authorities and, by extension, Inspectors to have due regard to the protection of local amenity and the environment. It is not uncommon for authorities to object to Traveller sites on the basis of conflict with development plan policies which particularly seek to protect the character and appearance of the countryside.

88. However, as discussed above, PPTS paragraphs 14 and 26 implicitly accept that Traveller sites – with all that they include – may be located in rural areas. If and where the use is acceptable in principle, that must encompass the stationing of caravans which are not uncommonly seen across the countryside on farms and holiday caravan sites as well as established Traveller sites.

89. Thus, the Inspector should decide whether any detraction from or harm to the character and appearance of a rural area would be unacceptable. That finding should be based on an assessment of the scale, characteristics and visual impact of the development in its context, rather than some generalised objection to caravans urbanising the countryside.

90. In R (oao Dowling) v SSCLG & Chichester CC [2007] EWHC 738 (Admin) the judge endorsed an Inspector’s finding that a Traveller site would not result in unacceptable harm although the local plan policy required that Gypsy sites ‘do not detract from the undeveloped and rural character and appearance of the area’. A literal reading of the policy would:

‘…render it unworkable because it is difficult to conceive in practice and reality that there would be any kind of development with regard to Gypsies which would not, at least in some way, detract either from the character, or from the appearance, or from both, of the countryside…it is reasonable to construe the policy as embracing detractions which are perhaps significant or material. That would give the policy real purpose and bite and…make it workable’.

91. PPTS paragraph 27 expects planning applications to be considered with weight attached to specified matters bearing directly or indirectly on the character and appearance of sites:

a) Effective use of previously developed (brownfield), untidy or derelict land, with regard to the NPPF Annex 2 definition of PDL.

<!-- Part2 p.132 -->

b) Sites being well-planned or soft landscaped in such a way as to positively enhance the environment and increase its openness.

c) Promoting opportunities for healthy lifestyles, such as ensuring adequate landscaping and play areas for children.

d) Not enclosing a site with so much hard landscaping, high walls or fences that the impression may be given that the site and its occupants are deliberately isolated from the rest of the community.

92. On travelling showpeople’s sites, it will be necessary to make provision for the secure storage and repair of equipment as an integral part of the whole development. If this cannot be properly assimilated into its surroundings, the entire development may be regarded as unacceptable; the scale and visual impact of the use will be one of the main issues in almost every case.

### Flood Risk and Drainage

93. NPPF Annex 3 classifies caravans, mobile homes and park homes intended for permanent residential use ‘highly vulnerable’ to flood risk. PPTS paragraph 13g) expects that local plan policies – and appeal decisions – ensure that Traveller sites are sustainable economically, socially and environmentally by, amongst other matters, not locating such sites in areas at high risk of flooding, including functional floodplains, given the particular vulnerability of caravans.

94. Any flood risk issue raised in a Traveller or travelling showpeople’s appeal should be determined with regard to the NPPF, the Planning Practice Guidance (PPG) chapter on Flood Risk and Coastal Change and the Flood Risk ITM. NPPF paragraph 181 and footnote 63 provide that a site-specific flood risk assessment (FRA) should support all applications relating to land in Flood Zones 2 and 3, and certain applications relating to Flood Zone 1, including those relating to land that may be subject to other sources of flooding, where its development would introduce a more vulnerable use.

95. NPPF paragraph 176 and footnote 62 expect that the sequential and exception tests are applied as appropriate to any application for a change of use to a caravan, camping, chalet, mobile home or park home site.

96. NPPF paragraph 175 requires use of the sequential test when determining applications relating to areas known to be at risk now or in the future from any form of flooding, except where the site-specific FRA demonstrates that no built development within the site boundary, including access or escape routes, land raising or other potentially vulnerable elements, would be located on the area at risk of flooding from any source, now or in the future.

97. The purpose of the sequential test is to consider whether appropriate sites are reasonably available in areas with a lower risk of flooding. This assessment should be entirely consistent with the assessments of the general need for,

<!-- Part2 p.133 -->

supply of and availability of Traveller sites. It may be that the site-specific FRA does not need to include a detailed sequential assessment if – for example – it is undisputed that there is a shortfall of Traveller sites and none are available.

98. Moreover, in considering future flood risk, regard should be had to whether permission may be granted on a permanent, personal or time-limited basis. It may be appropriate, given the circumstances and the period of the permission, to set aside (rather than ignore) any allowance for climate change in the site specific FRA. The planning balance could change on that basis.

99. The exception test should be applied where, having applied the sequential test, it is not possible for development to be located in an area at lower risk of flooding. However, the need for the exception test will depend on the potential vulnerability of the site and development – and PPG Table 2 indicates that the exception test is not required for highly vulnerable uses in Flood Zone 3.

100. However, if the site is in Flood Zone 2 and the sequential test is passed, a Traveller site would be subject to the exception test. It should be shown that:

a) the development would provide wider sustainability benefits to the community that outweigh the flood risk; and

b) the development will be safe for its lifetime taking account of the vulnerability of its users, without increasing flood risk elsewhere, and, where possible, will reduce flood risk overall.

101. If the exception test is not applicable, regard should still be had to NPPF paragraph 170 which requires that any development which is ‘necessary’ in an area at risk of flooding should ‘be made safe for its lifetime without increasing flood risk elsewhere’. To be clear, paragraph 170 allows for Traveller sites in Flood Zone 3 although PPG Table 2 advises that ‘highly vulnerable development’ should not be permitted there.

102. The Inspector’s findings on whether there are ‘wider sustainability benefits’ – or whether the development is ‘necessary’ – should again be integrated with their findings on the need for, supply of and availability of Traveller sites, plus the PPTS paragraph 13 ‘sustainability criteria’ and, where applicable, the personal need or circumstances of those intending to occupy the site.

103. In considering whether the development would be safe for its lifetime and users, and not increase flood risk elsewhere, regard should be had to all the criteria set out in NPPF paragraph 181.

104. The development of a Traveller site will often involve the laying of hard surfacing which could increase surface water run-off. Traveller sites also often lack connection to mains sewers and require the installation of a septic tank or cesspit. The Inspector should establish the intended foul and surface water

<!-- Part2 p.134 -->

drainage arrangements and whether the development would or could incorporate a sustainable drainage system.

105. NPPF paragraph 182 requires that applications which could affect drainage on or around the site to incorporate sustainable drainage systems, in order to control flow rates and reduce volumes of runoff. The system should be proportionate to the nature and scale of the development and, where possible, provide multifunctional benefits for water quality, biodiversity and amenity.

### Highway Safety

106. Where an issue is raised as to the actual or potential effect of the use of land as a Traveller site on highway safety, regard may need to be had to:

- Characteristics of the (rural) road network including the presence or otherwise of footways and street lighting.

- Local traffic volumes and patterns.

- Visibility splays and other safety issues pertaining to the site access.

- The proposed mix of uses, number of parking spaces and the nature and size of vehicles that would be moved on and off the land.

- Whether or not the number of residential pitches would generate similar trips per day than the equivalent number of dwellings, being in mind that Travellers tend to rely on their private vehicles, but do not commute daily for work, and there may be potential for shared trips on multi-pitch sites.

107. Travelling showpeople’s sites typically require access from a main road for large and slow moving vehicles. The necessary junction design, visibility splays and impacts of projected vehicle movements from proposed Travelling Showpeople’s sites should be assessed on an individual basis.

### Access to Services and Facilities

108. It is not unusual for local authorities to suggest that proposed Traveller sites would lack adequate access to shops or services by foot, bicycle or public transport. Any such objection should be assessed and concluded upon with regard to relevant policies in development plans as well as the evidence relating to the location of nearby services, the provision of footways and cycle paths, the nature of local roads generally and of local public transport services.

109. However, as noted above, Travellers have a variety of reasons for preferring sites in rural or semi-rural areas, and such developments may be permitted under PPTS paragraphs 14 and 26. It is therefore relevant that NPPF paragraph 110 recognises that opportunities to maximise sustainable travel solutions will vary between urban and rural areas. Inspectors should consider

<!-- Part2 p.135 -->

whether any rural Traveller site in the authority area could be reasonably and realistically expected to facilitate access on foot or by bike or public transport.

110. It should also be borne in mind that persons with a nomadic way of life will travel by definition and not work in employment areas that were planned along public transport routes. Wherever they live, Travellers rely on private vehicles for work and often for access to shops and services too. If there is a dispute over accessibility, it may be appropriate to consider the work catchment area and access to the motorway network as well as to local amenities.

111. Inspectors should also have regard PPTS paragraph 13 which is concerned with social, economic and environmental sustainability for Traveller sites. Only two of the criteria set out under paragraph 13 mention travel – and even they do not mention distance or means of transport to local shops and services. Rather, they expect that Traveller sites should:

13d) provide a settled base that reduces both the need for long-distance travelling and possible environmental damage caused by unauthorised encampment.

13h) reflect the extent to which traditional lifestyles (whereby some travellers live and work from the same location thereby omitting many travel to work journeys) can contribute to sustainability.

112. It follows from PPTS paragraph 13d) that, when addressing questions of accessibility or sustainable travel, consideration should be given to whether – on the evidence – a refusal of permission would be liable to generate more or longer journeys by private vehicle due the site occupiers being forced to live by the roadside, potentially further away from shops and services.

113. PPTS paragraph 13 is otherwise concerned with non-travel sustainability issues. Criteria b) and c), which promote ‘access’ to appropriate health services and ensuring that children can attend school on a regular basis, are concerned with ‘access’ in the sense of ability to secure health care and education. Anyone without a fixed address will find it harder to register for and/or sustain health care or education.

114. Appellants often argue that even a remote site will improve access to medical or educational facilities. Subject to the evidence, Inspectors may need to treat such an issue as relevant to sustainability (as well as personal circumstances). PPTS paragraph 4j) aims to enable the provision of suitable accommodation from which Travellers can access education, health, welfare and employment – and there are social benefits to everyone having proper health care, and all children attending school regularly. It may be necessary to address whether such benefits could be expected arise if any Travellers occupied the site.

115. Accordingly, any question as to whether the site would be suitable in ‘access’ terms should be considered in the light of not only the relevant development

<!-- Part2 p.136 -->

plan policies but also material considerations including PPTS paragraph 13. It may be necessary to acknowledge pros and cons to the development before concluding as to whether ‘accessibility’ or ‘sustainability’ in the round is a consideration for or against the appeal, or neutral in the planning balance.

### Living Conditions and Community Integration

116. PPTS paragraph 4k) expects authorities to have due regard to the protection of local amenity and the environment, while paragraph 13e) requires proper consideration of the effect of local environmental quality (such as noise and air quality) on the health and well-being of any Travellers that may locate there or on others as a result of new development.

117. Matters relating to living conditions, such as effect of the development on outlook, light and privacy for occupiers of the site and of adjacent properties, should be considered in the same way as for any residential development, including whether any harm can be overcome by imposing conditions.

118. The same usually applies to noise, although on mixed use Traveller or Travelling Showpeople’s sites, regard should be had to the potential for noise and disturbance from vehicle movements on and off the site, vehicular parking on the site, and any other on-site business activities.

119. It is not unusual for Travellers to enclose their sites with high hedges or walls that may prompt objections on the grounds of visual harm or overshadowing. Conversely, there may be objections that Traveller sites are insufficiently screened from adjoining properties.

120. The acceptability of the boundary treatment should be assessed with regard to PPTS paragraph 27d) which expects that weight is attached to Traveller sites not being enclosed with so much hard landscaping or high walls or fences that the impression may be given that the site and its occupants are deliberately isolated from the rest of the community.

121. PPTS paragraph 27 is also relevant to living conditions insofar as criterion c) expects weight to be attached to the promotion of opportunities for healthy lifestyles, such as ensuring adequate landscaping and play areas for children.

122. PPTS paragraph 13a) seeks to promote peaceful and integrated co-existence between Traveller sites and the local [settled] community. While not all Traveller sites are subject to objections, and there may indeed be letters of support, it is not usual for such appeals to attract considerable complaints from interested parties, including groups and/or politicians.

123. In dealing with concerns as to the impact of a Traveller site on a settled community, regard should be had to:

- The fact that a Traveller site is a residential use.

<!-- Part2 p.137 -->

- Any relevant findings and/or lack of objections on living conditions.

- The Government’s aims, set out in PPTS paragraphs 4e), h) and i), to promote more private traveller site provision, and increase the number of traveller sites (generally) in appropriate locations with planning permission, while reducing tensions between settled and Traveller communities.

- Whether the site, if it is or would be in a rural and semi-rural location, would respect the scale of and not dominate the nearest settled community in accordance with PPTS paragraphs 14 and 26.

- In accordance with PPTS paragraph 13f), whether the development would place undue pressure on (or, conversely, help to sustain) local infrastructure and services.

- Peaceful and integrated co-existence depends, by definition, on Travellers living in the same area as members of the settled community, so that they can interact and have shared interests in the use of shops, schools and community facilities such as churches.

### Intentional Unauthorised Development

124. A Written Ministerial Statement (WMS) made on 27 December 2015 confirms changes to national planning policy, initially made by a “Dear Chief Planning Officer letter”, to make ‘intentional unauthorised development’ a material consideration to be weighed in the determination of planning applications and appeals received since 31 August 2015.

125. In deciding whether there has been, and the weight to be attached to any ‘intentional unauthorised development’, it may be useful to have regard to:

- Whether the appellant did ‘intend’ that the site be unauthorised, given that they have sought to regularise the development by applying and paying the fee for a grant of retrospective planning permission.

- The appellant’s reasons for developing the land without waiting to obtain planning permission, for example, if they had nowhere else to live.

- Whether, or the extent to which, the works carried out exceeded the minimum necessary to site caravans and create a habitable environment.

- The WMS states that, where development is undertaken in advance of obtaining planning permission, there is no opportunity to appropriately limit or mitigate the harm that has already taken place. However, it may be – and so Inspectors should consider whether – any harm caused can in fact be limited or mitigated via planning condition or obligation.

<!-- Part2 p.138 -->

126. It should also be borne in mind, when attributing weight to intentional unauthorised development, that the TCPA90 provides through s73A and s177 for grants of retrospective planning permission. The enforcement code as contained in Part VII of the TCPA90 is intended to be ‘remedial not punitive’.

### Other Issues

127. PPTS does not say that Travellers must occupy a caravan and it has been held by the Court of Appeal that they do not22. However, Gypsies and Travellers have long lived in caravans to facilitate their nomadic lifestyle and tradition, and so PPTS is concerned with the development of caravan sites.

128. In most cases, success at appeal would mean a grant of permission with the word ‘caravan’ or ‘caravans’ somewhere in the description of development, and a condition imposed to restrict the numbers and/or types of ‘caravan’ on the land. Accordingly, it will be necessary to deal with any concerns raised that a relevant structure on the land may not be a caravan23. Regard should be had to the statutory criteria and relevant case law as well as factual evidence from the site visit and/or hearing or inquiry24.

129. Where an appeal is made for a bricks and mortar house for a Traveller family, it should normally be considered in accordance with the development plan and the NPPF as an appeal for general housing. It would rarely be reasonable to restrict occupation of a house to Gypsies and Travellers since they are nomadic by definition or tradition. Permanent buildings should also not be subject to personal conditions as advised by the PPG 'Use of Planning Conditions' and the Conditions ITM.

130. PPTS would probably be of limited relevance even if the new dwelling would be built on or close to an existing Traveller site for the purposes of occupation by a site manager or family member. However, regard should be had to PPTS, as well as personal circumstances, if it is proposed to develop a new Traveller site close to an existing house for family reasons.

131. As in any casework, it is necessary to show that any decision to allow the appeal is made strictly on the merits of the case25.

### Loss of a Traveller site

134. Where planning applications are made for a change of use from a Traveller site, it is not unusual for the authority to refuse permission on the basis of land supply harm through the loss of pitches or plots. Such development may conflict with any local plan policy that seeks to safeguard either existing Gypsy and Traveller and/or travelling showpeople’s sites specifically, or residential land more generally.

135. Even if there is no such policy or conflict, regard should be had to evidence of the need for and supply of the relevant kind of Traveller site – and then weigh in the balance the benefits of the proposed development against the loss of the pitches or plots. If the proposal is to construct bricks and mortar housing on the land, it may be necessary to compare and contrast the five year supply of land for general housing and Traveller sites.

136. If a major development proposal would require the relocation of a Traveller site, whether permanently or temporarily, PPTS paragraph 22 expects local authorities to work with the applicant and affected Traveller community to identify a suitable site or sites. If and where the development to be displaced was lawful, the applicant should identify and provide an alternative site.
