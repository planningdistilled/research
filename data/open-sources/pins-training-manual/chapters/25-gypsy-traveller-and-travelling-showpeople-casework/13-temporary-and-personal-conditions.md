# 25 Gypsy, Traveller and Travelling Showpeople Casework: Temporary and Personal Conditions

<!-- Part2 p.162 -->

## Temporary and Personal Conditions

240. When considering an appeal for a change of use of land for a Traveller or Travelling Showpeople’s site, appellants will often ask for a grant of permission on a permanent basis in the first instance or, failing that, a time-limited basis. Even if the appellant does not make that point, the Inspector should address the possibility of granting a temporary or personal permission for the site76.

241. As with any other casework, most Traveller appeals will be dismissed or allowed on a permanent basis. Where the latter outcome would be

242. The weight attached to all considerations should be revisited when the question is whether to allow the development on a time-limited basis. In Moore v SSCLG [2013] EWCA Civ 1194, the Inspector concluded against a grant of permanent and then temporary permission. In considering the latter, they gave increased weight to unmet need and reduced harm to the Green Belt, but did not reconsider the weight attached to visual harm or personal circumstances, or make clear findings as to what would happen to the appellant.

243. The Court of Appeal held that there was ample material before the Inspector on which to make a finding on the likelihood of roadside camping if permission was refused – and it was likely on the facts. That was important to the ‘harm’ side of the balance as well human rights considerations; the Inspector had recognised that roadside camping would probably be equally harmful to the Green Belt and potentially more harmful to the countryside than the appeal development.

244. The Court of Appeal thus held that:

“…the grant of temporary permission would still result in the harm identified by the inspector, and it may not be strictly accurate to describe that harm as being cancelled out or neutralised by the harm that would result from the refusal of temporary permission, but the overall balance would necessarily be affected if the harm resulting from the refusal of temporary permission would be equal to or greater than the harm resulting from…such permission.

“…it is far from inevitable that the Inspector would have reached the same conclusion if [they] had made a finding on the likelihood of roadside camping and followed through its implications…[the] refusal of temporary planning permission was [not] a reasonable reflection of the factors [the Inspector] was required to take into account in that context”.

245. In Ward v SSLUHC [2024] EWHC 676 (Admin), an Inspector’s decisions to refuse permanent and temporary planning permission for a Traveller site in the Green Belt were upheld; the Court found that the Inspector considered all the relevant factors and reached rational conclusions that were clearly open to them. However, that judgment again reinforces that, when considering whether to grant temporary (and/or personal) permission, Inspectors should consider the harms and benefits in the light of the duration of the development and a realistic assessment of what will happen to the site occupiers.

246. It will always be necessary to explain why a temporary condition is to be imposed and why it will last for whatever period is specified. There should be a

As noted above, account may need to be taken of anticipated changes in the circumstances across a wider area than just that of the local authority79.

247. The PPG states that circumstances where a temporary permission may be appropriate include where it is expected that the planning circumstances will change in a particular way at the end of that period80. Regard should be had to the likelihood of any change that may occur during the potential timescale of a temporary permission in respect of any of the main issues for the appeal, particularly in respect of the supply and availability of sites. Changes that may be anticipated can include the adoption of an emerging plan.

248. The period of the temporary condition will depend upon the circumstances of the case, but often depends on when alternative sites seem likely to become available. Relatively few temporary permissions have been granted for more than three years, but a longer period may occasionally be justified.

249. The PPG allows that permission may be granted on ‘exceptional occasions… for development that would not normally be permitted on the site…because of who would benefit from the permission’81. As indicated above, if a grant of permission is justified in whole or part because of personal need and circumstances, the permission should be subject to a personal condition which names the beneficiaries and refers to their dependants.

250. Those named in the condition need not be restricted to or even include the appellant. The condition should list the names of the leading members of each family or group per pitch; where the leading members are an adult couple, their names should be separated by an ‘and/or’ (eg, Henry and/or Mary Smith) to take account of possible family breakdown or death.

251. The condition should refer to the ‘dependants’ of the leading members of the family group – but not name them in case, for example, more children are born. The implication of using the term dependant is that when and if those people are no longer dependant on the named individuals, or when those named are no longer resident, the continued occupation of the site by the one-time dependants is in breach of that condition.

252. A personal condition should apply for the lifetime of the beneficiaries but may be adapted so that it can be imposed alongside a temporary condition.

However, personal conditions are time-limited in any event because the named beneficiaries may move or will eventually die.

253. It follows that personal and/or temporary conditions should be worded to ensure that the use is ceased and the land restored to its previous condition in accordance with a scheme to be submitted and agreed at the end of the relevant period. This is so that the authority can enforce against any continued use of the land in breach of the temporary or personal condition.

254. The condition should include an early timetable for the submission of the restoration scheme, when the previous state of the land can be more easily established, the site occupants are present and there is a clear incentive for them to avoid the potentially serious consequences of not complying with the condition. The submission of a scheme at the end of time-limited condition is less likely, and moreover a scheme that is approved early will be enforceable against any subsequent owners of the land.

255. When considering a grant temporary or personal permission, the Inspector will need to address what other conditions would be necessary and reasonable given the scale and nature of any works that might be required and the duration of the permission. For example:

- If highway safety concerns could only be overcome through significant alterations to the site access, consider whether it would be reasonable to impose the burden of the works on the appellant when the duration of the permission would be short – and if not, whether temporary permission should be granted at all.

- If harm to the character of the area would be mitigated but not overcome by landscaping, and it would not be reasonable to impose the burden of the works on the appellant when the duration of the permission would be short, consider whether the condition is necessary at all, bearing in mind that the shorter duration of the permission will also mitigate harm.

256. The PPG states that imposing conditions on planning permissions for a change of use so as to require the demolition of buildings are unlikely to relate fairly and reasonably to the development permitted82. It may be necessary to canvas with the parties what elements of the proposed development should be permitted and/or required by condition in the event that the decision is to grant temporary and/or personal permission:

- Whether day or utility rooms could be provided in temporary structures.

- Whether hardstanding could be required to be removed.

- What drainage facilities and/or boundary treatments would be required.
