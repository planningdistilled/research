# 39 Minerals: National Policy and Guidance

<!-- Part3 p.323 -->

## National Policy and Guidance

### National Planning Policy Framework August 2026

Chapter 11 – Facilitating the Sustainable Use of Minerals.

18. The provisions of Chapter 11 and relevant Glossary definitions at Annex B of the NPPF should be studied directly but they are briefly summarised as follows.

19. The introductory text to Chapter 11 states that a sufficient supply of minerals is essential to provide the infrastructure, buildings, green energy and goods that the country needs. It further recognises that minerals are a finite natural resource which can only be worked where they occur, and therefore that best use should be made of them to secure their long-term conservation.

20. For plan-making, Policy M1 states that development plans should provide for a sufficient and timely supply of minerals of local and national importance. It excludes the identification of new sites or extensions to existing sites for peat and coal extraction and promotes the use of substitute, secondary and recycled materials and minerals waste in preference to the extraction of primary materials, whilst aiming to source minerals supplies indigenously. Secondary materials include colliery spoil and furnace ash or slag, while recycled materials arise mainly from construction and demolition waste.

21. Policies M2 and M6 require the designation of Mineral Safeguarding Areas (MSAs) and, in two-tier authorities, Mineral Consultation Areas (MCAs). Policy M6 states that proposals for non-mineral development should not constrain the potential future use of mineral resources or associated minerals infrastructure, thereby helping to prevent the sterilisation of mineral resources.

22. For determining planning applications and appeals, Policy M3 states that substantial weight should be given to the benefits of mineral extraction and processing, including to the economy. In relation to plan-making, Policy M1 states that, where further extraction is necessary, landbanks of non-energy minerals should, so far as practical, be maintained through sites located outside National Parks, the Broads, National Landscapes and designated heritage assets, whilst recognising that minerals can only be worked where they are found.

23. Minimum aggregate landbanks of required supply should be maintained equivalent to 7 years for sand and gravel and 10 years for crushed rock.

<!-- Part3 p.324 -->

Unacceptable environmental impacts should be avoided and no new permissions should be granted for peat extraction. Early site restoration should be sought. Flexible provision is made for small-scale quarrying of heritage building and roofing stone.

24. Policy M1(3)(b) requires that minimum reserves should be maintained of at least 10 years for individual silica sand sites, 15 years for primary cement materials (chalk and limestone) and secondary cement materials (clay and shale), and 25 years for brick clay.

25. In terms of maintaining a sufficient and timely supply of aggregate minerals, Policy M1(2)(a) requires Mineral Planning Authorities (MPAs) to prepare an annual Local Aggregate Assessment (LAA), either individually or jointly, to forecast future demand based on a rolling average of ten years' sales data, other relevant local information and an assessment of all supply options. The policy also requires authorities to take account of the advice of Aggregate Working Parties (AWPs), the National Aggregate Co-ordinating Group (NACG), and any published national or sub-national guidelines on future aggregate supply.

26. With respect to maintaining a sufficient and timely supply of industrial minerals, Policy M1(3) requires Mineral Planning Authorities to use evidence of existing site capacities and demand, and to cooperate with neighbouring and more distant authorities to identify how future requirements can be met. The policy also requires the maintenance of appropriate reserves of industrial minerals, including a minimum 25-year reserve of brick clay, and recognises the need for brick clay to be sourced from a number of different locations to enable appropriate blends to be produced.

27. With reference to hydrocarbons, Policy M1(4) states that Mineral Planning Authorities should distinguish between, and plan for, the three phases of oil and gas development: exploration, appraisal and production, within licensed areas. The policy also states that development plans should not identify new sites or extensions to existing sites outside licensed areas.

28. In relation to coal, Policy M1(5) states that development plans should not identify new sites or extensions for coal extraction, while Policy M5 provides that proposals for surface or underground coal workings should be refused unless they are necessary for specified purposes, such as public safety, ground stabilisation, methane capture, or the extraction of other minerals of national or local importance.

Chapter 13 (GB1-GB8) – Protecting Green Belt Land

29. Policy GB6 of the NPPF states that development in the Green Belt is inappropriate unless it falls within one of the categories identified in Policy GB7. Policy GB7(1)(f)(i) confirms that mineral extraction and processing, together with engineering operations, are not inappropriate development in the Green

<!-- Part3 p.325 -->

Belt, provided that the impact on the openness of the Green Belt is minimised and there would not be a significant conflict with the purposes of including land within the Green Belt.

Chapter 19, N1- 6 – Conserving and Enhancing the Natural Environment

Major Development

30. Within Chapter 19, Policy N4(2) states that proposals for major development within Protected Landscapes, including National Parks, the Broads and National Landscapes (formerly Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty (AONBs)), should be refused other than in exceptional circumstances and where it can be demonstrated that the development is in the public interest. In determining whether exceptional circumstances exist, consideration should be given to the need for the development, the scope for meeting that need outside the Protected Landscape, and the potential environmental, landscape and recreational impacts of the proposal.

31. Mineral extraction is often allocated or proposed within Protected Landscapes, including National Parks, the Broads and National Landscapes, where the mineral resource can only be worked in that location and is required to maintain a sufficient supply of minerals to meet an identified need.

32. Footnote 59 of the NPPF states that, for the purposes of Policies N4 and N5, whether a proposal constitutes 'major development' is a matter for the decision-maker, taking into account its nature, scale and setting, and whether it could have a significant adverse impact on the statutory purposes for which the area has been designated or defined.

### Planning Practice Guidance

### General

33. The PPG Chapter 27 – Minerals – provides the prime source of guidance for all mineral planning work and should be studied directly. The advice of PPG Chapter 27 most frequently relied upon in connection with minerals planning policy formulation and appeals is summarised below. This includes advice on safeguarding and buffer zones and, planning for extraction by designating specific sites, Preferred Areas or Areas of Search, as well as assessing and dealing with environmental impacts, including by Environmental Impact Assessment, and on securing appropriate and timely site restoration.

### Aggregate Minerals

34. In order to ensure that minerals are provided where they are needed the NPPF and PPG provided a framework for a Managed Aggregate Supply System (MASS). The MASS works through local, regional and national partnerships, with MPAs preparing Local Aggregate Assessments (LAAs) and contributing to

<!-- Part3 p.326 -->

Regional Aggregate Working Parties (RAWPs) and the National Aggregate Co-ordinating Group, comprising RAWP members, monitoring overall provision, all underpinned by MPA landbanks.

35. The MASS seeks to ensure a steady and adequate supply of aggregate mineral to handle the significant geographical imbalances in the occurrence of suitable natural aggregate resources, and the areas where they are most needed. It requires MPAs with adequate resources of aggregates to make an appropriate contribution to national as well as local supply, while making due allowance for the need to control and limit environmental damage to an acceptable level. It also ensures that areas with smaller amounts of aggregate make some contribution towards meeting local and national need, where that can be done sustainably.

### Local Aggregate Assessments

36. In order to provide forecasts of future aggregate demand to inform policies in minerals local plans, annual individual or joint LAAs for different types of aggregate are usually produced by the relevant MPA. These should comprise forecasts based on rolling average 10-years mineral sales data and, importantly, other relevant local information, analysis of supply options, and assessment of the balance of demand and supply, to conclude on whether there is a surplus or shortfall and how the latter is being addressed.

37. Other local information would include levels of planned construction and housebuilding and other local evidence relating to future demand. Data sources would include the Annual Minerals Raised Inquiry Survey, the national four-yearly Aggregate Minerals Survey, local construction and demolition arisings and recovery data from the Environment Agency, annual RAWP reports, advice from the National Aggregate Co-ordinating Group (NACG), MPA Annual Monitoring Reports (AMRs), national and sub-national guidelines on future aggregates provision and British Geological Survey and Crown Estate data. Such sources are consistent with the requirements of Policy M1(2)(a) of the NPPF.

38. Supply options considered should include recycled and secondary aggregates from construction, demolition and industrial waste, marine aggregates from areas licenced by the Marine Management Organisation (MMO), imports and exports, as captured by the national four-yearly Aggregate Minerals Survey, and land-won resources in landbanks and local plan allocations.

39. In the examination of local plans, the calculation methodology used by the MPA to determine the LAA is often a contentious matter. The minerals industry may often argue that the calculation methodology used under-estimates that future demand. The LAA will usually be an important supporting document that underpins emerging polices and site allocations in a local plan. It will likely be discussed in the examination and therefore Inspectors need to ensure that they are familiar with this document and that its calculation basis is robust.

### Aggregate Landbanks

40. Landbanks, recalculated each year, are principally a monitoring tool to provide the MPA with early warning of possible disruption to the provision of a steady and adequate supply of land-won aggregates in their particular area and as a trigger to review current provision and whether to review the allocation of sites in the local plan, taking into account remaining planned provision. A low landbank informs a local plan examination or the determination of mineral development proposal as to the importance of the proposal to the maintenance of supply.

41. Separate landbanks are required for crushed rock and sand and gravel because they serve different markets and have different site infrastructure requirements. In general, quarries producing rock aggregates need a longer security of reserves to justify capital investment.

42. Where there is a distinct market for a specific type or quality of aggregate, such as high-specification rock, or soft sand used for concrete or mortar, a separate landbank may be justified based on the market for that material, where materials of different physical properties are needed for different end uses.

### Industrial Minerals

43. The PPG (27-086-090) provides detailed guidance on planning for industrial minerals, depending upon their geological and other characteristics, and on the calculation and significance of existing stocks, mainly in connection with specific planning applications.

### Hydrocarbons

44. The PPG (27-091-148) provides detailed advice on many aspects of hydrocarbon development planning. This requires detailed study depending on casework encountered but the main salient points are briefly summarised in the following paragraphs.

45. The PPG distinguishes conventional hydrocarbons (oil and gas from sandstone or limestone strata) and unconventional hydrocarbons (oil and gas from shale or coal measures) and the three separate phases of exploration, assessment and testing and production, each requiring planning permission. Such permissions can only be granted in areas subject to Government-issued Petroleum Exploration and Development Licences (PEDL) (pursuant to the Petroleum Act 1998) in competitive offerings (licence rounds) which grant exclusivity to operators who receive a licence in the area.

46. PEDL licences do not give consent for drilling or any other operations. An operator must seek planning permission from the MPA to drill and carry out operations. An operator can only seek planning permission in areas covered by a licence.

<!-- Part3 p.328 -->

47. Local plans should provide for hydrocarbon development based on available data, including identification of PEDL Areas, and use criteria-based policies or identify specific sites. In considering policies for hydrocarbon development MSAs are not necessary due to the depth of deposits.

48. The determination of applications for hydrocarbon development must take account of Government energy policy as set out in its Annual Energy Statement. For applications for unconventional hydrocarbon development in National Landscapes and National Parks great weight must be given their conservation, with major development justified only exceptionally in circumstances of demonstrable public interest and mitigated by conditions. In this connection, the PPG sets down model conditions.

### Mineral Planning Orders

49. As minerals development can occur over a long period of time, there may be changes in local circumstances where a MPA considers that further controls on such development are necessary. The PPG (27-149-177) provides guidance on a range of Minerals Planning Orders that can be used by MPAs to control existing minerals development.

50. Under Sections 97 and 100 and Part II of Schedule 5 and Schedule 9 of the Act, MPAs or by default the Secretary of State may use a range of Orders to control Minerals development by revocation, modification, discontinuance, prohibition of resumption or suspension, subject to consideration of the development plan and other material considerations. These are powers of last resort, may involve compensation and should not be used as an alternative to periodic review of minerals planning conditions.

51. Where there are objections to a Modification or Revocation Order, these are heard by a Planning Inspector at inquiry, hearing or by written representations, according to circumstances, under Section 98 of the Act. However, such appeals are rare and would likely be determined by a specialist minerals Inspector.

### Review of Mineral Planning Conditions

52. The Environment Act 1995 requires the MPA to review mineral planning permissions granted between June 1948 and February 1982, and to impose periodic reviews of permissions granted after February 1982. The purpose being to enable the MPA to impose modern operating, restoration and after-use conditions on these ‘old’ permissions. This process operates periodically, whereby each active site should be reviewed every 15 years. However, this frequency has since been relaxed under the Growth and Infrastructure Act 2013 which enables site operators to request a deferment where there has been no material change in circumstances.

<!-- Part3 p.329 -->

53. This ongoing updating process is known as the ‘Review of Old Mineral Permissions’ (ROMP). Like standard planning applications for mineral development, the ROMP applications for new schemes of conditions go through statutory consultation and administrative procedures before they are determined. Normally ROMP applications will be accompanied by an Environment Statement which assesses the likely environmental impact of the development.

54. Dormant and working minerals sites are subject to periodic review of planning conditions. Development of the whole or part of a dormant site cannot lawfully commence until conditions have been agreed by the MPA or at appeal, often subject to an Environmental Impact Statement where required under the EIA Regulations.

55. The PPG includes model forms of application and appeal and a procedural flowchart for minerals conditions reviews. The flowchart is copied at Annex B below.

56. The PPG provides guidance on the Review of Minerals Planning Conditions. An operator may appeal against the determination of conditions by the MPA on grounds that they are unreasonable or that there would be an impact on viability without compensation. This procedure may give rise to appeals to the Secretary of State which are invariably allocated to minerals specialists.

57. There are three main restrictions on planning conditions imposed at review: they should comply with the usual policy tests and not affect the viability of the site; they should impose a condition that the winning and working of minerals or depositing of mineral waste must cease not later than 21 February 2042, except where the original permission is already time-limited; and they should not with-draw permitted development rights other for exceptional reasons1.

58. There is no provision for compensation for the review process unless the MPA imposes different conditions from those submitted or viability or asset value is unreasonably affected.

## Minerals Local Plans

### General

59. The Mineral Planning Authority (MPA) is either the County Council in two-tier authority areas or a unitary authority or a National Park authority. Under Section 28 of the 2004 Act, two or more local planning authorities may combine to prepare a joint local plan and the format can variously include separate minerals, waste or minerals and waste local plans or such matters may be encompassed as a section of a general local plan.

<!-- Part3 p.330 -->

60. A specialist minerals (or waste) local plan is subject to the same requirements as a general plan to demonstrate compliance with the Duty to Co-operate, Sustainability Appraisal, Strategic Flood Risk Assessment, Equality, Habitats and Climate Change Regulations etc.

61. A minerals local plan should include a geographical and geological and strategic overview with vision and objectives for the sustainable winning, processing, transportation and use of minerals occurring or required in the plan area, linked to economic growth.

### Main areas of concern in plan making

62. In practice most mineral planning matters relate to maintaining adequate landbanks for the supply of aggregates, whether of sand, gravel or crushed rock, over and above existing supplies of land-won or recycled sources.

63. Main areas of concern are invariably the quantum of the plan requirements for individual minerals and especially aggregates and the selection of sites to form the requisite landbank to meet them.

64. Demand for aggregates has fluctuated over time according to economic circumstances with implications for the calculation of local plan requirements but in the longer-term, demand is predicted to increase, whilst replenishment rates for land-won and marine aggregates are well below 100%, implying future supply deficits.

### The nature of aggregates

65. Primary Aggregates are formed of crushed rock or sand and gravel deposits. There are three main rock types:

- sedimentary rocks - formed by settlement of particles or organic remains such as limestone or gritstone;

- igneous rocks - solidified from volcanic sources such as granite and basalt; and

- metamorphic rocks - resulting from heat and pressure due to subsequent geological activity.

66. Sands and gravels result from deposition by water or glacial ice and are shallower, resulting in relatively short-term extraction and restoration.

### Aggregate Landbanks

67. In some local plans, in addition to the minimum 7-year sand and gravel and 10-year crushed rock landbanks required by Policy M1(2)(b) of the NPPF, landbanks may be further subdivided for aggregate materials with a distinct and separate market. This can include, for example, soft sand (used for building and mortar sand) and sharp sand and gravel (used for concrete), where

<!-- Part3 p.331 -->

geological, resource, market and end-use characteristics justify separate consideration. Such distinctions can be technically contentious, as they may influence judgements regarding the allocation or permitting of mineral sites, including proposals within Protected Landscapes.

68. Examples of local plan examination reports dealing with the question of separate soft sand landbanks are provided at Annex A.

### Sources of data

69. The Government undertakes a four-yearly Aggregate Minerals Survey of regional and national sales, inter–regional flows, transportation, consumption and permitted reserves of primary aggregates, to inform review and update of guidance. The latest Survey took place in 2020.

70. There are seven Regional Aggregates Working Parties (RAWPs) in England, comprising local and national government officers and industry stakeholders. They are provided with Government apportionment figures as a basis for the amounts of aggregates each of their component MPAs should aim to provide in their local plans and provide sales figures for annual Local Aggregate Assessments (LAAs) of demand and supply in the MPA area. The NPPF also now requires authorities to take account of the advice of the National Aggregate Co-ordinating Group (NACG), where appropriate.

71. The LAA is an essential and crucial component of the evidence base for any local plan involving provision for land-won aggregates, to identify the plan requirements for aggregate production by forecasting demand, and assessing existing supply. The LAA is therefore likely to be the focus of representations and testing at examination.

72. The starting point for the LAA is a rolling 10-year sales average, together with other relevant local information, always taking into account recycled, secondary and marine sources, as well as imports and exports.

### Areas for future mineral working

73. The PPG states that MPAs should designate specific sites as a first priority, followed by Preferred Areas, and lastly Areas of Search. The approach taken to identify future areas for mineral working will need to be justified. The evidence base will include detailed site search, evaluation and selection evidence, often in combination with the Strategic Environmental Assessment (SEA) or Sustainability Appraisal (SA), using the traffic light or RYG categorisation, similar to housing land availability assessments. This evidence will often be challenged in detail at examination.

74. Plans should also include a criteria-based, windfall policy for flexibility as well as policies setting any necessary Mineral Safeguarding Areas (MSAs) or

<!-- Part3 p.332 -->

Mineral Consultation Areas (MCAs) in two-tier authority areas, where the District planning authority is a statutory consultee on minerals planning.

75. All such allocations must appear on the submitted Proposals Map for later incorporation on the Policies Map, the latter not being part of the plan under examination.

### Major Development

76. Any mineral extraction is invariably regarded, at both the plan-making and application stages, as major development and it is often allocated or proposed within National Parks, the Broads or National Landscapes as the only location from which adequate supplies can be obtained to meet identified need.

77. In such cases there is a clear tension between the principles of Chapter 11 and Policy M1, which recognise that minerals can only be worked where they are found and that a sufficient supply of minerals must be maintained, and Policy N4, which resists major development within Protected Landscapes, including National Parks, the Broads and National Landscapes, other than in exceptional circumstances and where it can be demonstrated to be in the public interest, taking account of need, local economic considerations, the scope for meeting the need elsewhere, and any detrimental effects on the environment, landscape or recreational opportunities and the extent to which these can be moderated.

78. Under the Advearse2 case, the question as to whether a proposal is for major development, and whether exceptional circumstances are demonstrated, is for the decision maker and the judgment is clear that this applies at the application stage, even where a site is allocated in the statutory development plan.

79. So, whilst this is a key test for all mineral applications in National Parks, the Broads or National Landscapes, it is invariably considered at the plan making stage where, to ensure the effectiveness and soundness of the plan, mineral allocations in such areas are subject to scrutiny to assess the prospect of a future application being exceptionally justified in practice to meet the need identified. This an area of potential dispute at examination.

80. An example of a national park examination report dealing with major development is provided at Annex A.

Recycled, secondary and alternative marine-dredged aggregates.

81. There is sustainability and climate change imperative to minimise inland extraction of minerals. As a result, submitted plans often contain policies that promote the use of secondary, recycled or alternative marine-dredged materials. However, monitoring of and data for the production and availability of alternatives is not of the same order of extent or quality as for primary

aggregates. Consequently, the contribution that alternative minerals may be able to make to future supply is usually not supported by reliable quantitative data as to their availably or inherent quality and suitability. For the same reason MPAs tend to avoid reliance upon secondary sources in determining overall primary aggregate requirements.

### Borrow pits

82. Borrow pits are temporary mineral workings opened locally to supply material for one or more specific construction projects requiring substantial amounts of aggregate over a relatively short period, typically road building schemes or reservoir construction. A main advantage is the minimisation of bulk road transport and resulting emissions.

83. In most cases therefore, it is preferable to open a borrow pit close to the project site to avoid the need to import material by road from further afield.

84. Borrow pits can also be used for the deposit of inert waste arising from the construction project to achieve restoration of the borrow pit.

85. Local plans should include a policy covering the provision and control of borrow pits wherever their use is likely to be necessary during the plan period, depending on anticipated engineering construction projects in the plan area, especially road building.

### Safeguarding of mineral resources and facilities

86. The planning system should safeguard proven mineral deposits with actual current or foreseeable economic importance. Local plans should therefore contain policies to prevent the sterilisation of such assets by non-mineral development. Such policies should refer to the resource without presumption as to whether they will be worked and should set out how various types of non-mineral development proposals within MSAs and MCAs are to be assessed, including by providing for prior extraction.

87. NPPF Policy M2 and M6 states that local planning authorities should not normally permit other development proposals in MSAs defined in the minerals local plan if it might constrain potential future use for mineral working. This reflects the long-established principle that non-mineral development should not prejudice the future working of safeguarded mineral resources.

88. In two-tier authority areas, responsibility for safeguarding mineral reserves, facilities and sites in local plans rests largely with the district planning authority, based upon MCAs defined in the minerals local plan.

89. Local plans should also provide for the safeguarding of existing, planned and potential mineral storage, handling and transport sites to ensure their

<!-- Part3 p.334 -->

availability if needed and prevent sensitive or inappropriate development that would conflict with the use of these sites.

90. Planning authorities should also consider the safeguarding of sites for the processing and distribution of recycled and secondary aggregates.

91. Appropriate buffer zones should be added to MSAs and MCAs according to the extent of the mineral reserve safeguarded, or the likely zone of influence of a processing facility.

92. Guidance on the designation of MSAs and MCAs is provided by British Geological Survey3. The evidence base for their designation should include a Mineral Resource Assessment and supporting Mineral Studies and Site Profiles, using a criterion-based system of selection.

93. Dispute can arise at examination as to whether the constraint of a MSA or MCA is justified in the case of any particular reserve or facility, or whether a buffer zone is necessary.

94. Examples of local plan minerals safeguarding policies and a minerals safeguarding DPD are provided at Annex A.

### Brick clay and fire clay

95. Most brickworks are located close to a brick clay quarry but may also import other materials to produce a range of products. The M1(3)(b)(iii) of NPPF requires a 25-year landbank for brick clay because modern brickmaking requires high capital investment and depends upon raw materials of consistent quality.

96. Fireclays are mudstones that underlie most coal seams. They are predominantly used in the manufacture of cream/buff bricks. Fireclay is usually won by prior extraction permitted in conjunction with surface coal mining operations. The importance of conserving fireclay resources is recognised in Policies M5(2)(b) and M5(3) of the NPPF, which provide for the separate extraction and, where necessary, stockpiling of fireclay when coal is removed incidentally to facilitate its recovery.

### Silica sand

97. Silica sand, or industrial sand, contains a high proportion of quartz and is an essential raw material for the glass and foundry casting industries, as well as the manufacture of ceramics and chemicals, and for water filtration.

98. Silica sand is a scarce and high-value resource of national importance and quarries serve a wider geographical market than other types of sand. The NPPF therefore requires the maintenance of reserves of at least 10 years for

individual silica sand sites, increasing to at least 15 years where significant new capital investment is required.

### Building and roofing stone

99. Natural building stone comprises naturally-occurring rocks that are able to be cut, shaped or split into blocks or slabs for use as walling stone, roofing slate and architectural masonry.

100. Policy M3(1) of the NPPF recognises the importance of maintaining a supply of building and roofing stone for the repair or extension of historic buildings and structures, and where the use of traditional materials is important to conserve or enhance the character of an area. The policy also recognises that planning permissions may need sufficient flexibility to accommodate the intermittent or low rate of working at many building stone quarries.

101. There are about 300 building stone quarries in England, usually small and worked intermittently but often located in National Landscapes and National Parks where particular policy constraints apply, including for major development (above).

### Coal

102. There are now no deep mines and very few, if any, surface coal mining sites operational in England. The NPPF now adopts a more restrictive approach to coal extraction. Plan-Making Policy M1(5) in NPPF states that development plans should not identify new sites or extensions to existing sites for coal extraction.

103. Decision-Making Policy M5 of the NPPF provides that proposals for surface or underground coal workings should be refused unless they are necessary for specified purposes, including public safety, ground stabilisation, methane capture, or the extraction of other minerals where coal removal is incidental to that purpose. Nevertheless, coal remains identified as a mineral resource of local and national importance and local plan policies may still be required in areas where coal resources are present.

104. Prior extraction from shallow coal measures may be an issue in non-mineral development allocations and proposals.

### Peat

105. In view of the NPPF policy that development plans should not identify new sites or extensions to existing peat extraction sites, and that proposals for peat extraction at new or extended sites should be refused, local plan policies are only likely to be required for the management of existing sites. This might include consideration of extensions of time for operations involving restoration, as distinct from physical extensions laterally or in depth.

### Industrial minerals

106. Dependent on local geology, local plans might contain policies including for gypsum, salt, potash, china clay (ball clay), vein minerals such as lead or fluorspar or industrial chalk.

107. For cement manufacture, the policy M1(3) in the NPPF requires the maintenance of reserves of at least 15 years for primary (chalk and limestone) and secondary (clay and shale) raw materials to support an existing plant, extending to at least 25 years where this is needed to support a new kiln.

### Onshore oil and gas

108. Oil and natural gas resources are broadly divided into conventional resources or reserves, within sandstone or limestone strata, and unconventional within shale strata, requiring extraction by hydraulic fracturing (fracking), or coal measures containing methane.

109. The NPPF policies M4-M5 recognises the role of these resources in energy supplies, notwithstanding the national target of net zero carbon emissions by 2050 and requires policies to be put in place for the exploration, appraisal and production of onshore oil and gas within licensed areas. However, it now adopts a more restrictive approach to extraction proposals, which should be refused unless they are necessary for specified purposes.

110. The regulatory process of obtaining consent to drill an oil well is the same for conventional as for unconventional hydrocarbons. The Government issues Petroleum Exploration and Development Licences (PEDLs) to specific operators under the Petroleum Act 1998 in competitive licence rounds. However, an operator must still seek planning permission from the MPA to drill and carry out operations within the area covered by the licence.

111. Local Plans should identify PEDL areas to be defined on the Policies Map and include criteria-based policies for the location and assessment of hydrocarbon development proposals within such areas. Existing hydrocarbon extraction sites should also be identified, and future locations promoted by the industry.

112. Policies should cover separately the three phases of hydrocarbon mineral development of exploration, appraisal and testing of identified deposits, and production, usually involving additional wells and facilities.

113. Draft local plan provisions for hydrocarbon mineral proposals often give rise to widespread public concern and misunderstanding of likely effects, especially when they involve fracking. This results in high volumes of objection, including from organised opposition groups, often legally represented. It may be helpful to establish statements of common ground in dealing with these concerns.

114. An example of a local plan hydrocarbon policy is provided at Annex A.

### Restoration of mineral workings

115. Timely restoration of mineral sites to beneficial use is a national policy requirement. Policy M4(1)(c) of the NPPF requires that planning proposals should provide for restoration and aftercare at the earliest opportunity, to be carried out to high environmental standards through the application of appropriate conditions. However, no guidance is provided as to what may constitute ‘high environmental standards’ and MPAs often interpret this as meaning a restoration that is appropriate for the locality.

116. When determining applications and appeals for mineral extraction proposals, Policy M4(1)(c) of the NPPF requires that such proposals should provide for restoration and aftercare at the earliest opportunity, to be carried out to high environmental standards through the application of appropriate conditions. It further advises that bonds or other financial guarantees to underpin planning conditions should only be sought in exceptional circumstances.

### Development management policies

117. Development management policies may be necessary to cover any of the following matters:

- noise (associated with all aspects of the operation)

- dust;

- air quality;

- lighting;

- visual impact on the local and wider landscape;

- landscape character;

- archaeological and heritage features ;

- traffic;

- risk of contamination to land;

- soil resources;

- geological structure;

- impact on best and most versatile agricultural land;

- blast vibration;

- flood risk;

- land stability/subsidence;

- internationally, nationally or locally designated wildlife sites, protected habitats and species, and ecological networks;

- impacts on Nationally Protected Landscapes (National Parks, the Broads and National Landscapes);

- nationally protected geological and geo-morphological sites and features;

- site restoration and aftercare;

- surface and, in some cases, ground water issues

- water abstraction
