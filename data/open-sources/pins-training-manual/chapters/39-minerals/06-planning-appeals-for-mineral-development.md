# 39 Minerals: Planning Appeals for Mineral Development

<!-- Part3 p.338 -->

## Planning Appeals for Mineral Development

118. There are relatively few planning appeals involving mineral development and, when they do arise, the national policy, practice guidance and practical advice provided above in connection with mineral issues in local plans applies in general to appeals.

119. Appeals under Sections 78 and 174 of the Act are approached in essentially the same way as any other planning appeal, subject to the particular national provisions applicable for minerals.

120. Planning issues in minerals appeals are likely to relate to: noise, vibration, blasting, dust, air quality, traffic generation, effects on groundwater, flood risk and ground stability, in particular with respect to hydraulic fracturing (fracking).

121. Mineral planning applications and appeals are likely to require Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA). Advice on dealing with Environmental Statements (ESs) is provided in EIA Chapter of the ITM and ESs are assessed for adequacy and legal compliance by the Planning Inspectorate Environmental Services Team (EST).

### Designated areas

122. Most mineral development necessarily takes place in rural areas, often subject to protective designations such as National Parks, the Broads, National Landscapes or green belt, where specific policy constraints apply.

123. In particular, major development within National Parks, the Broads and National Landscapes gives rise to considerations of exceptional justification for mineral extraction. (see paragraphs 77-78 above)

124. In the Green Belt, judgement is required under Policy GB7(1) of the NPPF as to whether proposed mineral extraction would minimise the impact on the openness of the Green Belt and not result in a significant conflict with the purposes of including land within it.

125. Whilst Policy GB7 regards certain forms of development as not inappropriate in the Green Belt, new buildings associated with mineral development may still be regarded as inappropriate, potentially requiring very special circumstances, such as an overriding need for minerals, to justify the development overall under Policy GB6.

126. The consideration of green belt openness is often applicable to mineral development for which there is recent case law. The case of Samuel Smith Old Brewery (Tadcaster) & Oxton Farm v North Yorkshire CC [2020] concerned a proposed 6 ha extension to a quarry. In that case it was found that ‘openness’ is a broad policy concept and is the counterpart of urban sprawl. Openness is not necessarily a statement about the visual qualities of the land, though in

<!-- Part3 p.339 -->

some cases it may be an aspect of applying the planning judgement involved in applying this broad policy concept. Nor does it imply freedom from any form of development as the judgement recognises that some forms of development, including mineral extraction, may in principle be appropriate, and compatible with the concept of openness.

127. A large quarry may not be visually attractive while it lasts, but the minerals can only be extracted where they are found, and the impact is temporary and subject to restoration. The judgement identified that as a barrier to urban sprawl a quarry may be regarded in Green Belt policy terms as no less effective than a stretch of agricultural land and that openness is a matter not of legal principle but of planning judgement.

128. Similarly, in the case of Europa Oil and Gas Ltd. v Secretary of State for Communities and Local Government [2013] EWHC 2643 (Admin) it was found that a factor that affects appropriateness, the preservation of openness and purposes of the Green Belt is “the duration of development and the reversibility of its effects…. Minerals can only be extracted where they are found”. The judgement also made clear that it is for the decision maker to determine the effect on Green Belt openness.

### Noise

129. The PPG provides detailed advice on noise limits that should be imposed by planning conditions. Higher noise levels may be acceptable for short periods during the daytime, such as those associated with soil-stripping, construction of baffle-mounds and site access works, where it is clear that such works will provide longer-term environmental benefits.

### Dust

130. The potential for dust pollution from mineral workings and its effects on health are often highly emotive issues in minerals planning.

131. The Institute of Air Quality Management (IAQM) publication ‘Guidance on the Assessment of Mineral Dust Impacts for Planning’. Provides detailed advice on assessment of dust impact.

132. Particles greater than 30µm (micrometres) make up the greatest proportion of dust emitted from mineral processing and they are largely deposited within 100m of sources. Particles between 10 and 30µm are likely to travel from 250 to 400m, while particles smaller than 10µm (PM10), which make up a small proportion of dust emitted from most mineral processing operations, may travel up to 1 km from sources. The IAQM guidance advises that adverse dust impacts from sand and gravel sites are unlikely beyond 250m from dust generating activities.

<!-- Part3 p.340 -->

133. A proportion of PM10 emitted from sand and gravel extraction could comprise respirable crystalline silica (RCS). Silica is a naturally occurring substance found in varying amounts in most rocks, sand and clay and the long-term inhalation of RCS may give rise to silicosis.

134. There is no guidance or accepted methodology to determine what proportion of RCS may be emitted or how likely the extraction of minerals would generate RCS emissions. There is evidence that RCS risk is increased where minerals are crushed as opposed to only screening. However, RCS is a recognised hazard for personnel working at quarries.

135. There is no UK established or recommended ambient air quality standard for RCS nor is there any recommended methodology for the assessment of potential RCS emissions. The Health and Safety Executive advice is that ‘No cases of silicosis have been documented among members of the general public in Great Britain, indicating that environmental exposures to silica dust are not sufficiently high to cause this occupational disease’.

### Climate Change

136. Concern over climate change is an increasingly prominent issue in minerals planning.

137. The Climate Change Act, as amended in 2019, and the Climate Change Committee commit the UK to net-zero carbon by 2050 but still forecast a national need for oil by 2050 of 82 million barrels, 90% from the UK, and seeks to avoid driving industry overseas which would increase emissions and damage the economy.

138. More recently, the Energy White Paper 2020 confirms the foregoing considerations and recognises the critical role of oil in maintaining energy security. However, it also makes clear that, during the transition to net-zero carbon emissions, the vast majority of oil supplies required to be maintained are from North Sea offshore production with the smaller proportion from the onshore oil and gas sector.

139. Oil and Gas Authority data of 2021 recorded UK oil production of some 0.79 million barrels per day of which only 1.82% was from onshore sources, with some 84% of that currently being produced by a single facility. It is accepted that the UK has become a new exporter of oil for the first time since 2004. At the same time North Sea Transition Authority data of October to December 2022 records a 17% decline in domestic oil and gas production.

140. The potential for oil and gas extraction to result in indirect greenhouse gas emissions through use by consumers, typically as a fuel for motor vehicles has been considered in the case of Finch v Surrey County Council (2024). The Supreme Court held that whether there was a sufficient degree of connection between the proposed development and effects, was a matter of fact and

<!-- Part3 p.341 -->

evaluative judgement for the decision-maker, and that it was not possible to say that “downstream” greenhouse gas emissions from the future combustion of refined oil products said to emanate from the development site were legally incapable of being an environmental effect requiring assessment.

## Types of Minerals Appeal

### Aggregates

141. In practice, most appeals are made under section 78 of the Act and concern new or extensions to sand, gravel or rock quarries to provide aggregate to meet local plan requirements. General examples are provided at Annex A.

### Oil and gas including hydraulic fracturing

142. The PPG advice on hydrocarbons covers the exploration assessment and production phases of on-shore oil and gas well development (see paragraphs 42-46 above).

143. In the case of oil and gas proposals, applications and appeals can be made for any or all of the three phases of exploration, assessment and production. Such appeals typically give rise to issues of amenity and other environmental interests including ground stability and groundwater pollution, traffic generation, air pollution and carbon emissions.

144. As major development within designated areas such as National Landscapes, appeals give rise to issues of landscape impact, level of need, availability and cost of alternatives and local economic benefits all fall for consideration in terms of whether the requisite exceptional justification exists.

145. In addition to Policies M1(4) and M5 of the NPPF, parties to an appeal may cite other Government and industry data sources to be taken into account in connection with the level of need for a particular onshore oil or gas development.

146. National Policy Statement for Energy 2023 (NPS EN-1) stated that fossil fuels are likely to play a significant economic role for some time to come and that it is critical that the UK continues to have secure and reliable supplies of energy as it makes the transition to a low-carbon economy. In the medium term, the UK faces the challenge of reducing energy demand and maximising economic production from declining domestic oil and gas reserves, with increasing reliance on imports of oil and gas in the context of rising world demand.

147. The Energy Security Strategy 2022 sought to maximise economic production of UK oil and gas reserves to provide reliable energy supplies not exposed to international supply risks.

148. The Annual Energy Statement 2022 (AES) noted the two key factors of reducing carbon eFmissions and ensuring energy security, with oil and gas remaining key to the energy system for years to come, despite increasing renewable energy sources.

Appeals against conditions.

150. Section 78 appeals seeking the removal or variation of the conditions of a minerals planning permission, arising under Section 79 of the Act (grant subject to conditions) or under Section 73 (continuation without compliance with previous conditions) are determined in the same way as for any other appeal against conditions.

151. Quarry operators may apply to continue working without complying with conditions limiting the period of operations. This may become necessary due to fluctuations in market conditions which can prolong the period of extraction beyond that envisaged when the original application was made. In such cases, the extension of the period of any disturbance such as from noise and dust, including from traffic movements will likely be considerations as will other environmental impacts. The need for the mineral will also remain a key consideration to which substantial weight should be given, in accordance with Policy M3 of the NPPF.

### Review of mineral planning conditions (ROMP cases)

152. There is special provision for appeals regarding the periodic review of existing dormant or active existing planning permissions.

153. An appeal can be made under paragraph 11 of Schedule 13 of the Environmental Protection Act 1995 against the determination of conditions on a mining site that differ from the proposed conditions set out in the review application.

154. In these cases, the powers at appeal are set down in Schedule 13, paragraph 16(3) of the EPA, applying paragraph 6 of Schedule 2 of the Planning and Compensation Act 1991 on the determination of appeals. This states that, on appeal, the Secretary of State may allow or dismiss the appeal or reverse or vary any part of the decision of the MPA whether the appeal relates to that part of it or not and may deal with the application as if it had been made to him in the first instance. The original planning permission is not at risk.

155. Schedule 13, paragraph 9(7) of the EPA 1995 states that when the MPA determines the conditions to which a dormant site is subject under 9(6), these may include any conditions which may be imposed on a grant of planning permission for minerals development and may be in addition to or in

<!-- Part3 p.343 -->

substitution for any existing conditions to which the permission in question is subject.

156. Paragraph 186 of the Minerals PPG states that all conditions must meet the policy tests, be necessary and should not affect the economic viability of the operation.

157. In terms of economic viability, the key test is the extent to which the further restrictions imposed by new conditions would cause extra operating costs or restrict revenue to the extent that economic viability would be prejudiced adversely to an unreasonable degree.

158. These provisions are likely to give rise to issues as to whether the disputed conditions meet the local and national policy tests that may be imposed on a grant of planning permission, with particular regard to the planning effects of the development and whether the economic viability of the operation would be affected to an unreasonable degree by imposition of any or all of the conditions in dispute, as informed by financial viability assessment.

### Other regulatory regimes

159. The planning system controls the use of land for minerals development in the public interest whilst other regulatory regimes are separate but complementary and apply control processes under health and safety and permitting legislation. Fundamentally, minerals planning is predicated on the assumption that these regulatory processes will operate effectively.

160. Separate permits may be required for

- surface and groundwater and mining waste, issued by the Environment Agency

- protected species habitats, issued by Natural England;

- coal extraction, issued by the Mining Remediation Authority (formerly the Coal Authority); and

- stopping up and diversion Orders for roads or public rights of way, issued by the highway authority.
