## Introduction

1. A purchase notice relating to a planning decision may be made under section 137 of the TCPA. A purchase notice is a form of compulsory purchase order in reverse. It may be served on the district council Welsh county, county borough or London borough in whose area the land lies by owners of land if, an application for planning permission is refused, or planning permission is granted subject to conditions and they consider that their land has become incapable of reasonably beneficial use. The notice must be served within the prescribed time, namely within 12 months of the Council’s refusal or conditional grant, or such longer period as the Secretary of State may at any time in any particular case allow (Reg 12(2) of the Town and Country Planning General Regulations 1992). Paragraphs 2.1-2.4 of the MHCLG Guidance indicates that the Secretary of State is normally prepared to extend time where “service is delayed for good reason” (see also EPL para P137.11). This is likely to include where there has been delay pending an appeal against the refusal or conditional grant of planning permission.

2. Note, though, that Section137(1)(a) does not cover failure cases or a refusal to approve details or reserved matters (see MHCLG Guidance paragraphs 1.1 and 1.2). Furthermore, the dismissal of an appeal or conditional grant of permission on an appeal cannot trigger the service of a purchase notice. The notice must relate back to the Council’s decision. If unhappy with conditions imposed on appeal, the landowner could apply for permission to carry out the development without complying with those conditions and if that were refused, they could then serve a purchase notice. The exception may be where an appeal against non-determination is refused. There will have been no decision of the Council to refuse planning permission, which could trigger a purchase notice, but the appeal flows from the application and dismissal of the appeal is effectively a refusal of that application. There is no authority on the point but, if the applicant appeals on the grounds of non-determination and that appeal is dismissed, it would seem illogical that there would be no opportunity to serve a purchase notice, where there would have been such an opportunity to serve one if the Council had refused the application.

3. Adverse planning decisions are the main reasons for the service of purchase notices but such a notice can also be served following the making of revocation, modification or discontinuance/alteration/removal orders. As revocation, modification and discontinuance orders are subject to confirmation by the Secretary of State, the 12 month time limit for service of a purchase notice will run from the Secretary of State’s decision.

4. There are also listed building purchase notices, which arise from the refusal of listed building consent, its conditional grant or its revocation or modification. The same 12 month time limit for the service of a notice and scope for the Secretary of State to extend that period applies by virtue of s32(1) of the LBCA and Reg 9(2) of the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas) Regulations 1990. Essentially, the listed building purchase notice process is the same as for purchase notices served under s137 of the TCPA and the guidance which

<!-- Part3 p.837 -->

follows below has been expressed primarily in terms of that section. However, important differences concerning listed building cases are highlighted, and the relevant legislation is s32 to s36 of the LBCA. All correspondence, reports and decisions will have to correctly reflect the relevant sections of that Act.

### The Procedure

5. There is no official form for a purchase notice and a letter will suffice provided it contains the relevant items noted in paragraph 4.1 of the MHCLG Guidance (including identifying the relevant land, owners and local planning authority, and the original planning or listed building consent application reference). Within 3 months of service of the purchase notice, the Council must serve a response notice on the owner indicating that (a) it is willing to comply, such as purchase the land; or (b) another local authority or statutory undertaker has agreed to comply in its place; or (c) it is not willing to comply, giving reasons, and has found no other local authority or statutory undertaker willing to comply.

6. If responding in terms of (c), the Council must first send a copy of the purchase notice and proposed response notice to the Secretary of State, and the MHCLG Guidance indicates what other information should be provided. If the Council fails to respond within 3 months, it is not bound to purchase the land by default (see paragraph 7.1 of the MHCLG Guidance). However, they could be compelled to respond by court order. The server of the notice can then comment on the Council's reasons. The Secretary of State, on the information before them at that stage, then makes a preliminary decision as to whether or not they intend to confirm the notice on the Council or some other authority or statutory undertaker1, or to take certain other courses of action in lieu of confirmation. These other courses of action are

- to grant planning permission or listed building consent for the development or works originally sought;

- to direct that some other planning permission or listed building consent be granted if applied for;

- to revoke or amend any conditions attached to the permission or listed building consent originally granted;

- in the case of a notice served on account of a revocation order, cancel the order;

[^1]: The prospect of substituting another authority or statutory undertaker is only likely to arise if the Council approached one on receiving the purchase notice. In any event, if another authority or statutory undertaker is to be substituted, notice must be given to them and they must be given an opportunity to be heard.

<!-- Part3 p.838 -->

- in the case of a notice served on account of a modification order, revoke or amend conditions;

- in the case of a notice served on account of a discontinuance order, revoke or amend the order; and

- to direct the grant of planning permission or listed building consent for part of the site and confirm the notice for the remainder of the site on the appropriate authority;

7. However, in planning decision cases, this last course of action is only available where the Secretary of State is satisfied that the land is incapable of reasonably beneficial use in its existing state and could not be rendered capable of reasonably beneficial use by the carrying out of other development for which planning permission has been granted. Furthermore:

- that conclusion must relate to all the land to which the purchase notice relates (EPL P137.07 and P141.03(5) and MHCLG Guidance paragraph 13.2); and

- the purchase notice, as originally served, must relate to the identical area of land the subject of the relevant refusal, conditional grant or order (see MHCLG Guidance paragraph 9.1; and EPL paragraph P137.07).

8. If the server cannot demonstrate that all the land the subject of the relevant refusal, conditional grant or order has become incapable of reasonably beneficial use, it is open to them to seek planning permission for that part of the land not capable of reasonably beneficial use and, in the event of a qualifying adverse decision, proceed under s137 in respect only of that area.

9. The position is different in listed building cases. The area of the relevant application site and the area of the listed building purchase notice need not necessarily be the same (see EPL paragraph P137.09). Furthermore, by virtue of s35(2) of the LBCA, a finding of no reasonably beneficial use may be made in relation to part of the land in respect of which the listed building purchase notice was served. This is however subject to s35(3) of the LBCA, which provides that the Secretary of State shall not confirm the notice, unless satisfied that the land to which it relates comprises such land contiguous with or adjacent to the listed building as is required for: preserving the building or its amenities; or affording access to it; or for its proper management or control.

10. Before reaching their final decision the Secretary of State is required to indicate their initial intention and give the parties at least 28 days to request an opportunity of “appearing before and being heard” by a person appointed by them. In that event an Inquiry or Hearing will be held and the Inspector will, after hearing the case, report to the Secretary of State, who will then make a final decision. In practice the initial indication of intention is typically drafted by the Planning Inspectorate, previously by a decision officer but more recently by an Inspector separate to the one tasked with holding the oral event and producing a

<!-- Part3 p.839 -->

report. The final decision is also usually made within the Planning Inspectorate. Again, this used to be made by a Decision Officer but can be made by a third Inspector. The casework team will carry out validation checks on receipt of the purchase notice application, but the Inspector(s) should also refer to the MHCLG Guidance including paragraphs 2.1, 3.1, 4.1, 5.1, 9.1, 11.1-11.3 and 19.1-19.4.

### Time Limits for the Secretary of State

11. In those cases where the Secretary of State fails to notify the owner by whom the notice was served that they do not propose to confirm the notice, or where they propose to confirm the notice, but has not done so and has taken no action in lieu of confirmation, TCPA s143(2) & LBCA s36(2) have the effect of imposing time limits for the determination of the notice. In such circumstances failure to issue the final decision within 6 months of the date on which the notice was sent to the Secretary of State,, or within 9 months of the date on which the notice was served on the authority, whichever is the earlier, results in the notice being deemed to be confirmed on the authority.

12. Decisions (both proposed and final) and Reports on purchase notice cases, where the time limits apply, must therefore be submitted well within the dates shown on the file. If the Inspector’s recommendation is contrary to the proposed decision to confirm the PN, that recommendation will be of no effect if the time limit is missed.

13. Where the Secretary of State (via the proposed decision) has given notice that they propose not to confirm the notice [TCPA s143(2)(c) and LBCA s36(2)(b)], the time limits do not operate at the post-proposal stage and the normal requirements for the submission of reports apply. Under s143(4) of the TCPA and s36(6) of the LBCA, the application of the time limits is suspended where there is a concurrent enforcement or other planning or listed building consent appeal affecting the same land until the appeal is determined. When this provision applies, a formal letter to this effect is sent to the parties and placed on the file. It is also customary to take no further action on the purchase notice whilst the appeal is being determined, in case it results in planning permission for the development which, if carried out, would render the land capable of reasonably beneficial use. Also, the Secretary of State has the power to extend the 12 month time limit for service of a purchase notice and may well do so if a planning or listed building appeal is in progress.

### Hearing/Inquiry

14. As the right is to appear before and be heard by an appointed person, the Written Representations procedure is not available (paragraph 22.1 of the MHCLG Guidance just says it is “usual to hold a local inquiry or a hearing”). The Hearing/Inquiry should be conducted in accordance with the general advice given in the Inspector Training Manual – Hearings or Inquiries. In the event of a Hearing/Inquiry, the related Procedure Rules do not apply but the parties are normally requested to observe them. At the event, the server of the notice should be required to put their case first, as the onus of proof is on them to show

<!-- Part3 p.840 -->

that the land has become incapable of reasonably beneficial use. The Inspector should obtain all the information necessary for the proper consideration of the case. This should include the existing state, the present planning status and present use of the land (and/or building in listed building cases), land uses in the area, the development/works initially sought, any suitable alternative development put forward, including that originally sought, and any proposed substitution of a different acquiring authority. Interested persons should be heard if:

- they might be affected if planning permission or listed building consent is granted for a refused scheme, or if conditions were revoked or amended;

- they might be affected by any alternative development or works under consideration;

- they have proposals or relevant information on beneficial uses; or

- they have some other legitimate interest.

15. Interested persons may include prospective owners who have information relevant to marketing the land or development issues.

16. Consider whether the human rights of any interested party or other local resident might be engaged and interfered with if you recommend that planning permission or listed building consent be granted or directed to be granted (most likely the right to respect for a person’s private and family life, home and correspondence under Article 8 of Schedule 1, Part I of the Human Rights Act 1998, including the best interests of any children, or the right to peaceful enjoyment of possessions under Article 1 of the First Protocol).

### Main Considerations

17. In purchase notice cases concerning planning permissions there may be three main considerations:

1. whether the server has proved on the balance of probability that the land is incapable of reasonably beneficial use (RBU) in its existing state and could not be rendered capable of such use by the carrying out of

<!-- Part3 p.841 -->

development for which planning permission has been granted2 or undertaken3 to be granted.

2. if, and only if, it is not so capable whether, in lieu of confirmation: (i) it would be expedient for planning permission to be granted for the refused scheme (or for conditions to be revoked or amended) to make the land capable of RBU); or (ii) the local planning authority should be directed to grant planning permission, if an application is made, for any other development to make the land capable of RBU within a reasonable time.

3. The third issue which may arise is whether, having regard to any probable ultimate use of the land, an alternative acquiring authority should be substituted.

18. If considering whether to grant permission or consent for the development or works, make sure you have been given copies of any interested party representations made at the application stage and consider whether they need to be notified of the purchase notice proceedings. In any event, interested parties may be able to contribute to the question of whether there is a RBU.

19. There is nothing to stop the Secretary of State granting permission or consent for the refused development or works, even if an appeal against the refusal had already been dismissed, but you would need good reasons for recommending this.

20. Similarly, if considering recommending the Secretary of State to direct the local planning authority to grant planning permission or listed building consent for any alternative development or works, you would need to be satisfied that anyone likely to be affected had an opportunity to comment. It would also be necessary to recommend a deadline for the submission of an application.

## Reporting

21. The standard template for Secretary of State reports should be used as a guide. Facts should not be found. This is because if the Secretary of State's decision were subject to a time limit and they wished to disagree with any finding of fact they would have to go back to the parties and there may be insufficient time to do so. The report should therefore end solely with the inspector's conclusions

and recommendation and, if crucial facts are in dispute, the recommendations would need to accommodate the different versions.

22. In the conclusions the Inspector should first deal with the issue of whether or not the site is capable of RBU in its existing state, or could be rendered capable of RBU. Whether or not the site is considered capable of RBU, the Inspector should deal with the merits of suggested alternative uses, in case the Secretary of State takes a different view on the issue. In the final conclusion on the merits the Inspector should state clearly that "I have considered all possible alternative uses and conclude that …". Where appropriate, the Inspector should then go on to deal finally with the matter of whether another authority should be substituted for the Council on whom the notice has been served. This must depend on the Council having sought to involve another authority and it depends on them having been given an opportunity to be heard..

23. In all cases reasons must be given in the conclusions for the recommendation which follows, including the reasons for any conditions imposed on a grant of planning permission or listed building consent for the development or works that have already been refused (via TCPA s141(2)(a)and (b), and LBCA s35(4)(a) and (b)).

### The Recommendation

24. The recommendation should be on the following lines, as appropriate: that the purchase notice be confirmed (on a different authority if appropriate); or

- that the purchase notice be confirmed and/or (in relation to a listed building notice only) confirmed in part, or confirmed on a different authority if appropriate; or

- that the purchase notice be not confirmed; or

- that in lieu of confirmation, planning permission or listed building consent be granted for the development or works originally sought; or

- that in lieu of confirmation, a direction be given that some other planning permission or listed building consent be granted if applied for; or

- that in lieu of confirmation, the conditions attached to the grant of the permission or listed building consent be revoked or amended; or

- that in lieu of confirmation of the notice in relation to the whole site, planning permission or listed building consent be directed to be granted for part and for the notice to be confirmed in relation to the remainder of the land on the appropriate authority.

25. Following the consideration of alternative uses, the Secretary of State cannot grant permission or consent for an alternative development or works for which no application has been made. The Secretary of State may however direct that planning permission or consent be granted if applied for. Natural justice

<!-- Part3 p.843 -->

demands that this must be dependent on those likely to be affected by any such grant having had an opportunity to make representations. Furthermore, a deadline should be set for the submission of any application.

26. Under the TCPA, the Secretary of State has no power to confirm a notice for part of the land and reject it for the remainder; any recommendation should deal with the land as one unit, with two exceptions. First, permission can be directed for one part of the land and the notice confirmed for the remainder. Secondly, a substitute acquiring authority can be named in relation to one part of the land only - for example where a highway authority or statutory undertaker could make use of it. The notice can then either be confirmed on the remainder of the land or a direction made that permission be granted. Under s35(2) of the LBCA, a listed building purchase notice can be confirmed in respect of part of the land.

### Reasonably Beneficial Use

28. Guidance on the concept of Reasonably Beneficial Use (RBU), as referred to at s137 of the TCPA and s32 of the LBCA, is given in paragraphs 12.1-12.12 and 13.1-13.4 of the MHCLG Guidance and see also the commentary at P137.08 of the EPL. Section 137(3)(a) of the TCPA requires "that the land has become incapable of reasonably beneficial use in its existing state" and this is mirrored in s32(2)(a) of the LBCA which requires “that the building and land in respect of which the notice is served have become incapable of reasonably beneficial use in their existing state”. The words 'has become incapable' have been taken in practice to mean 'is incapable'. The reason for the existing state of the land is immaterial to the assessment of whether it is incapable of reasonably beneficial use unless that state is the result of some unauthorised development which could be the subject of an enforcement notice requiring it to be returned to its original state.

29. 'Existing state' means the land as it is now. It precludes any use involving development for which express planning permission or listed building consent is required unless already granted or promised. It does not, however, preclude development that can be carried out by virtue of the GPDO or changes which

<!-- Part3 p.844 -->

are taken not to involve development by virtue of the Use Classes Order. The potential of the land can be considered, including if it is necessary to undertake work to realise that potential4. However, for listed buildings cases, paragraph 13.4 of the MHCLG Guidance advises:

“When considering whether a listed building has reasonably beneficial use, a relevant factor to be taken into account may be the estimated cost of any renovations believed to be necessary. It is therefore helpful (but not conclusive) if estimated figures for such renovations, and an indication of the likely return on the relevant expenditure, can be provided. If no reasonable person would undertake the works because the benefits would not outweigh the costs then the building would not have a reasonably beneficial use.”

30. Although a purchase notice usually follows an adverse decision on a planning application (a refusal or a conditional permission) the purchase notice is not a claim that the decision has made the land incapable of RBU, but that without the benefit of the permission sought, or the removal of certain conditions, it cannot be used reasonably beneficially. The onus is on the server to demonstrate that there is no RBU. The notice must relate to the whole of the land the subject of the planning decision giving rise to it and all that land must be shown to be incapable of RBU (except in relation to listed building purchase notices [LBCA s35(2)]. In certain limited circumstances, however, a 'split decision' is possible [TCPA s141(3)], i.e. the Secretary of State may direct that planning permission be granted for part of the land, whilst confirming the purchase notice in respect of the remainder. However, they can only do this in TCPA cases if satisfied that all the land is incapable of RBU.

31. What is a 'reasonably beneficial use' in one set of circumstances may not be so in another. Because of this, there is no statutory definition of the term. It is a question of fact and degree on which the Inspector must advise the Secretary of State. From various judgements a number of principles have emerged;

- It is not a comparison of the land in its existing state with its potential value had the permission sought been granted. The test is not whether the land is less valuable to the owner than if developed in accordance with the owner's wishes. Instead, it is whether the use is reasonably beneficial to the owner (or a prospective owner) in all the relevant circumstances of the particular site. (R v Minister of Housing and Local Government, ex parte Chichester Rural District Council (1960) WLR 587).

[^4]: This is so long as that work does not require express planning permission or listed building consent where none has been granted or promised (see EPL paragraph 137.08) JPL 973 at paragraphs 21 – 22 where Silber J noted that the Oxford English shorter dictionary definition of “incapable” is, among other things, “not susceptible of”; and MHCLG Guidance

<!-- Part3 p.845 -->

- The Secretary of State would normally expect to see some evidence that the server has attempted to dispose of the relevant interest. However, in considering that evidence, the decision maker must have reasonable grounds to conclude both that a prospective owner exists and that the prospective purchaser is prepared to pay a price commensurate with what would be a reasonably beneficial use. The owner should not be expected to sell at an artificially low (possibly peppercorn) price. See the Court of Appeal judgment in Gavaghan & Gavaghan v SoS for the Environment and South Hams District Council [1990] . However, letting potential is relevant – see MHCLG Guidance paragraph 13.1.

- A use which is only beneficial to the public at large must normally be disregarded - see Adams and Wade v Minister of Housing and Local Government and Another [1965]. However, there is an exception to this principle, where land has a restricted use as undeveloped or amenity land, by virtue of the terms of an existing planning permission. Typically, this would be a housing estate with landscaped or other amenity areas.

- The history of how the land came to be in its existing state is not relevant, even if the owner has contributed to the situation, for example by neglect. The only exception to this principle is where the existing state results from a breach of planning control that is still susceptible to enforcement action. Logically, enforcement action may render the land capable of RBU.

32. In assessing whether or not land has a RBU in its existing state, the existing use of the land, or any possible uses which do not require planning permission, should be compared with the uses prevailing in the area and the general pattern of development. For example, a small enclosure suitable for grazing might have a RBU for that purpose in a rural area, but the same use in an urban area might not be considered beneficial. If the Council suggests any alternative use (not requiring permission) they should produce evidence of a demand for that use; but where a server argues to the contrary, they should also produce supporting evidence. If they do not, the Inspector should ascertain whether there is any real demand. There have been occasions where Councils have introduced an alternative use without supplying any supporting evidence or where that use needs planning permission which they have neither granted nor undertaken to grant - see TCPA s138(1) disregard any unauthorised prospective use.

33. It may sometimes be possible for an area of land to be rendered capable of RBU by being used in conjunction with neighbouring or adjoining land provided that a sufficient interest in that land is held by the server of the notice, or by a prospective owner of the purchase notice land. Use by a prospective owner cannot be considered unless there is reasonably firm indication of intention to acquire the notice site (see MHCLG Guidance paragraph 12.6).

34. The concept of RBU is not synonymous with profit - MHCLG Guidance paragraph 12.7. Profit may be a useful comparison in certain circumstances but the absence of profit, however calculated, is not necessarily a material

<!-- Part3 p.846 -->

consideration (for example in the case of a purchase notice involving the garden of a dwellinghouse) and sometimes the value of the land after TCPA Schedule 3 development is sometimes referred to. Section 138(1) of the TCPA provides that in determining whether land is incapable of reasonably beneficial use in accordance with s137, no account shall be taken of unauthorised prospective use. Section 138(2)(a) states that a prospective use is unauthorised if it would involve the carrying out of development other than any development specified in paragraph 1 or 2 of Schedule 3. Aside from paragraphs 1 and 2, Schedule 3 has been largely repealed but, in any event, in Hudscott Estates (East) Ltd v SSETR (2001) 82 P&CR 8; (2001) JPL 973 it was held that s137:

“38…does not require any assumption to be made that Schedule 3 is always applicable…

42…The Secretary of State in an appeal decision reported at (1996) 2 P.A.D. 143 has accepted that Schedule 3 should be regarded relevant only as a guide to compensation once a purchase notice is confirmed, and has no relevance to the issue of whether or not the land in question was capable of reasonably beneficial use. In those circumstances, the Secretary of State was not at fault in the way contended …”

35. Indeed, paragraph P138.03 of the EPL now states, “Schedule 3 is essentially a compensation provision, with only a limited role in relation to purchase notices.” In short, paragraphs 1 and 2 of Schedule 3 appear relevant only for the assessment of compensation in the event of confirmation. Disagreement between the parties on the level of compensation is not a matter for the decision maker on purchase notices.

36. Where the land is incapable of RBU, under s142 the Secretary of State may refuse to confirm the notice if part or the whole of the land has a restricted use under the express or implied terms of an existing planning permission, in the context of the development already permitted and it remains appropriate to restrict the use of the land in the way originally intended. Examples of this would be land earmarked by condition or the approved plans for amenity land in a housing estate, or landscaped areas in business parks.

### Alternative Development or Works

37. If it is decided that the land has no RBU in its existing state, or that it cannot be rendered capable of RBU by the carrying out of any development or works for which planning permission or listed building consent has been granted or undertaken to be granted, it has then to be considered whether planning permission or listed building consent should be granted for either the development or works originally sought or some other form of development or works.

38. The question for the Secretary of State is whether it appears “expedient” to grant planning permission or consent for the development or works originally sought or whether it appears that planning permission or consent “ought to be granted” for

<!-- Part3 p.847 -->

some other form of development or works, such that the Council should be directed to grant such permission or consent. The statutory provisions do not expressly require the Secretary of State to have regard to the development plan. Accordingly, the requirement under s38(6) of the Planning and Compulsory Purchase Act 2004 to determine the matter in accordance with the development plan, unless material considerations indicate otherwise, does not apply in purchase notice cases. Nevertheless, relevant development plan policies and other material considerations, including the National Planning Policy Framework, must be important considerations in the judgement of whether it is expedient to grant planning permission, or whether planning permission ought to be granted for some other development, or whether planning conditions should be revoked or amended.

39. Bear in mind any natural justice or human rights issues if you are considering recommending that permission or consent should be granted for the development or works originally sought, or that alternative development or works should be granted. Ensure that you have sought representations from any affected parties. Any alternative scheme would need to be something specific and covered with or by the relevant parties (the server, the Council, and any affected interested parties). Split decisions are also possible (to part confirm the purchase notice and grant/direct the grant of permission for the remainder of the site) but the same fairness principles apply.

### Substitute Authority

40. In considering a request by the Council that some other authority should be substituted, the overriding consideration will be whether that authority is likely to have a functional use for the land. An example would be where a highway authority needs all or part of the land for highway purposes. The suggested substitute authority's views should be sought. The Secretary of State has, however, no powers to confirm a purchase notice on a Government Department (see MHCLG Guidance paragraphs 21.2 and 21.3).
