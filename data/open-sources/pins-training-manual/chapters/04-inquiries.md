### Introduction

1. This advice applies to England only. It relates mainly to the conduct of Inquiries in planning, advertisement and listed building consent appeals although the principles have wider relevance. Further advice on the conduct of Inquiries for enforcement (s174) and lawful development certificates (s195) can be found in the ‘Enforcement’ chapter of the Inspector Training Manual.

2. In accordance with the Planning Inspectorate’s Code of Conduct and the Franks’ Principles (See ‘Role of the Inspector’) your main objectives when holding an Inquiry are to ensure that:

- the evidence is examined and tested to enable you to reach a reasoned decision or recommendation.

- all parties and interested persons have a reasonable opportunity to participate and to have a fair hearing.

### Legislation and procedural guidance

3. The statutory rules governing Inquiries are:

- Section 78 appeals determined by the Inspector: The Town and Country Planning Appeals (Determination by Inspectors) (Inquiries Procedure) (England) Rules 2000 (SI 2000/1625).

- Section 77 and s78 appeals determined by the Secretary of State: The Town and Country Planning (Inquiries Procedure) (England) Rules 2000 (SI 2000/1624).

- Section 174 and section 195 appeals determined by the Inspector: The Town and Country Planning (Enforcement) (Determination by Inspectors) (Inquiries Procedure) (England) Rules 2002 (SI 2002/2685).

- Section 174 and section 195 appeals determined by the Secretary of State: The Town and Country Planning (Enforcement) (Inquiries Procedure) (England) Rules 2002 (SI 2002/2686).

4. These rules have been amended since their publication so, if you need to refer to them, you must use the consolidated versions.

5. References to the Rules in this document are to the ‘Determination by Inspectors’ Rules (SI 2000/1625 in England) unless otherwise stated.

6. Procedural guidance can be found in:

- The planning appeals: procedural guide applies to planning appeals, householder development appeals, minor commercial appeals, listed

### The Inquiry process

7. The Inquiry process is set out in the Planning appeals: procedural guide, which applies to most Inquiries determined by Inspectors in England. Slightly different processes may apply to appeals determined by the Secretary of State, enforcement appeals and other type of Inquiry – however the basic principles – focussed on the Franks Principles – will be the same.

8. Most Inquiries have been, conventionally, conducted in person. This has changed with advances in virtual meeting technology (such as Teams), meaning that in-person events, virtual or hybrid events may all be undertaken.

9. It is at the Inspector’s discretion how the event will be held, having taken views from the main parties as necessary. It could be held entirely virtually or as a hybrid event e.g. with parts taking place virtually (such as discussions on conditions or obligations, and closing submissions). The Inspector may also deal with some elements of an appeal which is proceeding by way of an Inquiry as written submissions.

10. When you are appointed to conduct an Inquiry, you will need to provide the Case Officer with the dates you want to sit, along with the Case Management Conference (CMC) date and time, in accordance with the Inquiry timetable.

### Who is entitled to appear at an Inquiry?

11. The Appellant, the Local Planning Authority (LPA) and various other bodies, such as other local authorities in the area, parish/community councils, ‘Rule 6 parties’ and statutory parties, are entitled to appear at the Inquiry.

12. Paragraph 11.2.6 of the Guidance notes that interested parties will usually be allowed to participate in the inquiry. Rule 11(2) states that there is nothing that

<!-- Part1 p.189 -->

shall prevent you from permitting any other person to appear and such permission shall not be unreasonably withheld.

13. Rule 11.3 explains that those given Rule 6 status will be treated like the main parties, and that this status will normally be given to groups rather than individuals.

### What are the key evidential documents that you should receive?

14. A Statement of Case sets out the parties’ cases, identifying what they intend to argue. Section 12 of the Procedural Guide: Planning appeals – England provides more detail about what should be included (see also Rule 2(1) on “full statement of case”).

15. In cases where the appeal is against the LPA’s failure to issue a decision, there can often be delays with an LPA indicating its putative reasons for refusal. For example, this can be due to this needing to be agreed at committee. It may lead to delays because the appellant states that they do not know what case they have to answer. The template at Annex A can be adapted to request the putative reasons for refusal as soon as a failure case is allocated. This may help overall timeliness.

16. Rule 6(8) gives the Inspector/SoS the power to request further information about the matters contained in the Statement of Case if the submitted version is inadequate.

17. Proofs of evidence are the documents that contain the evidence of specific witnesses. A summary of the proof is required unless the proof is less than 1,500 words (Rule 14(2)).

18. The cases for the Appellant, LPA and any Rule 6 party should already have been set out in full in their Statements of Case. Consequently, the main purpose of a proof of evidence is to allow expert witnesses to:

- bring together evidence necessary for the presentation of their case at the Inquiry.

- give their opinion on the evidence provided by other parties in their Statements of Case.

19. Rebuttal proofs are not a formal part of the process and are accepted at your discretion. They can narrow areas of disagreement which may save inquiry time. But that may often be an unhelpful recitation of the parties’ cases, and this type of rebuttal should be firmly resisted. They are not an opportunity to introduce new evidence.

<!-- Part1 p.190 -->

20. The Planning appeals: procedural guide provides more advice about the contents of proofs and rebuttals, their length and the need for summaries. Chapter 15 sets out specific requirements for Expert Evidence.

21. Annex C: Content and Format of Proofs and Appendices gives further suggestions on the style and content of proofs. You may wish to include a version of this, tailored to your specific requirements, with your CMC note. Annex C: Content And Format of Proofs and Appendice

22. Statement(s) of Common Ground (SoCG) should set out the range of details on which the parties agree and, much more importantly, disagree. PINS has published guidance on SoCG for appeals. The guidance was prepared with the Planning & Environmental Bar Association (PEBA) and is intended to assist all professional representatives and expert witnesses in compliance with their duty to assist the Inspector.

23. In addition to the main SoCG, topic based SoCGs are a very useful document which will be of assistance to you. These will most likely be in relation to complex matters where the differences between the parties are unclear or where there is a vague reason for refusal such as “Insufficient information has been provided to enable a determination on highway safety”. Getting the parties to spell out the key areas of dispute may well save you a lot of questioning. You should flag up the possibility of topic based SOCGs at the CMC and set a deadline for their submission, usually alongside the proof of evidence. They can also be very useful instead of a rebuttal proof.

24. Advice on the content, form and purpose of a SoCG is also provided in Section 13 to the Procedural Guide

### Case Management Conferences (CMC) and Pre Inquiry Meetings (PIM)

25. The CMC is almost always held virtually via Teams. The Case Officer will send the parties (and you) instructions for the Teams call. Before the CMC you should produce a pre-Conference note and agenda (see below).

26. On the day of the CMC the Case Officer will normally make sure all the parties are present before contacting you on Teams and asking you to join. Make sure you know if they are going to contact you on Teams or by email.

27. The Appellant, the LPA and any Rule 6 parties take part in the CMC but you have discretion to allow others to participate if you consider it necessary. This might be the case of local groups who are likely to submit substantial evidence but who have not sought/been granted Rule 6 status this could help the smooth running of the inquiry and clarify their role.

28. The main purpose of a CMC is to allow you to give clarity to the parties about the management of the case and explore any procedural issues. This will include:

<!-- Part1 p.191 -->

- alerting everyone to issues in which you are particularly interested and on which you would like evidence to focus (e.g. a specific aspect of technical data).

- identifying issues on which you do not wish to hear further evidence beyond that set out in the Statements of Case.

- setting out how you wish the evidence to be presented and the timetable for receipt of any outstanding documents / evidence.

- Seeking the parties’ views on or requesting action on any matters about which you have any concerns (e.g. missing documents or venue capacity).

- Encouraging parties to limit evidence in chief, as you will have already read the evidence of each witness. Make it clear that you will challenge unrealistically long time estimates.

- Explore whether the parties, at this stage, consider that the Inquiry can be concluded in the allocated time, plus reserve day(s).

- Ensuring that the parties keep a suitable additional reserve period after the scheduled duration of the inquiry in case of unforeseen delays. This could otherwise cause an unacceptable delay if not scheduled early.

- The meeting must not go into the merits of the case. However, it is entirely permissible, and indeed very useful, to explore how far apart the parties are on particular topics. This information will be very useful in terms of timetabling.

- Ensuring that the parties work closely together and have ongoing dialogue in the period between the CMC and the Inquiry. Where parties do not collaborate early on, this can result in abortive work and disruption to the timetable.

29. You will need to be very specific about how you want the Core Documents library to be organised, and by whom. It is usually done by the Council, but it is acceptable for the appellant do undertake this – provided the location is flagged prominently on the Council’s website

30. Key features of a Core Document library:

- It must be easily publicly accessible.

- It needs to be organised in a logical fashion (See Annex D).

- Hyperlinks should be provided for each core document - which should generally be in PDF format

<!-- Part1 p.192 -->

- Any appeal decisions or legal cases on which a party intends to rely must each be prefaced with a note explaining the relevance of the specific part of the decision/case.

- No additional CDs should be added to the CD library after proofs are submitted other than with the Inspector’s permission. The PINS Case Officer should be consulted in such cases, explaining why the material is late.

31. You will be allocated time to prepare for the CMC. Your preparation time will usually be a week before the CMC.

32. The CMC must take place within seven weeks of the start date but bear in mind that the LPA’s Statement of Case will not be due until week five. If you are unable to prepare for and carry out the CMC due to other commitments, there may be scope for another Inspector to carry it out on your behalf. This Inspector will need to be briefed by you about any particular requirements which you may have (for example, hard copy documents or electronic working; views on round table sessions).

33. You will need to prepare a pre-Conference note and agenda for the Case Officer to issue to the parties. These should normally be issued at least three days before the date set for the CMC.

34. The pre-Conference note should set out all the matters concerning the Inquiry arrangements about which you wish to inform the parties or discuss with them. There may be some matters on which you need to have a discussion during the CMC before coming to a view. An example of a short pre-Conference note and agenda is provided at Annex B1.

35. It is best to prepare some notes for the CMC, to ensure that you cover all the matters that you need to. A check list of matters you may need to cover at the CMC is provided at Annex B2.

36. Prepare for the CMC as you would for any other case. Read the key documents and consider the following:

- Do you understand the proposal and know which are the relevant plans?

- Are any documents missing? If so, request them in the pre-Conference note, and/or ask for them during the CMC.

- Are there any procedural matters on which you need clarification (such as the nature of the proposed development, amended proposals, revised plans or updated surveys)?

<!-- Part1 p.193 -->

- What are the potential main issues at this stage? Start by looking at the reasons for refusal and the parties’ statements of case (and the SOCG if available) Note any other issues raised by anything raised by interested parties. See The Approach to Decision Making ITM chapter for further advice.

- Identify relevant development plan and national policies. Is any clarification necessary?

- Has reference been made to planning obligation (See Planning Obligations ITM for more information)? Have the parties started to draft conditions? You should set a clear timetable for these to be produced-probably at least two weeks before the Inquiry opens.

- Are there likely to be any procedural problems (complaints about the venue, likely requests for postponements1 or adjournments)?

- Which topic(s) would be best dealt with through cross-examination, which by round table and which as written representations? It is your choice but seek the views of the parties and try to get buy-in to your position. It is open to you to defer the decision on the way in which you will hear evidence on particular topics until later in the run up to the Inquiry.

- For main issues being dealt with by cross-examination, consider whether it be most effective to group the witnesses by party (i.e. hearing all the witnesses for one party and then all the witnesses for the next party), or on a topic basis (hearing all the witnesses on each main issue before moving on to the witnesses on the next main issue). Topic based inquiries are becoming increasingly common as opposed to the more traditional party-by-party approach. But the choice is up to the Inspector.

- Is there any appetite for an evening session for interested parties (e.g. if there is a large number of representations)? If so, explore why this would assist. Is there anyone (for example a Parish Council), who can assist with marshalling the interested parties?

- It will not be possible to finalise a timetable for the Inquiry at the CMC. But the parties can help with a general indication. A timetable is formally required for Inquiries of eight days or more – Rule 8(1)). Even if the Inquiry is scheduled for fewer than eight days, a timetable is necessary.

- Your post-CMC note will include a date by when you will be provided with time estimates (normally two weeks before opening).

- Proofs of evidence must be submitted at least four weeks before the Inquiry opens. It is usually sensible to set the same (or an earlier) deadline for any other documents you may request during the CMC (such as Statements of Common Ground).

37. In some cases there may remain procedural matters which cannot be resolved at the CMC. Many of these may be resolved by subsequent email exchanges, but it is also possible - and often useful - to have a second, more focussed, CMC. This could deal with material changes in circumstances, the approach to be adopted to a change in the LPA stance etc. Don’t leave matters unresolved to the Inquiry if possible.

38. One of the key purposes of the CMC is to encourage joint working between the parties. This will help narrow the real issues between the parties and save pointless inquiry time. If you are not convinced that this is happening Inspectors can either schedule a second CMC or follow up the matter with emails.

39. When you have covered everything on the agenda and established that there are no outstanding matters, you should close the CMC.

40. You will need to issue a post-Conference note via the Case Officer within a few days after the CMC. This should confirm the agreed arrangements and – most importantly - the timetable for submitting documents. An example is provided at Annex B3.

### Requests for postponement and issues leading to adjournments

41. It is far from uncommon for parties, having had an inquiry date agreed or imposed, to approach PINS with a request to postpone the date. This should be strongly resisted. At the CMC and at other times it is important to stress

<!-- Part1 p.195 -->

that parties should have contingency plans in place in the event of illness or absence – including the unavailability of a preferred advocate. PINS will only agree to postponements in exceptional circumstances, and only where it would not jeopardise targets.

42. This applies equally if the postponement request comes from both parties. It must not be forgotten that local residents and interested parties may have been waiting for a decision on a proposal for some years, and a postponement might disadvantage them.

43. In the same vein, requests for adjournment during the inquiry should be resisted unless it is entirely unavoidable. At the CMC and in the subsequent timetable it is important that reserve days are scheduled in case of unavoidable delays. The parties must be realistic with their time estimates and adhere to the Inspector’s timetable. At the CMC and at the start of the Inquiry, Inspectors should stress that both examination in chief and cross-examination must be as short as possible and to the point. Inspectors must intervene if the inquiry is becoming repetitious or fixated on irrelevant detail.

### Preparation before the Inquiry

44. Ensure you have sufficient time allocated between the submission deadline for the proofs of evidence and the opening of the Inquiry, to carry out your necessary preparation. At this stage you will need to:

- Read the proofs, pertinent core documents and interested party representations, noting down any questions as you do so. You may need to raise the questions at the Inquiry if the opposing party does not.

- Prepare your opening (and closing) remarks (see Annex I for an example).

- Prepare a list of anything you want to see on the site visit (and add to it during the Inquiry, as necessary).

- Make a list of everything you need to take with you to the Inquiry.

### Pre-Inquiry visit to the site and Inquiry venue

45. It is good practice to carry out an unaccompanied site visit, to view the site and surrounding area, before the Inquiry. This is most easily done the day before or at any point beforehand if you are holding a virtual event. You should only view the site from public land (including footpaths across the site) and should not go onto the site – even if the landowner has given permission for you to do so.

46. If going onto the site is essential at an early stage, one option is to open the inquiry, hear from interested parties and then undertake the site visit after that

<!-- Part1 p.196 -->

point, but prior to formal examination. See the Site Visit ITM chapter for more information.

47. If you are approached near the site, then explain your purpose as briefly as possible. Be polite but avoid getting involved in conversation. If this does occur, it is good practice to mention it in your opening in case others have seen you speaking (especially if the person with whom you spoke is also present at the Inquiry). Also explain in your opening that you have looked at the site and the surrounding area.

48. The advantages of a pre-Inquiry visit are that it can:

- show the parties that you know the site and how it relates to its surroundings.

- help avoid unnecessary explanation about the site at the event.

- help you to follow and understand site specific evidence or that relating to other issues (e.g. specific road junctions).

- help you ask informed questions.

- allow you to check that the site notice has been posted (if you know this might be an issue).

49. It is often helpful to visit the Inquiry venue the day before so that you know how to find it and, if necessary, where to park. If nothing else it reduces stress on the day.

### The day of the Inquiry for in-person events

50. Aim to arrive at the venue around 45-60 minutes before the Inquiry opens. This will allow you to:

- ensure the room and venue are suitable in terms of accessibility, overall setup and health and safety (see Annex H). If the room is unsatisfactory or requires furniture to be moved, request changes. You should not make them yourself unless they are very minor in nature. If you consider the facilities to be wholly unacceptable you should report this to the Council at once.

- Public inquiries; Hearings and Examinations - venue and facilities requirements guidance provides advice on the location of the venue and the layout of the Inquiry room.

- Check that you have water and that it is available for other parties.

<!-- Part1 p.197 -->

- Familiarise yourself with any microphone and livestream system. Ensure that someone is monitoring the livestream so that you can be advised if it fails.

51. Once you have set out your belongings, it is best to leave the room, taking your notes with you. If anyone wants to engage you in conversation about the appeal, ask them to raise it once you have opened the Inquiry. You can, however, and may well need to, discuss matters relating to the Inquiry venue (such as the layout of the room) with those present – preferably someone responsible for the venue, not a witness.

52. Return to the Inquiry room a few minutes before the Inquiry starts. While you wait to open the Inquiry you can use the time to check that the main parties are present, that the room is still set up as you would like and whether there is anything upon which you need to act (e.g. placards or external noise).

53. You may wish to greet the representatives of the parties. There is no harm in being polite and friendly but do so equally.

### The day of the Inquiry for (partly) virtual events

54. Ensure your device is fully charged and running on mains and that wi-fi is working and the camera is set up as you would like it, for example blurred background or PINS Inspector background. Make sure that you will not be disturbed by anybody, think about a sign on the door to stop any deliveries from disturbing you. Use your headphones if this would stop any background noise.

55. Ensure you have water to hand; you are comfortable and have everything you need in reach.

56. Enter the virtual room at 10:00. Ask advocates and current witnesses to keep their cameras on, but everyone else should have cameras off and be muted.

### Running the Inquiry

### The order of the Inquiry

57. Of the Rules governing the procedures at an Inquiry2 the most pertinent are:

- Rule 16(1): “except as otherwise provided in these Rules, the Inspector shall determine the procedure at an Inquiry”.

[^2]: Note that there are specific differences relating to enforcement and LDC Inquiries

<!-- Part1 p.198 -->

- Rule 16(4): the LPA shall begin and the appellant has the right of final reply (unless you determine otherwise). Other persons will be heard in such order as you determine.

- Rule 16(5): A person entitled to appear shall be entitled to call evidence and the appellant, LPA and any statutory party shall be entitled to cross-examine persons giving evidence.

58. The Inquiry will follow the running order agreed during the CMC and/or confirmed in your post-Conference note (subject to any changes arising since). An indicative Inquiry programme can be found at Annex F.

59. Inquiries normally follow the order below:

- Inspector’s opening announcements including confirmation of the main issues and the timetable, an outline of where you have been and what you have seen already, confirmation of who is representing the parties, and identification of anyone else present who wishes to speak. Seek the views of the parties as to whether an accompanied visit is necessary, and when.

- Opening statements (if you deem them necessary – the main parties will assume this) from the main parties (usually the Appellant followed by the LPA and any Rule 6 party).

- Evidence from any interested parties present who wish to speak.

- The evidence from the main parties using the method(s) agreed during the CMC, and subsequently. As well as the formal presentation of evidence and cross-examination, there may be round table sessions led by you.

60. After all the evidence has been heard, the rest of the Inquiry normally follows this order:

- Discussion of conditions and planning obligations by means of an Inspector-led round-table discussion.

- Site Visit if this has not taken place at another point during the inquiry.

- Closing submissions, usually beginning with the LPA, followed by any statutory or Rule 6 parties and ending with the Appellant.

- Cost applications if any.

- Closing of the Inquiry.

61. It is good practice to check that everyone has been heard before you move on from each stage and before you close the Inquiry.

### General Approach

65. Inquiries are more formal than Hearings. Witnesses are introduced by their advocates3. and there is a set procedure for giving evidence in chief, cross-examination and re-examination which is led by the advocates.

66. It is important that you demonstrate that you are in charge of proceedings. Avoid being tentative, passive or quiet. Clearly direct the transition between different stages of the Inquiry. For example:

“Mr A, you may now cross-examine Mrs B”.

“Mrs C, would you now call your 2nd witness”.

“We will now move on to the third main issue, which we are going to deal with in round-table format”.

67. Be prepared to intervene during the formal presentation of evidence rather than sitting inscrutably. Questions and interventions, notably to move cross-examination along or to ensure you understand a point, will reduce Inquiry time and assist the advocates. Interventions will also be necessary where there is unacceptable behaviour from advocates or witnesses.

68. Although you must retain an appropriate degree of formality, you are human and should appear so. You can smile and inject a degree of humour. Indeed, for many attendees this will be their first, and perhaps only, experience of a planning Inquiry. These can be daunting events and so you need to be able to put them at their ease. But do so carefully and avoid referring to controversial subjects or making light of the issues at the Inquiry.

69. Expert witnesses should sit at the witness table (but see the advice below about interested persons).

### Opening statements

73. This is where the main parties (including Rule 6 parties) briefly outline their overall case. You will know it already, but it will set the scene for what is to follow and can be particularly helpful to other persons who are present and may not be familiar with the entirety of the cases. The openings should also be submitted in writing and sent electronically to the Case Officer.

74. If opening statements are made, encourage brevity. 5-15 minutes should be enough for even the most complex of cases. They should not be used to recite or present evidence.

### Formal presentation of evidence

75. The order in which witnesses are usually heard is the LPA’s first, then any Rule 6 party, and finally the Appellant. The same order applies whether witnesses are grouped by party or by topic. However, this may not be the most suitable approach in cases (such as some Call-In cases) where the LPA is in favour of the development – in that case the applicant will present a more substantial case and should go first. Inspectors have the power to vary the procedure (Rule 14(4).

76. For each witness the following sequence is followed:

- Evidence-in-chief, led by their advocate.

- Cross examination by the opposing advocate(s) for the main party and by any opposing Rule 6 parties.

- Any other questions to the witness (from others wishing to speak at the Inquiry who are opposed to the party calling the witness).

- Re-examination by their advocate.

### Evidence-in-chief

77. This is where individual witnesses are taken through their evidence, as set out in their proof, by their own advocate. It is not necessary for the proof, or even a

<!-- Part1 p.203 -->

summary of it, to be read out in full. You should make this clear in your opening. Key paragraphs or sections may be read out if the party feels it provides useful context. Evidence in chief should be brief and to the point. Questions from the advocate to their witness should be neutral and non-leading (i.e. not leading the witness to a particular answer).

78. Evidence-in-chief is not always necessary, as it should not tell you anything that you will not already know from your reading of the proofs. It is important to ensure that advocates keep it brief. On receipt of timings from the parties, it can be helpful to review the length of time suggested for examination in chief and ensure that it is proportionate.

79. Evidence-in-chief may assist by:

- explaining something in more detail to you that you hadn’t quite grasped in the proof. You can make the advocates aware before the Inquiry by email or at the CMC if you wish them to focus on something specific.

- helping make others who are present aware of the case in more detail.

- allowing a nervous witness to settle before being cross-examined.

### Cross-examination

80. This is the key part of the Inquiry process. It is the point at which the evidence of one party is tested by the advocate(s) for the opposing party/parties in an adversarial format. Advocates may ask a series of questions, many of which will be leading, that are intended to take the witness towards a particular answer. This will often be aimed at making them concede points or parts of their evidence. The aim of the questioning may not always be clear at the outset and it is best to avoid intervening too early.

81. The advocates have a duty to assist the Inquiry, so be prepared to intervene when the questioning is not helping you or you have understood the point they are making. Do not be afraid to ask the witness to answer the question that is being asked of them either, if they are being evasive, or to reframe it for them if they do not appear to understand it. It is very helpful if the questions are framed to allow for a yes/no answer – with any qualifications. Stress this to the advocates and witnesses.

### Re-examination

82. This is where the advocate can ask questions of their own witness again, following their cross-examination by the opposing advocate. Generally, this will be used to clarify matters or recover ground that may have been lost in cross-examination. Emphasise that re-examination should be tightly focussed.

<!-- Part1 p.204 -->

83. It must only be directed at matters raised in cross-examination. Advocates should not introduce new points nor ask leading questions of the witness. If this occurs, you should ask the witness’s advocate to desist. Do not wait for an objection from the opposing advocate.

### Round-table sessions (RTS)

84. A RTS still allows for the robust testing and understanding of evidence but, as in a Hearing, the inquisitorial burden is upon you.

85. The main parties, any Rule 6 parties, and any interested persons who have indicated a wish to speak may take part in a RTS. Advocates may take part on the same basis as the other participants. It is for each party to decide whether their witness or their advocate (or both) is best placed to deal with a particular point. But remember that advocates are not professional witnesses – they can help but not give evidence.

86. RTS can be extremely useful in cutting down on inquiry time and allowing you to get the information that you need, direct from the parties. This is particularly so where there is little between the parties on certain issues.

87. RTS are often suited to cases where an issue relates, for instance, to the effects on views, impacts on heritage assets, or living conditions.

88. A RTS may also be useful when there is common ground between the main parties on an issue but significant objection from interested parties. You may wish to request a brief proof from the appellant’s witness, who could then be called to field questions from any interested parties and the Inspector.

89. The term “round table” describes the format of the discussion rather than the physical layout of the room. You lead the discussion, inviting the participants to make contributions about the points on which you wish to hear their views. You should ensure that all participants can have their say on each point, before you move on, and that the appellant has the final say.

90. Asking in advance for the evidence to be presented in summary table form can be helpful in highlighting the differences between the parties and informing any RTS agenda. You will need to issue an agenda, where necessary, for each RTS session ideally about a week before the event (although it's focus may change as the event progresses).

91. The following considerations may inform your approach:

- Have the main parties expressed a reasoned preference for a RTS? Are they agreed?

<!-- Part1 p.205 -->

- Even if they are agreed, but you don’t feel comfortable running a RTS because the subject matter is complex or novel, then you can require the evidence to be given formally.

- Are the matters raised by interested parties in dispute between the main parties? If not, how would the interested parties be best served? If there are lots of interested parties, a RTS might be unmanageable.

- How far apart are the main parties on the issue? If either party likely to change its position or concede anything in cross-examination or would a RTS with directed questions better assist your understanding of the evidence?

- With proportionality in mind, what would cross-examination really add to your understanding of an issue beyond the written evidence? How significant a factor is the issue likely to be in determining the outcome?

- If there is a lot of technical data but you are interested only in specific aspects of it, a focussed discussion with the professional witnesses can sometimes be the best and shortest way to get the necessary information.

- Will the Inquiry venue lend itself readily to the RTS (think about layout, microphones, audibility) so that all who want can readily take part?

Consider a different/new seating arrangement for the round table session, if that is possible, so that people are sitting loosely in a format that will facilitate the discussion. This can also emphasise to everyone present that this is different from the formality and structure of the other aspects of the event.

### Conditions and Planning Obligations

92. Conditions are usually best dealt with after all the witnesses and any others present have been heard, and by means of an Inspector-led RTS involving the Appellant, the LPA, any Rule 6 party and any others (e.g. statutory consultees) who wish (or need) to be involved.

93. It is good practice to get the parties to submit a set of agreed (or partly agreed) conditions well before the inquiry. You can issue a Tracked Changed version of conditions, with your suggestions or comments, well before the RTS to aid the discussion.

94. You will need to consider whether the suggested conditions meet the tests in the Framework, even if they have been agreed by the main parties (N.B. you may have had conditions suggested during the Inquiry. Do not forget to cover them).

<!-- Part1 p.206 -->

95. See the Conditions section of the ITM for further advice.

96. Planning Obligations are also best considered by means of an Inspector-led RTS. You will need to assess whether each obligation complies with the three tests set out in the relevant section of the Framework (and CIL Regulation 122 if relevant) and whether it would be effective (see Section 18 of the planning appeals: procedural guide)

97. If an obligation (or lack of one) was central to a main issue, it may already have been discussed. Even so, you might have questions about its format, wording and effectiveness. Keep an eye out for ‘blue or grey pencil clauses’ which require you to certify or delete provisions in the s106. In the case of grey pencil clauses you may be asked to pick a figure dealing with, for example, education contributions. This sort of clause requires you to do the calculations and justify them and should be avoided unless strictly necessary.

98. Inspectors should be vigilant to ensure that interested parties who are concerned with the terms of any obligation have an opportunity to inspect the draft and make comments thereon, so as to ensure natural justice. There is unlikely to be a need for a formal reconsultation or for this to be done routinely.

99. You should request a CIL Compliance Statement at the CMC, to be prepared by the LPA. This should contain a detailed justification for each obligation sought, setting out how it complies with the CIL Regulations and the Framework, how any financial sums have been reached and should include reference to any policy support for the obligations. It should be submitted no less than two weeks before the inquiry opens.

100. See the Planning Obligations chapter of the ITM for more advice.

### Closing submissions

101. Invite each party that called witnesses to make a closing submission. This will usually entail each advocate reading out a pre-prepared text, a copy of which will be provided to you and a copy sent electronically to the Case Officer.

102. Generally, it should not be necessary to interrupt a closing submission, but you should intervene if it appears that new evidence is being introduced, new points are being made or if anything is unclear.

103. You can ask the advocates at any point during the Inquiry to cover specific points in Closing. They may also state openly that they will leave certain points “to submissions” (code for Closings), usually if headway is not being made on something with a witness in cross-examination.

104. PINS have produced a guidance note for advocates as to the general form and contents of Closings for SoS cases. It is only directly relevant to those cases, but many of the principles are relevant to transferred cases SoS Casework.

<!-- Part1 p.207 -->

105. If the Inquiry lasts eight or more days, the closing submissions must be provided in writing (Rule 16(14)). That said, written submissions are nearly always provided in shorter Inquiries and are invariably helpful. You can request this at the start of the Inquiry, and/or at the CMC, and although you cannot strictly require them, it is now common practice.

106. Well-prepared closing statements can be very helpful when writing your decision as they will summarise the key points. Take careful notes if they are not submitted in writing, or if advocates depart from the script that they have given you. If a reference is made to a legal judgment, secure a full reference and get a copy of the judgment from the party that raised it.

107. It is important to arrange the programme so that the advocates have time, without having to work through the night, to prepare their closings in writing. It can also be helpful to hold closing submissions virtually (perhaps a few days after the final discussions), especially if the only reason to bring everyone back together after, say, a weekend adjournment is to hear the submissions. You can raise this during the CMC or during the event.

108. Sometimes, sequential closings in writing (with the LPA, then any R6 and finally the appellant) are suggested by the parties. Though not conventional practice this can save time – virtual closings can also help. Some Inspectors find that closings in writing are less helpful than in person or on Teams. Do not hesitate to say so if that is the case for you. There is also a risk that new points could be raised in the written versions, necessitating further exchanges between the parties.

109. If written closings are to be submitted, ensure that a submission timetable is agreed. You would then close the Inquiry in writing via the Case Officer, pending any applications for costs. You also need to ensure that the submissions are placed on the Inquiry website, for all interested parties, as soon as they become available to you.

### Costs applications

110. National guidance on the award of costs is provided in this link - Claim planning appeal costs: Overview. Detailed advice is also provided in the ITM chapter on Costs. All costs applications must be made formally before the Inquiry is closed and it is good practice for them to be submitted in writing.

111. To assist with timetabling, you should ask about the costs intentions of the parties during the CMC and in your opening. Even so, you should always provide the formal opportunity at the end of the Inquiry because matters may have arisen during evidence.

112. If both parties make applications these should be heard one after the other. Start with the LPA or R6.

<!-- Part1 p.208 -->

113. If the Inquiry is adjourned to another day, then any costs applications should be heard at the end of the resumed event.

114. If a costs application has been made in writing:

- Does the applicant intend to add anything to it, orally?

- Has the written application been provided beforehand to the other party and to you? If not, ensure copies are provided and, if necessary, allow an adjournment for both you and the other party to read the application.

- If it was provided beforehand, has the other side responded to it in writing? If so, do they have any further response? If they have not prepared a written response, they should be given the opportunity to respond orally.

- If you and the parties have had time to read and understand the application and any response, neither needs to be read out.

115. If the costs application is made, or added to, orally, the other side should be given the chance to respond, and the costs applicant should then be given the final chance to comment.

116. If the costs application and response are made orally, you will need to take a full note of both. Ask the parties to proceed at a steady pace. In some cases it may be reasonable, in the interests of fairness, to allow an adjournment so that a response to a costs application can be prepared.

### Site visit

117. Rule 17(1-3) provides that:

- You may make an unaccompanied site inspection, before or during the Inquiry, without giving notice.

- You may visit the site in the company of the Appellant, LPA and any statutory party during an Inquiry or after its close and shall do so if requested by the appellant or LPA.

- If you intend to make an accompanied site inspection this must be announced during the inquiry.

118. You should have made an unaccompanied visit to the surrounding area in advance of the Inquiry. Hopefully you will have been provided with an agreed itinerary by the main parties (you should request one either at the CMC or subsequently). Note that the provisions of the Act do not allow you to conduct an accompanied visit before the Inquiry opens, even where this is suggested/agreed at any CMC.

<!-- Part1 p.209 -->

119. This pre-Inquiry visit may be sufficient for you to see everything that you need to. In which case, you should make clear in your Opening where you have been and that you do not, at present, see the need for a further, accompanied, visit unless it is requested. But you should note that you may change your mind after hearing the evidence.

120. There is no reason why you cannot visit the area on your own again during the Inquiry.

121. If you feel that an accompanied site visit is necessary, or if it is requested by one of the parties, it must take place during or after the Inquiry as there is no provision for any ASV to occur before the Inquiry has opened. You may wish to timetable it so that the advocates can prepare their closing submissions during this time (as they would not normally attend the visit).

122. Given the Inquiry proceedings are based on the formal presentation and examination of evidence, it is not appropriate to allow discussion at the site visit. The purpose is for you to see the site and surroundings. Explain that you cannot listen to any representations/discussion/arguments, but that the parties can point out physical features.

123. If the visit is accompanied, representatives of both the Appellant, the LPA, and a R6 party representative must be present. Interested persons may attend, although if the site visit is to go on private land they will need the permission of the landowner. Try to discourage large numbers from attending by explaining the purpose of the visit and asking for one or two representatives to be appointed.

124. If you travelled to the venue by public transport, it may be expedient to accept a lift from one of the main parties. If so, you must ensure that representatives of both the appellant and the LPA travel with you in the vehicle. Explain this to any interested persons.

125. Some of the advice in the ITM Site Visits Chapter is also relevant, including on the conduct of the visit and about requests to view other sites in the area, taking photographs and health and safety.

### Closing the Inquiry

126. In many cases, you will agree to receive a completed s106 agreement after you have closed the Inquiry. Set a firm deadline, normally no more than two weeks later, for receipt. Make it clear that if the material is not received on time, it may be returned and that you will proceed to make your decision without it. Rarely would you accept any material other than this (see “Should I accept evidence after the Inquiry has closed?” below).

<!-- Part1 p.210 -->

127. You may be asked when your decision will be issued. There is no requirement in the Rules or advice in the Procedural Guide that says you must specify a date. You could, however, advise of the target dates.

128. Before you close the Inquiry, it is good practice to double check that everyone has said what they want to, that all matters have been covered and that you have received all necessary documents. If anyone does speak, ensure the appellant can provide the final comment. Thank everyone for their attendance and contributions, then close the Inquiry.

129. It may sometimes be necessary to close an Inquiry in writing. The most common occurrence of this is if, for example, you have agreed to the exchange of written closing submissions (see Closing Submissions).

### Your interventions at the Inquiry

130. As part of the management of the inquiry, it is important that you as Inspector play an active role. You should intervene during the event to:

- stop discourteous/disruptive, aggressive or bullying behaviour. Do this as soon as it first occurs.

- stop advocates trying to score points which are not directly relevant to your consideration of the planning merits of the case.

- ensure the witnesses answer the questions put to them.

- prevent questions which are repetitious, unhelpful, irrelevant, clearly designed to annoy or are beyond the witness’s area of expertise.

- stop cross-examination on legal matters unless this is clearly in the expertise of the witness. Such matters are normally dealt with in submissions rather than through the cross-examination of a non-lawyer by an advocate.

- prevent leading questions during evidence-in-chief and especially during re-examination.

- remind interested persons asking questions of a witness that it is not an opportunity for them to make a statement.

- move questioning along or ask for evidence to be focussed on specific areas, if that is most helpful to you.

131. You should issue a warning if anyone makes a potentially slanderous or discriminatory remark. See the Inspector Training Manual chapters on Human Rights and Equality and The Approach to Decision Making for more

<!-- Part1 p.211 -->

information. The advice in Annex K ‘Managing Disruptive Parties’ may also be relevant in this context.

132. Inquiries may be attended by large numbers of people who have strong feelings about the proposal. They are likely to be unfamiliar with the planning system and with Inquiry procedures. They may be frustrated by having to wait to present their case. You may need:

- act quickly to stop disruption, including audible whispering, general conversation, gasps, applauding, booing or unsolicited comments. It will usually be enough to stress the need for politeness and that you need quiet so that you can hear all the participants and that the procedures are designed to allow everyone to have their say.

- amend the programme (if it is not already set up appropriately) so that interested persons are allowed to speak as early as possible in the proceedings. This can help to defuse tension.

- call a short adjournment if things become heated, which can sometimes help restore calm.

133. If the approaches outlined above are not successful you have the power to:

- Refuse to permit irrelevant or repetitious evidence or cross-examination (Rule 16(6)). Be aware, however, that the Rules state that if you refuse to permit oral evidence, the person must be allowed to submit the evidence in writing before the close of the Inquiry.

- Require a person behaving disruptively to leave, refuse to permit the person to return or permit them to return subject to conditions. Even so you must allow any such person to submit any evidence in writing before the close of the Inquiry (Rule 16(9)).

- Proceed with an Inquiry in the absence of a person entitled to appear (Rule 16(11)). This should only be done in exceptional circumstances.

134. Only rarely will you find it necessary to give a formal warning or ask someone to leave. If you do, make a careful written note for future reference in the event of a complaint. If you have asked someone to leave and they refuse, or if disorderly behaviour is disrupting proceedings despite your best attempts to maintain control, you should ask that building security is contacted in the first instance.

<!-- Part1 p.212 -->

135. Your final option is for the police4 to be called, preferably by building security, and/or to adjourn to another day.

### Your questions

136. During the formal presentation of evidence you can, and should, intervene to ask questions of a witness. You can do this during evidence in chief or during cross examination. This might be to seek clarification on a particular point, to address something that you feel has not been covered adequately or to reframe a question being put by an advocate. It may also be helpful to clarify a position for the benefit of interested parties. This is rare during Opening or Closing although it may be necessary to clarify a statement.

137. Traditionally Inspectors reserve their questions of a witness until after cross-examination has finished. However, there may be times when it would be difficult to follow the evidence without intervening. If that is the case, you should do so – otherwise you may lose the flow of the evidence.

### Adjournments

138. Rule 16(13) allows you to adjourn an Inquiry.

139. Short adjournments may be necessary to allow:

- a party to read concise new evidence, which has been exceptionally accepted, and to prepare their response (or if you need to read it).

- the parties to discuss and seek agreement on a particular matter, such as the wording of conditions.

140. All adjournments must be to a definite time and place. This should be announced before adjourning (Rule 16(13)). For short adjournments it will usually be at a given time later the same day, or the next day, at the same venue.

141. Longer adjournments might be necessary to give the parties time to reach common ground on a technical issue, if you feel that there is a likelihood of resolving matters more quickly than through further cross-examination.

142. An adjournment will be required if the Inquiry overuns the time allocated for it. However, given the importance of agreeing overrun days and adherence to the

[^4]: Section 4(1)(a) of the Public Order Act 1986 relating to disorderly behaviour applies.

<!-- Part1 p.213 -->

timetable, this should be a very rare occurrence in exceptional circumstances. The resumption date must be as early as possible.

### Note taking

143. You need to record the discussion to assist you when writing your decision and in the event of any complaints or challenges. You do not, however, need to keep a word-by-word account. Focus instead on the main points made, particularly those which have not been set out in writing elsewhere.

144. A more thorough note will be needed if a costs application or legal submission is made orally. If necessary, you can ask the parties to slow down or repeat a point if you wish to make sure you record something accurately.

145. Be aware that your notes might be made available following a request from one of the parties (for example, in connection with a complaint or High Court challenge).

### Conduct of the parties at the Inquiry

### Interested persons

146. Interested persons (often also called ‘third parties’ or ‘interested parties’) may not be able to stay for all the proceedings. You cannot expect them to be familiar with the Inquiry process and the planning system, or to have full knowledge of the case. Accordingly:

- You may need to hear from people out of the normal order.

- Ensure that people do not feel intimidated by the proceedings or by any of the participants. It is your role to help put them at ease so that they can get their arguments across. Some people may feel more comfortable speaking from where they are sitting rather than from the witness table.

- Ask if they are prepared to be asked questions by the party they are opposing. Inform them that that, if not, it will decrease the weight that can be attached to their evidence (untested evidence legally carries less weight).

- You may need to help interested persons frame questions they may want to ask witnesses.

147. There is nothing in the Rules to prevent an interested person making an opening statement or a closing submission. This is quite unusual and is at the Inspector’s discretion. You should ascertain why they want to do this and, if allowed, their opening statements will usually be heard after the main parties have opened, and any closing submissions made before those of the

<!-- Part1 p.214 -->

Appellant. Many interested parties will not understand the distinction between opening/closing statements and their evidence – so there is a danger of repetition and unintended time wasting.

### Rule 6 parties

148. “Rule 6 status” allows interested parties to take an active part in the Inquiry, and they become main parties. However, it is not the case that for a group to participate in an Inquiry they have to seek Rule 6 status. It is unusual for Rule 6 status to be granted to individuals.

149. To have Rule 6 status, the third party must comply with the requirement to provide a Statement of Case within four weeks of being required to do so.

150. If it is a group/organisation making the request, they should provide information on whether or not they are incorporated (for example, a limited liability company), have a written constitution, officers, a membership list, hold regular meetings and have an address (for example, a registered address if a limited liability company). They will often be legally represented.

151. In most cases the Rule 6 party will prepare proofs of evidence and will take part in the same way as the Appellant and LPA. They will be invited to any Case Management Conference(s).

152. If there are multiple Rule 6 parties, it may be beneficial and more efficient to ask them to join forces, especially where they are presenting similar arguments. If they are unwilling to do so, you will need to manage the Rule 6 parties, before and during the Inquiry, to ensure that evidence is not repeated and time is not wasted.

153. Rule 6 parties have the same responsibilities as main parties and can be the subject of an award of costs for unreasonable behaviour.

154. You will need to adjust the running order to accommodate Rule 6 parties. If they are opposing the proposal any opening statement, witness evidence and closing submission from a Rule 6 party would usually follow those of the LPA.

155. Rule 6 parties can cross-examine the opposing party (usually the appellant), normally after the LPA advocate has done so. They may find that the LPA advocate has covered all their ground, and there is no need to repeat questions.

156. Further advice is provided in the ‘Guide to Rule 6 for interested parties involved in an Inquiry – planning appeals and called-in applications – England’.

### Discussion between an advocate and their witness

157. Once a witness has started giving evidence (Evidence in Chief), they should not be permitted to speak with anyone on their team until their re-examination

<!-- Part1 p.215 -->

has been completed. Consequently, where possible, it is best to avoid adjourning overnight or for weekends if a witness has not completed their session. If this is not possible you should remind the witness of the need to avoid communicating with members of their party.

### Who has the right to cross-examine?

158. Technically under Rule 16(5) only the Appellant, the LPA and statutory parties5 have the right to cross-examine persons giving evidence. In the interests of natural justice Rule 6 parties should always be allowed to cross-examine and interested persons may be allowed to ask questions of a witness giving evidence for the side they oppose. Make sure that questions do not repeat those already put by another advocate, and that they are questions rather than statements.

159. Statutory parties should normally be given the opportunity to present their case and cross-examine/ask questions before any other parties. This is convention rather that based on Rules.

### Can people ask a question of their own side?

160. There is nothing in the Rules preventing this. The general convention is, however, that a witness should not be cross-examined by their own side because the point of cross-examination is to test the evidence. Occasionally, however, Rule 6 parties or interested persons may want to ask a question of a witness who is on the same side as them. This will usually be if there are fundamental differences between them in some areas, even if they agree on others. Generally it can be confusing and is best discouraged.

### Advocates who are also witnesses

161. Sometimes professional persons may appear in a dual capacity as an advocate and as an expert witness. This should not normally present any difficulties. It is important to distinguish between the two roles, however, and they should sit at the witness table when giving evidence. They should not give evidence when acting as the advocate. For obvious reasons, they will be unable to re-examine themselves.

### Officers who disagree with their local authority

162. Elected members do not always accept the advice of their professional officers and, consequently, some decisions are made against the officers’ recommendation. This is perfectly legitimate and reflects the democratic process, of which Planning Committee decisions are a part.

[^5]: The term statutory party is defined in Rule 2(1).

<!-- Part1 p.216 -->

163. In these circumstances, it is for the LPA to decide whether to call officers as witnesses. Section 15 of the Procedural Guide: Planning Appeals explains the duty of an expert to help the Inspector. Expert evidence is evidence that is given by a person who is professionally qualified to express an opinion on a particular subject. This duty overrides any duty the expert may have to the party that involved them in the appeal or that is paying them (this applies equally to appellant witnesses). The evidence should be accurate, concise, and complete and should represent the expert’s honest and objective opinion.

164. If the officer who gave the original advice does represent the LPA, therefore, it would be reasonable for the opposing advocate to ask the officer questions about their own professional views and any advice they provided to the LPA.

165. In most such cases the LPA will employ a consultant or use a different officer or an elected member to give evidence instead.

### Appearances by more than one authority

166. In areas where there are two tiers of local government the authority responsible for dealing with the application is the local planning authority for the purpose of the Rules (Rule 2(1)).

167. The other authority has, however, a right to appear and give evidence (Rule 11(1)(c)). They are not entitled to cross-examine witnesses and can only do so at the Inspector’s discretion.

168. If there are significant differences in the cases of the two authorities, you may wish to permit the other authority to cross-examine on these matters. Cross-examination must be permitted if a refusal to allow it would result in a denial of natural justice.

169. Any attempt to cover the same ground should be prevented and where there is no significant difference between the two cases, you should make it clear that only the party with the right to do so will be allowed to cross-examine.

170. Where any local authority has expressed an adverse view in writing, which has been included in the LPA’s statement of case, that local authority may be required to make a representative available at the Inquiry (Rule 12). You may wish to hear from them if they have made independent representations.

171. The representative may be called as a witness by the LPA. Alternatively, the authority may wish to present its own case, particularly if its evidence is contrary to that of the LPA.

### Representatives of organisations (non-Rule 6)

172. Check the position of anyone who states that they are representing an organisation. Do they have authority to represent that organisation?

<!-- Part1 p.217 -->

173. It can also be helpful to know the number of members and how the organisation arrived at their position on the appeal. Was there, for example, a vote at a meeting or a committee decision? This is something the opposing advocate may ask.

### Representatives of government departments

174. Where the Secretary of State or Historic England has given a direction6 or the Secretary of State, another Minister of the Crown or a government department or certain other bodies have expressed a view about the proposal7, the Appellant, LPA or a person entitled to appear can apply in writing for a representative of those bodies to appear at the Inquiry (Rule 12(1)).

175. Rule 12(2) states that in these circumstances the Secretary of State shall make a representative available. That person shall give evidence and be subject to cross-examination (Rule 12(3)).

176. Rule 12(4) requires that the representative shall not be required to answer a question which is directed to the merits of government policy (although such questions can be asked; it is up to the representative to decide how or if they should respond).

177. The LPA may call such representatives as witnesses. Otherwise, they should normally be called upon to give their evidence independently at an early stage in the proceedings or in the appropriate topic area.

178. Departmental evidence must be made available to the other parties. The departmental witnesses are required by Rule 12 to give evidence and to be subject to cross-examination to the same extent as other witnesses. The balancing of departmental views against other material considerations is a matter for the Secretary of State or the Inspector.

179. Sometimes representatives of a government department attend the Inquiry other than in pursuance of the above rule. They may then appear on their own or be called by a party. You should give them the same protection against questioning on the merits of government policy.

### Assessors/specialist advisors

180. An Assessor is a specialist adviser, usually scientific or technical, selected to assist you by hearing, testing and weighing evidence of a specialised nature that may be outside your experience but which may have an important bearing

### Issues that might arise during or after the Inquiry

### What if a main party is not present?

181. If a witness or advocate for the main parties, including any Rule 6 party, is not present at the appointed time you should still open the Inquiry.

182. You should them adjourn, initially for 15-20 minutes, to allow contact to be made, by a member of their team, with the absent person. It is most likely that the person is ill, has been delayed while travelling or has gone to the wrong venue.

183. If there is no prospect of the absent person attending, and you have no reason to believe that they have behaved irresponsibly, you will need to consider if the parties can continue their case without them or adjust the programme accordingly. Be very careful that the party is not prejudiced. Continue only exceptionally.

184. In most cases, the preference will be for the Inquiry to be rearranged. You may be able to agree a resumption date with the parties. Otherwise, adjourn the Inquiry and contact the Case Officer who will write to the parties.

185. If you consider that it is possible to carry out the appeal by the written representations’ procedure (instead of rearranging the Inquiry), you should first seek the views of principal/statutory parties and/or any interested parties you deem necessary.

186. If you consider that the missing party has acted deliberately and irresponsibly then consider the implications for costs.

### What if the venue is not large enough?

187. If it becomes clear that the venue is not large enough to accommodate all of those who wish to attend, you will need to open the inquiry, seek the views of the main parties and then, as necessary, adjourn to allow the LPA to find a more suitable space.

188. Do not accept a suggestion that people should be admitted on a first come, first served basis, or that attendance should be prioritised. If the venue is changed, ensure that the LPA has a representative to re-direct people, and/or signs are erected at the original venue.

189. If you think there will be a high number of interested parties, you should request at the CMC that the event is livestreamed. Confirmation of this should be included in the time, date and venue notification letter. See also “Should I Allow Filming and Recording?”.

### Rulings

190. You will make many rulings without even realising, as often they are just directions that you give. You may be asked, however, to make a formal ruling at any stage of the Inquiry. This might, for example, be about:

- whether you will accept new evidence or revised plans.

- whether a procedural problem may have led to unfairness, which needs to be remedied.

- whether the appeal or application is valid.

- whether the Inquiry should be adjourned for some reason.

191. Ask the parties for their views. Hear from the party/parties making or supporting the request first, then from anyone opposing it. Ask any questions you may have.

192. It is good practice to adjourn to consider the matter and write out your ruling – which you should read out on resumption.

193. When considering a ruling, bear in mind the following:

- natural justice: the ruling should not put any party at a material disadvantage.

- your own interests: provided there is no breach of natural justice, a point may be resolved based on how you may best be helped.

- a breach of the Rules does not itself invalidate the proceedings or require redress. If no-one is at a disadvantage, the breach is unlikely to be serious.

- a simple common-sense ruling is more likely to be appropriate than one which is complex or is based on complicated reasoning.

- it is best to make your ruling at the Inquiry. In some exceptional circumstances, however, it may be preferable to resolve the dispute in your decision.

- Where relevant, your ruling will carry greater weight if made in pursuance of a power afforded to you under the Rules.

- Do not be rushed into giving a rapid ruling.

194. If, after making your ruling, a party persists in objecting, you should advise them that you intend to proceed with the Inquiry but if they have a complaint, they should contact the Customer Team. Alternatively, they would have the option of

<!-- Part1 p.220 -->

applying for judicial review or, once the decision had been issued, lodging a High Court challenge.

195. You should never say that a ruling has been based on instructions or advice from the office as you alone make all rulings.

### What if the LPA no longer intends to defend a reason for refusal or withdraws its entire case?

196. The LPA may announce at the start of the Inquiry, or before it, that they no longer intend to defend a particular reason for refusal or withdraw their opposition completely. This may often occur in cases where the refusal has been against officer advice and the withdrawal is on the basis of subsequent legal advice..

197. Even though this may have been made clear in writing, it is helpful to ask the LPA to explain the reasons for their position, particularly if other people are attending the Inquiry. This could be in the form of a short opening statement. It is important to ascertain if the Council considers that the proposal is still in conflict with the development plan but other considerations outweigh this, or if the proposal is now considered to comply with the development plan.

198. The LPA may state that it no longer intends to present any evidence on certain matters. Or at all. Even so, if you have questions or if there are interested persons who continue to oppose the proposal on these grounds, you may request that an LPA witness is made available to answer questions.

199. If the issue is a technical one (such as traffic or noise) it can be useful to hold a session where the expert witnesses for the LPA and Appellant share the witness table and answer any questions from you and other parties in turn. In any case the LPA would still be expected to take part in the discussions about conditions and obligations.

200. The Appellant may still want to present their evidence-in-chief on the subject, particularly if there are third-party objections. Similarly, they may wish to ask questions of interested persons opposing the proposal. You should allow this.

201. If the LPA declines to present evidence on a topic or as a whole they should not be allowed to cross-examine the Appellant. The evidence of the Appellant will be un-tested, therefore, except by any questions that you or interested persons raise.

202. It is becoming increasingly common for an LPA to withdraw its entire case before the Inquiry. This is often in cases where officers have recommended approval, but Members have refused permission. Close to the Inquiry they reverse their position.

<!-- Part1 p.221 -->

203. The question at that point is how best to proceed with the appeal. It is at the Inspector’s discretion whether to continue by way of written representations. (bearing in mind that a position statement explaining the LPA’s position would be needed and the public notified), proceed by way of a Hearing, or continue with a (truncated) Inquiry. Experience shows that the latter is often the most efficient if the change in the LPA stance is relatively close to the opening date.

204. This approach enables interested parties to put their case, for the Inspector to ask questions of witnesses to clarify matters, and for the LPA to set out its position. The detail of such a short Inquiry will vary in each case, but little more than half a day is usually needed to ventilate outstanding issues and get the answers to the Inspector’s questions. If this is an option you are considering seek advice from your IM or PfL on the running order and procedure.

### What if there are no notification letter(s) or site notice?

205. There should be two notification letters (Rules 4(4)(b) and 10(5)). The first notifying relevant parties about the lodging of the appeal and the second about the Inquiry arrangements. A site notice should also have been posted or a press notice published.

206. Check that the copies of the letters and site notice you receive from the LPA are correctly dated, relate to the correct appeal and venue and have been sent to the right people. It is important that you request a copy of both letters, if they are not already on the file, at least two weeks before you open the Inquiry to ensure they have been sent. This could avoid abortive delays.

207. If the correct notification has not taken place you will need to decide whether to postpone/adjourn the Inquiry to address this deficiency. You will only need to do this if you consider that there is a significant risk that the interests of any person or party could be prejudiced. Were they, for example, deprived of the opportunity to attend or to respond to evidence? Seek the views of the parties, as necessary. Be pragmatic but try to keep the inquiry on course.

### What if late evidence is offered at the Inquiry?

208. Rule 16(10) states that you may allow any person to alter or add to their full Statement of Case. Rule 16(12) allows you to consider any written representation or evidence or any other document received by you before the Inquiry opens or during it (if you disclose it at the Inquiry).

209. It is best to establish at the start of the Inquiry if anyone intends to submit new evidence or documents and what the exceptional circumstances are which might justify late evidence. The PINS start letter makes it clear to the parties that late evidence will only be accepted in exceptional circumstances. Look at the PINS Procedural Guide for practical guidance on what may/may not be accepted. Why was it not submitted in accordance with the PINS/Inquiry timetable?

<!-- Part1 p.222 -->

210. If you are offered late evidence at the Inquiry, you will need to decide whether to exercise your discretion and accept it. If you do so, consider how this can best be handled – do you need a short adjournment at that point, or is it something which can be read overnight – thereby not using Inquiry time.

211. When doing so you should have regard to the case of Wainhomes & Cornell v SSCLG & Wiltshire Council At Paragraph 10 the Judge explains that:

“the late submission of additional materials is liable to produce inefficiency, delay, increased expense and, at worst, injustice. However, it is inevitable that there will be occasions when information that is material to an Inspector's decision will become available for the first time at a date which prevents compliance with the normal framework and rules. Against that eventuality the Inspector has a discretion to admit materials which have not been provided in accordance with the normal procedural timetable.”

212. If the new evidence relates to a material change in circumstances that occurred very close to the inquiry, and which could not possible have been identified earlier, then it should probably be accepted.

213. You may also be presented with evidence that pre-dated the appeal start letter or does not relate to a change in circumstances. In which case you should critically consider:

- Whether the late evidence is material, and

- If it is material, would rejecting that evidence be a proper, and thus lawful, exercise of your discretion to accept or reject evidence in the circumstances prevailing?

214. For evidence to be rejected, you need to undertake a balance where you weigh the factors in favour of and against accepting the late evidence and come to a reasoned view (Para 49 of Wainhomes).

215. Even material evidence can be rejected if there are sound grounds to do so. Perhaps, for example, the appellant decided to try and submit an entirely new Transport Assessment at the Inquiry. That would be material evidence but is it’s unlikely to be fair or appropriate given the delay that would be caused if it is accepted.

216. Therefore, to arrive at a reasoned view on whether to accept late evidence or not, you will require an explanation from the party submitting about why, exceptionally, it was not received by PINS in accordance with the rules and how the material is relevant.

217. You will also need to consider whether it would be procedurally fair to all parties, including interested parties, if the late evidence is considered. You will need the parties’ views on this.

<!-- Part1 p.223 -->

218. The Procedural Guide makes it clear that if the Inspector accepts late evidence unrelated to a change in circumstances, then another party may make an application for costs, or the Inspector may initiate an award of costs8. A warning should be issued about that possibility. This will allow the party wishing to submit late evidence time to reflect.

219. Where late evidence has been provided and accepted at an event, ensure that it is added to the PINS electronic file and the Inquiry CD documents.

220. The same principles apply if an interested person requests that you accept late evidence. However their material is less likely to be substantial enough to warrant an adjournment.

221. Keep a running list of any documents received, as these will need to be listed in your decision/recommendation. You may also wish to nominate the LPA/appellant to keep a track of this list and provide you with a final list of Inquiry documents at the end of the Inquiry.

### Should I accept evidence after the Inquiry has closed?

222. Rule 18(2) states that the Inspector may disregard any written representations, evidence or documents received after the close of the Inquiry. The intention of the Rule is to ensure that the parties provide evidence and documents, including s106 obligations, on time. There may be occasions, however, where you do need to accept late evidence.

223. For example, important matters may arise after the Inquiry has closed but before you have made your decision. This could include a change in national or adopted local planning policy or the issuing of a clearly relevant appeal decision. A failure to take these into account could leave a decision vulnerable to challenge in the High Court9.

224. Issues may be brought to your attention by one of the parties or they may be apparent to you for other reasons. In either case, if the issue is one which might reasonably have a material bearing on your decision, you should accept the evidence offered or proactively raise the issue.

225. Rule 18(3) states that if, after the close of an Inquiry, you propose to take new evidence into account which was not raised at the Inquiry you shall afford those entitled to appear at the Inquiry with an opportunity to make written representations or to ask for the re-opening of the Inquiry.

### Amended plans and proposals

227. If amended plans have been provided with the appeal or during the appeal process (including at the Inquiry itself), you will need to decide whether you intend to determine the appeal based on these plans or on those which were before the LPA when it made its decision. In most cases this question will have been resolved during the CMC. If it arises at the Inquiry, you should seek the views of the main parties.

228. Applying the principles from the Holborn Studios v LB Hackney [2017] EWHC 2823 (Admin) judgement, you will need to decide if accepting the revised plans would pass the substantive and procedural tests.

229. The substantive test is whether the proposed amendment(s) involves a "substantial difference" or a "fundamental change" to the application.

230. The procedural test is whether the proposed amendment(s) would cause unlawful procedural unfairness to anyone involved (in the appeal) and, if so, whether such unfairness could be cured, for example by re-consultation.

231. Further advice is provided in Part 1 of The Approach to Decision Making ITM chapter and Section 16 of the Procedural Guide: Planning appeals – England.

This is particularly important if you consider that your decision could turn upon matters that may already have been agreed by the main parties (for example, in a SoCG, about which you have concerns, or upon matters that they have not considered10.

### What if the appellant wishes to withdraw the proposal at the Inquiry?

235. If this happens on your arrival at the event you do not have to formally open the Inquiry. However, the withdrawal of the appeal must be confirmed to you there and then in writing. You should also ensure that any interested parties arriving for the Inquiry are made aware that the appeal has been withdrawn.

236. If the Inquiry has opened, the appellant can withdraw the appeal orally at the Inquiry. Always ask for confirmation of the withdrawal in writing.

237. If the appeal is withdrawn during an adjournment to a different day the Inquiry can be closed in writing. You will need to make sure all parties are informed. If the appeal is withdrawn very close to the day of resumption, it may be necessary to resume the Inquiry briefly and then close it in person. In either case, ensure that the Case Officer writes to all parties to confirm that the appeal has been withdrawn.

238. If any party seeks to apply for costs, refer them to the costs section of the Procedural Guide: Planning Appeals, which advises that any applications should be made to the Planning Inspectorate within four weeks of receiving confirmation that the appeal has been withdrawn.

### What if the validity of the appeal or application is challenged?

239. Listen to the arguments put to you. Unless the interests of a party have been seriously prejudiced you should continue with the Inquiry. A breach of the Rules (including the displaying and advertising of notices) does not in itself invalidate the proceedings. If no party is prejudiced the breach is unlikely to be serious.

240. If objections persist you may need to advise the person making them that, although you intend to continue with the Inquiry, they should make their concerns known in writing to the Case Officer and have the right to challenge the appeal decision in the High Court.

### Requests for recovery of jurisdiction by the Secretary of State

241. You may be asked to refer the appeal to the Secretary of State. If so, note the arguments put forward and inform the parties that consideration will be given to seeking the Secretary of State's ruling as to whether jurisdiction should be recovered. The matter must be brought to the attention of the Case Officer immediately so that a decision on recovery can be made.

### Hearing evidence under oath

242. You have the statutory authority at an Inquiry to take evidence on oath or under an affirmation.

243. Hearing evidence on oath is most unlikely to be necessary at most s78 Inquiries, although it might occur where factual evidence is disputed. It is more common in Enforcement and similar Inquiries.

244. Further advice is provided in the Enforcement ITM Chapter and for advice on oaths at virtual / blended / Hybrid events, see ‘Advice on oaths at virtual and other Inquiries’.

### Requests to summons a witness

245. It may be that a representative of a body from which you wish to hear will not agree to attend the Inquiry. If this persists, following clear statements that attendance will be very helpful to you, which will often achieve the desired result, you have the power under s250(2) of the Local Government Act 1972 to issue a witness summons. It is a power that is used very rarely and should be exercised only as the very last resort. In any case, although you can compel someone to attend, you cannot require them to speak – so the exercise may be pointless. Seek advice from your IM or a PfL before using this power. For more information see Annex D.

### Should I hear any matters in private?

246. Section 321 of the 1990 Act requires that Inquiries are held in public, subject to very limited exceptions related to national security. Oral evidence shall be heard in public and documentary evidence shall be open to public inspection.

247. In such cases the Secretary of State can direct that the hearing of specific evidence be in private. If this suggestion arises, seek guidance from your IM or a PfL.

248. Commercial confidentiality or the privacy of individuals is not a sufficient justification for an in-camera session. Such requests must be rejected.

249. However it sometimes occurs that a party indicates that there is a personal matter which they need to discuss in private, as it could have an effect on how the inquiry progresses.

250. Inspectors must not have conversations with a single party, but it is important that you respond sensitively to this sort of occurrence. It might be that a witness (or an advocate) is unwell or has a personal issue (such as a bereavement) which they naturally do not want discussed in an open forum.

251. Under these circumstances you should invite the advocates for both parties (and any Rule 6 party) - perhaps with the person concerned if appropriate – to

<!-- Part1 p.227 -->

discuss the matter in your retiring room. It is impossible to be proscriptive about how you might resolve the issue – you will have to balance the need to progress the inquiry with the need to be sensitive to the particular issue.

252. When you resume the inquiry you should announce that you have a conversation with representatives of the main parties about a particular personal issue which has arisen. You have not been hearing evidence. You should not give any further explanation but announce any effect on the inquiry (especially related to timetabling).

253. All the above applies equally if you have a personal issue which requires a discussion with the parties.

### Should I allow filming and recording?

254. The presumption is that filming and recording will be allowed. You should ask in your opening if anyone intends to film or record the event. If so, check that everyone is comfortable with this (some may not, for example, wish to have their faces shown or voice recorded).

255. If there are concerns, you can ask that filming/recording is restricted to certain angles. It is never appropriate to film children or vulnerable adults even if no objections are raised. If filming/recording does take place, ask that it is carried out responsibly.

256. Inquiries may be live streamed (and therefore often automatically recorded). The LPA should normally arrange for the livestreaming, as they will usually be providing the venue (but there is no reason why the Appellant cannot do so). The livestreaming should generally be arranged either prior to or at the CMC, but increasing numbers of people or specific requests for livestreaming may change the position. If the Inquiry is to be livestreamed (and recorded), you should advise participants of this and ensure that the information is included in the Council’s notification letter to local residents if it is known at that stage

257. In circumstances where there are factors which outweigh the public interest in allowing the Inquiry to be filmed or recorded, it may be necessary for you to prevent or restrict it. If the venue has restrictions on filming in place, then confirmation will be needed before the Inquiry that those restrictions can be lifted.

258. If PINS receives a request to film or record beforehand, the Press Office or the Case Officer will inform you that this is being proposed.

### High profile events

260. Some events will attract a significant number of interested parties, demonstrations and scrutiny by the press. It is essential that you refer to the Role of the Inspector chapter for full detail on how to deal with these circumstances.

### Audibility, linguistic or literacy difficulties

263. If there is no PA system and someone advises that they cannot hear the discussion, invite them to sit closer. Ask the parties to speak up and to look up when speaking. If it seems that audibility will be a continuing problem, for example, if there are large numbers of people present, consider a short so that microphones can be arranged.

264. If the livestream or Teams link goes down, you should immediately pause proceedings. Interested parties have been invited to a Public Inquiry, and whether they are listening or actively participating, it would be prejudicial to exclude them. In most cases the link can be restored rapidly, and when that happens you need to explain when you resume that the inquiry has not progressed in the meanwhile. If this takes longer you may have to adjourn until it can be restored.

265. ‘The venue and facilities for public Inquiries and hearings’ states that venues should have an installed and operational hearing loop and that a sign language interpreter should be arranged if necessary. These issues may be flagged up at the CMC or may arise on the day.

266. Some participants may not have a good understanding of English or may have poor literacy skills. See the advice in the Inspector Training Manual chapter on Human Rights and Equality. This may involve finding someone who can assist the participant.

### Procedures at a re-opened Inquiry

270. Although uncommon, there are various reasons why an Inquiry may be re-opened. This may include new evidence, new issues of fact or matters relating to natural justice. It may be at the request of the parties, the Secretary of State or at your own discretion. Consult your IM or a PfL if you have this in mind.

271. When new evidence or opinion is to be considered, the relevant witness(es) must attend the re-opened Inquiry to give their views on this evidence only.

272. When a new issue of fact has caused the Inquiry to be re-opened, the parties will have been told what it is, and they will be entitled to submit any evidence that reasonably bears upon it. It may not be necessary for anyone to attend and give evidence, although you can explain how you would like to proceed. If anyone does appear, this will be on the terms set out in the preceding paragraph.

273. When re-opening the Inquiry, you should emphasise that the proceedings are strictly limited to the consideration of the specific topic(s) or matter(s) requiring further examination.

274. Ideally, the re-opened Inquiry is taken by the original Inspector. If this is not possible, and it has fallen to you, you should prepare as you would for any other Inquiry, albeit the focus may be very narrow. Should you be faced with this discuss the position with your IM and/or PfL.

275. The usual procedure for your opening should be complied with. Before any representations are heard, you should explain the procedure to be adopted. If there are any procedural objections you should hear them and, if possible, resolve them by agreement. It may be relevant to consider what additional conditions, if any, might be imposed in relation to the matters discussed.

### Call-in applications and recovered appeals

285. Under s77 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 the Secretary of State may “call-in” planning applications for them to make the decision instead of the LPA. ### Programme Officers

290. A Programme Officer may be appointed to assist you in the administrative and procedural aspects of a long Inquiry, particularly one in which there are many participants. They can be of considerable benefit to you. If you feel that a Programme would be potentially helpful consult your IM and request one. A good Programme Officer can save time at the event and relieve some of the administrative burden on the Case Officer and the Inspector beforehand.

291. The Programme Officer must be accepted and recognised by all as an officer of the Inquiry responsible to and under the sole direction of you. During the pre-Inquiry period, and throughout the Inquiry itself, the Programme Officer must be, and must be seen to be, completely impartial. It is essential that the Programme Officer can deal directly with the public.

292. You should make these points at the CMC and at the opening of the Inquiry. It is very useful for a programme Officer to be appointed as soon as possible so they can attend the CMC.

293. It is normally the case that a Programme Officer is appointed by the LPA. There are many independent Programme Officers, or the LPA may provide an officer unconnected with the proposal. If the appointed Programme Officer is unfamiliar with the role, as may well be the case with an LPA officer, it is essential that the Inspector contacts them at an early stage to agree the role and expectations.

294. The Programme Officer could be responsible for:

- organising the Inquiry programme, dealing with procedural queries relating to the programme and managing event logistics (including room set up and day-to-day queries from attendees).

- providing a link between you and the participants.

- facilitating site visits, whether accompanied or otherwise.

- receiving and distributing all Inquiry documents and ensuring that all documents received before and during the Inquiry are available in an up-to-date online Inquiry library.

295. The Programme Officer should be provided with an office outside the Inquiry room. Their contact details will need to be available to all those with an interest in the appeal.

### Inspector Panels

296. Occasionally, a case may require a panel of Inspectors. This will be due to the length or complexity of the case being determined; recent examples are the Bristol and Luton Airport appeals and the Cambridge Guided Busway. There will always need to be a Lead Inspector, although just the term ‘Inspector’ should be used on a daily basis. The role of each Inspector is to work on aspects of the appeal both before, during and after the Inquiry and to write part of the decision/report. The overall report and conclusions/recommendations should be jointly agreed.

297. It is for the Inspectors to agree what tasks each panel member undertakes, under the guidance of the Lead Inspector. Each Inspector may:

- Read the evidence on particular topics, including supporting evidence, and prepare any questions on those topics.

- Conduct sessions of the Inquiry on specific topics.

- Draft parts of the report, including (but not exclusively) sections on particular topics where they conducted the Inquiry session.

298. All the Inspectors should, if possible, attend the CMC and all sessions of the Inquiry and any site visits. In the event of an Inspector becoming ill after the Inquiry has been opened, it may be possible for the Inquiry and decision/report to proceed if this is appropriate. In that way the need to re-start or re-open the Inquiry would be avoided.

299. If you are provided with the assistance of one of our APOs, they will have been selected to assist you in administrative tasks and can help with things like summarising representations or reports. They do not need to join you at any part of the event/CMC, unless this has been agreed as a development opportunity for them, but if they do you should briefly introduce them and explain their functions. It should be made clear that they have no part in the decision making process and that any recommendations / conclusions in your report / decision remain yours.

### Evening sessions

300. Evening sessions may occasionally be necessary when there are a high number of interested parties who wish to speak, and/or if there are a high number of potential attendees who are not free during the day. Virtual participation may reduce the need for/length of evening sessions.

<!-- Part1 p.234 -->

301. You should seek the agreement of the main parties well in advance, to ensure that they are able to accommodate the evening session, given potential domestic commitments or logistical issues.

302. Evening sessions can be beneficial in facilitating public access to the event, but must not result in an excessively long day for anyone – including the Inspector. The main session should adjourn by early/mid afternoon, to enable everyone to take a decent break.

303. An evening session needs to be arranged and controlled carefully. You must make it clear that it is part of the Inquiry and not a public meeting. All speakers must observe the normal Inquiry rules, addressing you rather than the public at large and not repeating points already made (although be mindful that speakers may be inexperienced and be nervous). All attendees should be respectful, refraining from commenting, clapping or cheering.

304. If possible, the Programme Officer or an LPA representative should collect in advance a list of those wishing to speak, together with a brief outline of the points they wish to make. You should hear first from those who have requested to speak, before asking if any other attendees wish to speak.

### Controlling the pace of a long Inquiry

305. You need to set a reasonable pace, as Inquiries that go on for many weeks are tiring for all concerned. Especially for those who have to travel long distances.

306. You should ensure that you have adequate time for preparation; your programme immediately before the event should not include cases of significant size. All outstanding work should be completed before a long inquiry begins – otherwise timeliness will suffer and it will be hard to get back to an earlier case when you emerge from a long inquiry.

307. The Inquiry programme should be based on a programme running from Tuesday to Thursday and a half day on a Friday, or simply Tuesday to Thursday if your own or the parties’ travel distance is considerable.

308. Be realistic when estimating how long the various stages of the Inquiry will take and understand that there will be considerable amounts of work taking place ‘behind the scenes’ by the main parties, who may need time to address matters between themselves. You may also need time to catch up and/or prepare for subsequent sessions.

309. You may find that an adjournment of a week part way through a multi-week Inquiry is beneficial to allow you and the parties time to consolidate the outcomes of the previous weeks. This possibility should be flagged up at the CMC and incorporated into the programme - this is not wasted time and would

<!-- Part1 p.235 -->

be unlikely to delay the decision/report as you can be working on the first stage(s) of the inquiry in the gap.

310. Inspector Managers facing a long Inquiry should ask a fellow IM to deal with day-to-day queries from members of their groups.

### Joint Inquiries and non-planning cases

311. Some joint Inquiries are difficult to programme. An example is a joint Inquiry into an application for planning permission and a CPO relating to the same site, where the relevant procedural rules cannot be followed strictly.

312. When considering the programme of an Inquiry other than a s78 case, it should be remembered that the party that is asking the Secretary of State to do something should normally go first and end last. Thus, if the subject of the Inquiry is the confirmation of a CPO, the order making authority should go first.

313. If the Secretary of State is making a proposal, such as a modification or revocation order, their representative should make their statement first.

314. You should declare at the outset what variant of the Rules should be applied and ask the main parties to consent to it. The procedures customarily followed in Inquiries under the Highways Acts should not, however, be used in other types of case. If you are in doubt about the best and fairest procedure, first seek advice from your IM of IPL, and seek the parties agreement.
