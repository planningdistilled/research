# 02 Site Visits: Site Visits

<!-- Part1 p.122 -->

## Site Visits

4. For most people the site visit will be the first, and possibly last, time they encounter an Inspector or their representative. Therefore, the way you conduct the site visit is extremely important.

5. Your site visit must be carried out in accordance with the Franks’ Principles (Openness, Fairness, and Impartiality) and the Conflicts of Interest policy. The advice in this guide will help you do this and further information about the Code and the Franks’ Principles can be found in the ITM Chapter ‘Role of the Inspector’.

6. You should always take your PINS ID card and have a supply of “Calling cards”. Make sure your car does not have any badges or stickers which might cause people to doubt your impartiality. For the same reason you should not wear or display anything that identify an organisation or society.

7. A site visit is not an opportunity for anyone present to discuss the details of the appeal or the written evidence they may have previously provided. The Inspector, or any PINS representative, should not allow any discussion about the case with anyone at a site visit. The only exception would be where the Inspector, or PINS representative, has asked any invited parties to point out physical features that they have referred to in their written evidence or to clarify other factual matters.

[^1]: See the Planning Inspectorate’s homepage on GOV.UK for more information.

<!-- Part1 p.123 -->

8. Regardless of the type of visit you are undertaking; you should not leave the site until you have seen everything you need to allow you to write a well-reasoned decision.

9. There are 3 types of site visit unaccompanied (USV), access required (ARSV) and accompanied (ASV). The majority are ARSV or USV. This chapter, therefore, first refers to these types of visit and the steps to be taken in advance of those visits. Information regarding ASVs is included at the end of the chapter.

Unaccompanied (USV)

10. The parties to the appeal will not attend and you will not normally be able to enter the appeal site because you will not have the appellant’s agreement to do so. You would normally only view the appeal site and its surroundings from the road, a public right of way or some other public vantage point and would not normally go onto neighbouring sites. It is important that as part of the visit you DO NOT go onto private land unless expressly authorised to do so.

11. If you are unable to see everything you need from public land in order to reach a sound decision you may need to abandon the site visit. You should inform the Case Officer straightaway and if it is possible for you to keep the case you should do so, otherwise the case will be re-allocated to another Inspector.

12. If you are approached by the appellant or neighbours during the USV, briefly and politely explain the purpose of the visit, note that you cannot listen to any comments or representations and that it is necessary for you carry out the visit unaccompanied. Do not get drawn into conversation about the appeal.

13. If, when carrying out a USV, you decide that it is essential to go on the appeal site, the Procedural Guide – Planning Appeals – England indicates that you can “approach the occupants to gain permission/access”. If you follow this approach you will need to explain very clearly the purpose of your visit and that you cannot enter into any dialogue.

14. PINS provides a ‘calling card’ for Inspectors to use where they were expecting access to be provided but the owner/occupier did not answer. The card is not meant to be used as a replacement for calling. Neither does the calling card replace any of the processes that should be undertaken after an Inspector informs the Case Officer that s/he was unable to complete the site visit.

Access Required (ARSV)

15. An ARSV will be arranged where you need to go onto the appeal site.

16. The principles are as follows:

- The Procedural Guide states that “If the appellant’s or agent’s presence is required at the appeal site it will be required solely to provide access to the site” and that “The local planning authority will not attend the site visit”.

<!-- Part1 p.124 -->

- The appellant will be told the day of the site visit and whether the Inspector or his/her representative will call in the morning or in the afternoon with the relevant 2 hour window slot. Wherever possible, you should arrange your visits so that you arrive in the first 30 minutes of the time slot.

- The appellant will be asked in advance to make arrangements for you to access the site. They may be present themselves; they may arrange for someone else to be present to allow access or they may provide a written agreement that you can go on the site (preferably beforehand in writing via the Case Officer or, exceptionally, by leaving a note pinned to the door).

- If the site is not land associated with a building or property it will be sensible to establish where the appellant or their representative will wait for you.

- In areas with limited parking facilities it may also be necessary to check in advance whether you can also park on the appellant’s land.

- When you arrive at the site you should always ring the doorbell2/knock on the door even if you think it would be possible to do the visit unaccompanied. This is because the appellant will be expecting you and may have waited in. Similarly, if the appellant is waiting somewhere in order to allow you access to the site.

- You should make it clear that the appellant’s attendance is only required to allow you to access the site. Politely discourage any attempt to engage in discussion about the appeal. However, you may wish to indicate that you have read all the representations made and are familiar with the case.

- If it has been arranged that you will also view from a neighbouring site – explain to the appellant that this will take place without them being present, that it is merely to allow you to view the relationship between the two sites and that there will be no opportunity for the neighbour to engage you in conversation or discussion.

- The appellant can be asked to wait inside while you carry out the site visit. However, to prevent accusations of theft, damage or interference with personal possessions, it is best to be accompanied by the appellant/their representative if you intend to enter any rooms inside the property.

17. On completion of the visit thank the appellant/their representative for providing access. If asked how long it will be until the decision is issued do not give a

timescale. If necessary, refer to the average time taken after the event or that it will be forthcoming as soon as possible.

18. If the appellant or their representative is not present:

- Has the appellant confirmed in writing that you can go on the site - either by leaving a note on site or, preferably, via the Case Officer?

- Are there are safeguarding concerns such as unattended minors on site? If yes, you should rearrange the visit for when an adult is present.

- If not, could you carry out the site visit unaccompanied without going on the appeal site? If so, you must inform the Case Officer.

- If you need to go on site, are you able to contact the appellant via the Case Officer (if you have time without delaying your programme)?

- If the appellant is not present and you need to go on the appeal site but you have no clear permission to do so - you will need to abandon the site visit and it will need to be rearranged. You should not go on to private land without express permission to do so. You should inform the Case Officer by email explaining the circumstances in order that they can confirm to the parties what happened.

- Where the site visit is abandoned and arrangements have been made to view the appeal site from a neighbouring property – you should visit the interested party and explain that the ARSV has been abandoned, and why, and that they will be advised of the new arrangements

- Alternatively, if you carry out the site visit unaccompanied and view the appeal site from the agreed neighbouring property you must inform the Case Officer.

- You should use the Calling Card if there is no answer

19. Viewing the appeal site from a neighbouring property

- The questionnaire asks the LPA if the Inspector will need to enter a neighbour’s land or property and asks for details of the address of that land or property. It is your decision as to whether it is necessary to do so. The question to ask yourself is whether it will help in reaching my decision and whether it would be prudent to do so given the cases made by either the LPA or the neighbour. In particular, if the LPA has requested a visit, if there are representations from the affected party and if the reasons for refusal concern the direct impact on that property, then it is advisable to view from the neighbouring property.

- Although usually picked up by Case Officers, it is also best practice to check the appeal questionnaire to see if the LPA has made such a request and that it has been actioned.

<!-- Part1 p.126 -->

- If you do need to visit a neighbouring property, it will be undertaken as ARSV and the Case Officer will notify the neighbour of the date and time (am or pm) of your visit.

- If an interested party has been asked to provide access you must ring the doorbell3/knock on their door even if you think it would be possible to do the visit unaccompanied. This is because the neighbour will be expecting you and may have waited in.

20. When you visit:

- briefly explain the purpose of your visit.

- politely discourage any attempt to engage you in conversation or discussion about the appeal.

- you then can ask the neighbour to wait inside while you carry out the site visit. However, to prevent accusations of theft, damage or interference with personal possessions, it is best to be accompanied if you intend to enter any rooms inside the neighbour’s property

21. If the neighbour is not present at the notified time:

- Complete your inspection from the appeal site and public land, along with any other visits arranged for the appeal.

- If you have enough information to make your decision inform the Case Officer.

- If you consider that it is essential to visit the neighbouring site provide the Case Officer with a clear explanation as to why so that they can to write to the parties to inform them the site visit will be re-arranged. If you have seen everything else at the site visit then you need only rearrange the visit to the neighbouring property.

22. If you consider it is essential to view from a neighbouring site in order to arrive at a sound decision and this has not been arranged beforehand you should inform your Case Officer and explain the reasons for requiring the site visit. However, before pursuing this option you should very carefully consider whether it really is necessary that you view from the neighbouring site. You should only do this if it is essential to allow you to make your decision. If you are satisfied that you can properly judge the effect of the proposal on neighbours from within the appeal site then there is no need to visit their properties (see Hallinan v SSE and Barnet LBC [1993] JPL 584).
