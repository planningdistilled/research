 •    The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
      against a refusal to grant planning permission.
 •    The appeal is made by Mrs Andrea Egas-Kitchener against the decision of Cornwall Council.
 •    The application Ref is PA25/02674.
 •    The development proposed is for the change of use and conversion of piggery to form a holiday
      cottage.

Decision
1. The appeal is dismissed.
Preliminary Matters
2. A revised National Planning Policy Framework (the Framework) was published on
   17 August 2026 and applies to decision-making from that date. It post-dates the
   parties' representations, and I have had regard to it in the determination of this
   appeal. I am satisfied that no party has been prejudiced by its publication, as the
   determinative matters in this appeal were already in issue between the parties.
3. Although described as a holiday cottage and capable of control by an occupancy
   condition, the unit would be self-contained with the physical characteristics of a
   dwelling, and would not lose those characteristics through holiday, part-year or
   successive occupation. Whether the proposal is properly a dwelling for the
   purposes of Policy 7 of the Cornwall Local Plan Strategic Policies 2010-2030
   (CLP), or tourist accommodation properly assessed instead under Policy 5, raises a
   genuine question of characterisation. However, both main parties have addressed
   the appeal on the basis that Policy 7 is engaged. I also note that Framework policy
   S5(1)(c) applies substantively the same construction-quality test to the re-use of
   any existing building outside a settlement regardless of that characterisation. I have
   therefore considered the proposal against both Policy 7 and Policy 5, reflecting that
   the application was made, and permission is sought, for a tourism use.
Main Issues
4. The main issues are whether:
     (i) the site is an appropriate location for the proposed accommodation, having
         regard to accessibility, reliance on the private car, and locational necessity;

Reasons
Location of development
5. The appeal site comprises a single-storey former piggery of breeze block
   construction, located in open countryside around 400 metres from Trewarmett and
   over a kilometre from Tintagel. Although there are scattered dwellings in the wider
   area, it forms no part of any group or cluster of buildings and is separated by open
   land from Trewarmett and other built development. Trewarmett is a hamlet and is
   not defined as a settlement in the development plan or the Framework. The site
   therefore lies outside of a defined settlement.
6. Criterion 3 of Policy 5 supports tourism accommodation of a scale appropriate to its
   location and accessibility by a range of transport modes, and requires a well-
   balanced mix of economic, social and environmental benefits. Consistent with the
   case officer's assessment, I agree that, given its modest scale, the proposal would
   be of an appropriate scale to its location and accessibility.
7. Nonetheless, as observed on my site inspection the lanes around the site are
   narrow, hedge-enclosed, unlit, and lack footways. This is consistent with the
   previous Inspector's finding, in the 2020 appeal1 for a dwelling on this site, of little
   substantive evidence that local footpaths would provide suitable access to local
   services and facilities. Walking for day-to-day purposes is largely unrealistic, and
   cycling is unattractive and unsafe in poor weather or after dark. The nearest bus
   stops, on the B3263, are served by the 95, which runs every two hours between
   0735 and 1845, Monday to Saturday only. Given this frequency, I am not satisfied it
   would provide a realistic alternative to the car for all journeys made by occupiers.
8. The holiday let use would not reduce this reliance, since visitors would be at least
   as car-dependent as residents, given they are likely to travel to attractions beyond
   the site. This reliance on the car would not minimise travel need or support the
   modal hierarchy in Policies C1 and T1 of the Climate Emergency Development
   Plan Document 2023 (CEDPD) and would generate environmental harm that is not
   offset by the modest economic benefits. Based on the evidence before me, the
   development would not achieve the well-balanced mix of economic, social and
   environmental benefits required by criterion 3 of Policy 5.
9. The Council's Statement also raised the point that criterion 1(c) of Policy 5 requires
   an overriding locational and business need for tourism development to be in the
   countryside. That concept is reflected in Framework policy S5(1)(b), which supports
   rural tourism development outside settlements only where such a location is shown
   to be necessary. The appellant's case addresses the economic contribution the
   proposal would make to the tourism economy generally, but does not identify why
   this location, rather than a site within or better related to a settlement, is necessary
   for the tourism use proposed.

10. The appellant also relies on the Framework's provision that development should be
    refused on transport grounds only where there would be an unacceptable impact
    on highway safety or a severe adverse impact on the road network, now reflected
    in Policy TR6(4). That is not the relevant test in this case because TR6(4)
    addresses matters of capacity and safety, and neither issue is in dispute here. The
    concern here is the car-dependent travel pattern the development would generate,
    as explained above. Moreover, Policies TR3(1)(e) and E4(2) of the Framework both
    expect proposals to take opportunities to improve rural connectivity or to use
    previously developed or well-related sites where such opportunities exist, and the
    appeal proposal has not been shown to do so given the site is relatively
    inaccessible by sustainable modes of travel.
11. In conclusion, for the reasons stated it has not been demonstrated that the
    proposal would be in an acceptable location for a holiday cottage. The proposal,
    therefore, conflicts with Policies 2 and 5 of the CLP, and Policies C1 and T1 of the
    CEDPD. It also does not fall within the exception provided by Framework policy
    S5(1)(b), and conflicts with policies TR3 and E4(2) of the Framework.
Re-use of the building
12. As found above, the site's location outside of a defined settlement engages
    Framework policy S5. If the proposal falls to be assessed as a dwelling, it would,
    for the reasons given above, also be an isolated home for the purposes of
    Framework policy S5(3), such that Framework policy HO11 would be the applicable
    test. However, it is unnecessary for me to resolve whether that characterisation is
    correct, because any re-use of the existing building independently engages policy
    S5(1)(c), which requires the building to be of permanent and substantial
    construction whether or not HO11 applies.
13. Both parties have, as explained in Preliminary Matters, proceeded on the basis that
    Policy 7 is engaged. That policy permits new homes in the open countryside only in
    special circumstances, restricted to five categories. The only one that could apply
    here is criterion 3, which concerns the re-use of suitably constructed, redundant,
    disused or historic buildings that are considered appropriate to retain and would
    lead to an enhancement of the immediate setting, the building having an existing
    lawful use and being at least ten years old. I am satisfied that there is no dispute
    the age and lawful use conditions of Policy 7 are met.
14. When considering the requirements of Policy S5 of the Framework and Policy 7 of
    the CLP together, the dispute is whether the building is suitably constructed and
    appropriate to retain. In this regard, I acknowledge that the appellant contends that
    the works would be repair and adaptation rather than reconstruction. The appellant
    also refers to a report confirming sound load-bearing walls and only limited repair.
15. However, no such report has been submitted to the appeal, and the 2019 survey
    appended to the Council’s statement does not support the appellant’s position in
    these regards. That survey envisaged a new steel frame on new foundations to
    carry the roof and wall loads, together with a new ridge beam and roof structure, so
    the completed building would derive its structural integrity from new work rather
    than from the existing fabric. The building is now seven years older and there is
    nothing to suggest structural improvement, and I am unable to conclude on the
    evidence before me that it is suitably constructed for conversion.

16. Nor am I persuaded that retaining the building as a phase of post-war agricultural
    modernisation weighs significantly in the proposal's favour. That characteristic
    speaks to the building's historical interest, not to whether it is suitably constructed
    for conversion, and it does not overcome the absence of evidence on that point
    addressed above. Had the structural evidence supported conversion, the building's
    utilitarian origins would not, of themselves, have made it inappropriate to retain for
    the purposes of Policy 7.
17. I have also considered the permission at Land North West of Trewinnick Farm and
    the appeal decisions cited. They confirm that partial replacement of structural
    elements is not necessarily rebuilding, and that the judgement is one of degree.
    However, each turned on its own evidence, and the Trewinnick scheme was
    supported by a structural survey finding the building sound and capable of
    conversion. No such evidence is before me, and those decisions do not, therefore,
    provide any justification for the proposal in this case.
18. Policy 21 of the CLP also does not assist the appellant. The appellant's case relies
    in part on the embodied-carbon benefit of reusing the existing structure rather than
    demolishing and rebuilding. However, no such benefit has been objectively
    quantified, and the information before me indicates that substantial new works,
    such as a new steel frame, new foundations and an entirely new roof structure,
    would in any event replace, rather than preserve, the greater part of the building's
    embodied carbon. Agricultural buildings are also excluded from Policy 21's
    definition of previously developed land, the site is not sustainably located for the
    reasons above, and no intensified-agricultural-use benefit has been demonstrated.
19. In conclusion, I am not satisfied the evidence adequately demonstrates that the
    building is capable of conversion without, in substance, amounting to a new
    building in the countryside, which is a form of development not supported by the
    development plan, nor falling within the provisions for proposals outside
    settlements as set out in Framework policy S5(1)(c).
Biodiversity Net Gain
20. The Biodiversity Net Gain exemption applies only below defined habitat thresholds,
    which it is for the appellant to demonstrate are met. It is not clear how the appellant
    arrived at the figures relied on, since the exemption statement omits the grey water
    system, foul treatment plant, charging point and associated excavation, and gives a
    site area of 123.20 square metres, whereas the Council's Statement records that
    the area within the red line boundary, when measured, totals over 1,000 square
    metres. Without a habitat plan, survey or metric establishing the baseline against
    the correct site parameters, I cannot be satisfied that the exemption applies.
21. Nor is there any metric or indicative habitat plan showing the mandatory
    biodiversity gain objective could be met if the exemption does not apply. The
    appellant has relied solely on the claimed exemption, and the Council has not
    proposed a condition capable of resolving that deficiency.
22. In the absence of sufficient evidence to demonstrate otherwise, I am not satisfied
    that the exemption applies, nor that the biodiversity gain condition deemed imposed
    by paragraph 13 of Schedule 7A to the 1990 Act would secure the mandatory
    biodiversity gain objective without any evidential basis for it. The proposal therefore
    conflicts with Policy G2 of the CEDPD and Framework policy N2, as well as the
    mandatory biodiversity gain requirements under Schedule 7A to the 1990 Act.

Other Matters
23. The site lies within the Cornwall National Landscape and Pentire Point to
    Widemouth Heritage Coast. The 2020 appeal Inspector found that residential
    conversion would not be incongruous and would improve the building's
    appearance, and the Council reached the same view. Given the retention of the
    existing footprint, scale and form, I also reach the same conclusion and find the
    proposal accords with Policy 23 of the CLP and saved policy ENV1 of the North
    Cornwall Local Plan.
24. No objection arises in relation to neighbour amenity, highway safety, drainage,
    contaminated land or sustainable construction, and I have no reason to reach a
    different view.
Planning Balance and Conclusion
25. Section 38(6) of the Planning and Compulsory Purchase Act 2004 requires the
    appeal be determined in accordance with the development plan unless material
    considerations indicate otherwise. As set out, the proposal conflicts with Policies 2,
    5 and 7 of the CLP and Policies C1, G2 and T1 of the CEDPD. Although no conflict
    arises with Policy 23, the development plan, read as a whole, is not complied with.
26. A single holiday unit would make a modest economic contribution to an established
    tourism area, but no evidence of unmet need for this type of development has been
    provided, so I give it limited weight. I give any embodied-carbon benefit no weight,
    since the evidence indicates the works would not, in substance, preserve the
    building's existing fabric.
27. Against these benefits, the absence of any realistic alternative to the car means the
    development would conflict with the development plan, and the site's location has
    not been shown to be necessary for the tourism use proposed. I give this
    considerable weight, not solely because visitors would travel by car, but because
    the proposal would introduce permanent accommodation in a location where car
    reliance is unavoidable and no need for the development has been demonstrated
    to offset that harm. Independently of that finding, the proposal has also not been
    shown to be suitably constructed for conversion and would in substance amount to
    new residential-type development. The unresolved conflict with the mandatory
    biodiversity gain requirements adds further weight against the proposal.
28. As the proposal does not fall within any of the categories in Framework policy
    S5(1), under S5(4) the proposal should be approved only where exceptional
    circumstances exist and the benefits would substantially outweigh the adverse
    effects, including to the character of the countryside and sustainable patterns of
    movement. No such circumstances exist and the limited benefits fall short of that
    test. Accordingly, there are no material considerations indicating a decision other
    than in accordance with the development plan.
29. For these reasons, and having regard to all other matters raised, the appeal is
    dismissed.
