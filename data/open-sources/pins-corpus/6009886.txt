•    The appeal is made by Matt Harding against the decision of the Council of the London Borough of

Decision
1. The appeal is dismissed.
Applications for costs
2. An application for costs was made by Matt Harding against the Council of the
   London Borough of Croydon. This application is the subject of a separate decision.
Preliminary Matters
3.   The appeal is accompanied by a Unilateral Undertaking (UU) made pursuant to section
     106 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended), section 16 of the
     Greater London Council (General Powers) Act 1974, section 111 of the Local
     Government Act 1972, and section 1 of the Localism Act 2011. The UU secures a
     sustainable transport contribution of £10,500, waste management obligations, and a
     monitoring fee of £1,500. I return to these matters later in this decision.
4.   On 17 August 2026, a revised National Planning Policy Framework (the Framework)
     was published. Annex A makes clear that the policies in the Framework are material
     considerations which must be taken into account in decision-making from the date of
     publication. The main parties were given the opportunity to comment on the revised
     Framework. I have taken any comments received into account in reaching my decision.
5.   For clarity, references to the Framework in this decision are to the August 2026
     version.

Reasons
Character and appearance
7. The appeal site comprises a vacant, broadly rectangular parcel of land within an
   established residential area. It was formerly occupied by a substantial detached
   dwelling set within a spacious plot that included an extensive rear garden. The
   surrounding area is characterised by a varied pattern of residential development,
   incorporating a mix of dwelling types, plot sizes and building scales that reflect the
   area's incremental change over time.
8. While the predominant pattern of development is of buildings fronting Harewood
   Road, backland development is a feature of the locality. Examples are evident in
   the vicinity of the appeal site and form part of the area's existing character.
9. Harewood Road exhibits a strong and readily discernible building line, with
   properties generally set back from the highway behind front gardens. These front
   gardens, together with the mature street trees that line the road, contribute
   positively to the spacious, verdant and attractive appearance of the street scene.
10. Backland development within the area is generally screened from public view by
    existing buildings, boundary treatments and vegetation. As a result, it has limited
    visibility from publicly accessible viewpoints and makes only a modest contribution
    to the appearance of the surrounding townscape. The visual experience of the area
    is therefore derived principally from the frontage development, associated
    landscaping and mature street trees along Harewood Road.
11. The surrounding area encompasses a range of architectural styles and external
    materials, contributing to a visually varied townscape. The area's appearance is
    influenced more by its consistent spatial qualities, including building setbacks,
    generous garden areas and mature landscaping, than by any singular architectural
    style or palette of materials.
12. The appeal proposal comprises a 3-storey flatted block located towards the front of
    the site, set back from the highway behind a front garden area and broadly aligned
    with the established building line of adjoining development. The bock of flats would
    be constructed beneath a pitched roof and would include a broadly L-shaped 2-
    storey projection to the rear. The rear portion of the site would accommodate 2
    detached dwellings of 2-storey height, each designed with a pyramidal roof form.
13. The 2-storey projection would extend beyond the rear building line of the adjoining
    Ian Austin Mansions, projecting further into the site whilst remaining set in from the
    common boundary. The arrangement would create an internal courtyard-like area.
    In considering the relationship between the buildings, it is relevant that Ian Austin
    Mansions is deep in plan form and characterised by a staggered rear building line
    rather than a single continuous rear elevation.

14. Notwithstanding the depth of the proposed projection, there would be a sufficient
    separation from the shared boundary and adequate space within the resulting
    courtyard area to ensure that the development would not appear unduly cramped
    or overdeveloped. While such an arrangement is not typical of the prevailing
    character of the area, given the limited views into the interior of the site, such a
    departure would neither detract from nor harm the area’s character.
15. The proposed standalone dwellings situated to the rear of the site would only be
    visible from limited points from the public highway, and given their distance from
    the public highway, and the proposed intervening landscaping, their visual
    prominence would be significantly reduced in public views.
16. The area between the flatted block and the detached dwellings would
    accommodate parking provision. Whilst this would represent a relatively urban
    feature within the site, its visual impact would be moderated through landscaping,
    boundary treatments and the use of appropriate surfacing materials. These
    measures would assist in integrating the parking area into its surroundings and
    reducing its prominence in wider views.
17. Accordingly, the proposal, having particular regard to the scale, form and layout of
    the L-shaped rear wing, together with the siting of the detached dwellings to the
    rear of the site, would not constitute overdevelopment and would preserve the
    character and appearance of the area.
18. The proposal would be in accordance with policy DM10 of the Croydon Local Plan
    2021 (the Local Plan) and policies D3 and D4 of the London Plan 2021 (the London
    Plan), which in so far as is relevant support high-quality design and the optimisation
    of site capacity in a way that responds to a site’s context and capacity for growth.
19. The policies of the Local Plan and the London Plan are consistent with the policies
    of the Framework insofar as they are relevant to character and appearance.
Whether the proposal would provide a safe, convenient and inclusive environment
20. The proposal includes a shared-surface arrangement throughout the development.
    Although a pedestrian path is shown along the southern elevation of the flatted
    block and is shown as being finished in contrasting surface materials, the swept-
    path analysis within the submitted Transport Note (December 2025) indicates that
    vehicles, including delivery vans, would overrun this area when travelling through
    the site. As a result, the route would not provide a clearly segregated pedestrian
    facility and its effectiveness in offering a convenient, attractive and safe
    environment for pedestrians would be diminished.
21. Furthermore, the principal entrances to the proposed flats would be located along
    the southern elevation of the building. Consequently, residents entering and leaving
    the flats would be required to interact directly with vehicles travelling through the
    site, giving rise to pedestrian and vehicular conflict. This concern is heightened
    given the size of the proposed flats, which would be capable of accommodating
    families, including those with young children. In addition, the site's areas of open
    space are likely to attract regular use by children living within the development,
    increasing pedestrian movement throughout the site. In the absence of dedicated
    pedestrian refuge areas or other meaningful segregation measures, the potential
    for conflict between vehicles and pedestrians would be increased.

22. The absence of suitable pedestrian segregation throughout the site would also give
    rise to particular concerns in respect of inclusive design. Any future residents with
    visual impairments, restricted mobility or other disabilities could experience difficulty
    with the shared-surface environment, particularly given the lack of segregated
    pedestrian routes and pedestrian refuge areas. The reliance upon a shared-surface
    arrangement, in circumstances where vehicles would overrun areas intended for
    pedestrian movement, would increase the potential for conflict between different
    users.
23. I have considered whether this matter could be addressed through the imposition of
    a planning condition requiring the submission and approval of a revised internal
    layout. However, the evidence before me does not demonstrate that amendments
    to the layout could be secured without giving rise to consequential effects on other
    aspects of the development, for example off-site queuing or stacking impacts, a
    concern given the limited space on site. In the absence of sufficient evidence
    demonstrating that such matters would not arise, or indeed could be satisfactorily
    resolved, I cannot be confident that the harm identified could be overcome through
    the use of conditions. Consequently, it would neither be reasonable nor appropriate
    to impose a condition requiring the submission of a revised layout.
24. I have had regard to the guidance contained in the Manual for Streets 2007 (MfS)
    in my assessment. It is important to note that the MfS constitutes technical
    guidance and does not set out any new policy or legal requirements. Nevertheless,
    my conclusions are consistent with the advice contained within that document. In
    particular, paragraph 7.2.10 of MfS recognises that shared-surface environments
    can present difficulties for blind or partially sighted people and others with
    disabilities.
25. I have had regard to the appeal decisions and planning permissions identified by
    the appellant in which shared-surface arrangements were found to be acceptable.
    However, I have no evidence before me to indicate that those schemes are directly
    comparable to the proposal before me. Moreover, my findings should not be taken
    as suggesting that shared-surface environments are unacceptable in principle.
    Rather, my concerns relate to the specific layout, design and characteristics of the
    development before me.
26. In terms of site access and egress, the site would be accessed via an existing
    access point. The information before me indicates that, in order to achieve the
    required visibility splays, control over third-party land would be necessary. The
    appellant has stated that an agreement is in place with the adjacent landowner to
    prevent the erection of structures exceeding 600mm in height within the land
    required for the splays. The appellant has also indicated that it intends to purchase
    that land. However, I have not been provided with a copy of any such agreement,
    and there is no substantive evidence before me demonstrating either that the
    visibility splays could be secured on third-party land or that they would be
    maintained for the lifetime of the development.
27. I have had regard to the submitted Transport Note, which states that there is no
    record of accidents associated with the existing access and that it has operated
    safely for many years. The evidence before me also identifies existing visibility
    constraints on-site arising from the existing street tree at the site frontage, to the
    north of the access point. I further note the Council's position that, despite the
    uncertainties regarding the provision and maintenance of visibility splays, the

     circumstances would not result in a demonstrable highway safety impact requiring
     mitigation.
28. However, the absence of recorded accidents and the historic operation of the
    access are not, in themselves, determinative. The implications of the proposed
    development must be assessed having regard to the nature and intensity of the
    proposed use. In this regard, the Transport Note indicates that visibility splays of
    1.5 metres by 1.5 metres, free from obstructions above 600mm in height, could be
    achieved.
29. I am not satisfied, for the reasons given above, that such splays could be reliably
    provided and maintained. This is a matter to which I attach significant weight given
    the evidence before me that the proposal would generate approximately 34 two-
    way vehicle movements on a typical weekday, resulting in a noticeable
    intensification in the use of the access. In the absence of certainty that adequate
    visibility splays could be secured and maintained, the proposal would give rise to
    an unacceptable risk to highway safety.
30. I have considered whether a planning condition could require the visibility splays to
    be provided before the development is brought into use and thereafter retained.
    However, given the absence of evidence that the appellant has the necessary
    control over the land required to achieve the splays, I am not persuaded that
    compliance with such a condition could be reasonably secured.
31. Accordingly, the proposal would result in a layout, access and circulation
    arrangement that would fail to provide a safe, convenient and inclusive environment
    for future users of the development and users of Harewood Road, and would have
    an unacceptable impact on highway safety.
32. The proposal would be contrary to policies SP8, DM10, DM29 and DM30 of the
    Local Plan and policy T4 of the London Plan, which in so far as is relevant require
    development to be safe and inclusive.
33. The policies of the Local Plan and the London Plan are consistent with the
    Framework insofar as they relate to highway safety and seek to ensure that
    development is safe, inclusive and accessible, whilst prioritising walking, wheeling
    and cycling.
Sustainable Transport and Waste Management
34. A completed UU has been submitted by the appellant as part of the appeal
    submission. It secures a sustainable transport contribution of £10,500, waste
    management obligations and a monitoring fee of £1,500.
35. The Council has confirmed that a UU could overcome the third reason for refusal.
    The Council has not undertaken a detailed review of the submitted obligation.
    However, it has raised concerns regarding the waste management provisions and
    whether the UU would be effective, given that part of the site is not within the
    appellant's ownership.
36. In respect of the waste management matter, the Council has suggested that its
    concerns could be addressed by means of a planning condition. Having considered
    the suggested condition, I am satisfied that, were I minded to allow the appeal, it
    would meet the relevant tests for the imposition of planning conditions.

Other Matters
38. I have had regard to the representations concerning the design of the proposed
    development, its effect on the living conditions of the occupiers of neighbouring
    properties, and parking concerns. However, the Council has not raised any
    concerns in respect of these matters, and based on the evidence before me,
    including my observations during the site visit, I have no reason to reach a different
    conclusion.
39. I have also considered the representations relating to flood risk and the potential
    effects of construction activities on the living conditions of the occupiers of
    neighbouring properties. If I were minded to allow the appeal these matters could
    be satisfactorily addressed through the imposition of planning conditions.
Planning Balance and conclusion
40. Policy S3 of the Framework states that decisions on development proposals should
    apply the presumption in favour of sustainable development. In relation to the
    appeal before me this means policy S4 of the Framework should be applied.
41. Policy S4 states that development proposals within settlements should be approved
    unless the benefits of doing so would be substantially outweighed by any adverse
    effects, when assessed against the national decision-making policies in the
    Framework. The policy further states that, in applying policy S4, the circumstances
    in which the benefits of approving development are likely to be substantially
    outweighed by adverse effects include (but are not restricted to) situations where
    the development proposal would, amongst other things, fail to comply with one of
    the national decision-making policies which state that development proposals
    should be refused in specific circumstances.
42. In this case, I have concluded that the development would have an unacceptable
    impact on highway safety. Given that Policy TR6 of the Framework directs that
    development should be refused where it would have such an impact, this matter
    attracts substantial weight against the proposal and leads me to conclude that the
    benefits of the development would be substantially outweighed by its adverse
    effects. Consequently, the proposal does not benefit from the presumption in favour
    of sustainable development set out in Policy S3 and applied through Policy S4.
43. The proposal would secure a net gain of 6 dwellings, many of which would be of a
    size capable of accommodating families, on a site that is well connected to services
    and facilities by a genuine choice of transport modes. In the context of the identified
    need for housing, I afford these benefits substantial weight. In addition, there would
    be economic benefits arising from both the construction and occupation of the
    development, to which I afford moderate weight. The provision of accessible homes
    would be a further benefit of moderate weight. Moreover, biodiversity
    enhancements could be secured through the imposition of a planning condition,
    also attracting moderate weight.
44. The proposal would be of an acceptable design and would not materially harm the
    living conditions of neighbouring occupiers; these are neutral considerations.

45. Weighed against these benefits is the failure of the proposal to secure a layout,
    access and circulation arrangement that would provide a safe, convenient and
    inclusive environment for future users of the development and users of Harewood
    Road, together with the unacceptable impact the proposal would have on highway
    safety. I attach substantial weight to these harms.
46. Overall, drawing these matters together, I find that the proposal would not accord
    with the development plan taken as a whole and there are no material
    considerations, including the Framework, which indicate that a decision should be
    taken other than in accordance with the development plan.
Conclusion
47. The appeal is dismissed.
