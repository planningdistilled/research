•   The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended) (the
    Act) against a refusal to grant planning permission.
•   The appeal is made by Altus Homes and Morro Partnerships against the decision of Warwick District
    Council.
•   The application Ref is W/24/0706.
•   The development proposed is redevelopment of the site and erection of 28 residential dwellings plus
    site access, parking, landscaping and associated infrastructure.

Decision
1. The appeal is dismissed.
Preliminary Matters
2. A revised version of the National Planning Policy Framework (August 2026) (the
   Framework) was published three days before the Hearing. I am suitably content
   that the parties had sufficient opportunity to familiarise themselves with its
   provisions and any implications for the appeal scheme prior to relevant discussions
   taking place at the Hearing.
3. It has been brought to my attention that the emerging South Warwickshire Local
   Plan (the ESWLP) has reached the Regulation 19 consultation stage. Whilst the
   emerging plan provides an indication of the possible future direction of local
   planning policy, its content can be expected to considerably evolve prior to formal
   adoption. Accordingly, I afford only limited weight to the emerging policies of the
   ESWLP and shall determine the appeal on this basis.
4. A suite of revised plans and additional supporting documents were submitted
   shortly before the Council resolved to refuse planning permission on 19 September
   2025 but were not taken into account in the Council’s determination. Nevertheless,
   having considered the minor extent of the plan revisions and the nature and scope
   of relevant supporting documents, I am content that no party with a potential
   interest in the outcome of the appeal is prejudiced by me accepting the revised
   plans and additional documents for determination purposes.
5. Planning permission was originally refused for seven reasons. However, following
   the submission of the revised plans and supporting documents, together with
   additional evidence provided during the appeal process (including noise modelling
   data) and the progression of the planning obligation, the Council has indicated that
   it no longer wishes to defend its fourth, fifth, sixth, and seventh reasons for refusal.
   These relate to the noise environment for future occupiers, the provision of on-site

     Public Open Space, site drainage, and carbon reduction aims. Accordingly, for the
     purposes of this decision letter, l shall formulate the appeal’s main issues to reflect

6. The Council’s remaining objections include their considerations that the proposal
   would amount to inappropriate development in the Green Belt whilst bringing
   forward development in an unsustainable location served by limited facilities and
   services without safe cycle and pedestrian routes available. On the basis that the
   relevant inappropriate development exception – which I shall come on to introduce

   reference to a policy of the Framework that sets out various related principles, I
   shall consider these matters concurrently under the appeal’s first main issue as set
   out below. I am satisfied that no party with a potential interest is prejudiced by me
   taking this approach.
7. A planning obligation pursuant to Section 106 of the Act (the legal agreement) is
   before me. This is dated 9 September 2026 and has been executed by the

   sustainable drainage, reptile translocation, biodiversity net-gain, healthcare,
   education, libraries, road safety education, and maintenance of public rights of way.
   I shall refer to the legal agreement at points later in this Decision.
Main Issues
8. The main issues are:
       •   Whether or not the proposal represents inappropriate development in the
           Green Belt;
       •   Whether or not the number and concentration of affordable dwellings proposed
           at this location is acceptable;
       •   The effect of the proposal upon the character and appearance of the area,
           having regard to the intended layout, scale, and density of development; and
       •   Whether or not the harm by reason of inappropriateness, and any other harm,
           would be clearly outweighed by other considerations so as to amount to the
           very special circumstances necessary to justify it.
Reasons
Whether or not inappropriate development in the Green Belt
     Relevant provisions of the Framework
9. The site lies in the West Midlands Green Belt. Policy DS18 of the Warwick District
   Local Plan (September 2017) (the Local Plan) confirms that national planning policy
   shall be applied to proposals within the Green Belt. The Framework states that
   development in the Green Belt is inappropriate unless it falls within one of the
   categories set out in Policy GB7. Inappropriate development is, by definition,
   harmful to the Green Belt and should not be approved except in very special
   circumstances.

10. The provisions of Policy GB7 were explored at the Hearing. Discissions centred on
    Category 1(g), under which development meeting all four criteria (i-iv) may be
    regarded as not inappropriate. In relation to criterions (i) and (ii), it is common
    ground that the proposal would utilise ‘grey belt’ land, would not fundamentally
    undermine the purposes, taken together, of the remaining Green Belt across the
    plan area, and would respond to an evidenced unmet need for housing. I have no
    reason to take a different view on these matters.
11. Criterion (iii) of Category 1(g) requires the development to be in a sustainable
    location, with particular reference to Policy TR3 of the Framework. Policy TR3 sets
    out principles (a-e) that development proposals should reflect. These principles
    include identifying circumstances where development should be located
    sustainably and what that entails; ensuring opportunities are taken to utilise existing
    or proposed transport infrastructure; mitigating any significant adverse impacts on
    the transport network or highway safety; assessing environmental impacts; and, in
    rural areas, taking opportunities to improve walking, wheeling, cycling and public
    transport, and to enhance connectivity where such improvements exist and can be
    supported by the proposed development. I consider below whether the proposal
    complies with criterion (iii).
12. Criterion (iv) requires major development proposals to comply with Policy GB8,
    which, under Section 1, sets out the Golden Rules (a-c) for major housing
    development. It is common ground that suitable contributions would be made
    towards affordable housing and publicly accessible green space, thereby satisfying
    parts (a) and (c). Moreover, the legal agreement secures comprehensive
    provisions related to both affordable housing and Public Open Space, including
    separate ‘Allotment’ and ‘Parks and Gardens’ contributions should any on-site
    deficiencies arise with respect to these particular open space typologies.
13. For the avoidance of doubt, I am content that the Public Open Space to be
    provided would make a positive contribution to the landscape setting of the
    development and support nature recovery. Thus, with regard to parts (a) and (c) of
    the Golden Rules, I have no reason to take a different view to the parties.
    However, part (b) requires that necessary improvements be made to local or
    national infrastructure, a matter I return to below.
     Sustainable location (Policy GB7(1)(g)(iii))
14. The site is situated adjacent to the Infill Village Boundary for Hatton Station. This is
    a small settlement containing a very limited range of facilities and services,
    principally comprising a railway station, a yacht club, and a nursery. These
    facilities are all located to the north of the site and are subject to accessibility
    constraints, particularly in relation to the useability and safety of Station Road as
    the connecting route. I consider these matters in greater detail below in the context
    of the railway station before assessing the sustainability of the location in relation to
    the proposed development and the requirements of Policy TR3 of the Framework.
15. Other facilities and services are available in the wider area but are generally
    located such that walking would not be a realistic means of access. For example,
    the nearest bus stops are approximately 1.1 kilometres away along Station Road.
    These stops are served only twice a week by a single return service between
    Leamington Spa and Henley-in-Arden. The route to them is in-part a high-speed
    vehicular corridor that is unlit and lacks footways or dedicated cycling infrastructure.

16. My attention has also been drawn to a demand-responsive transport service
    operating within the Hatton and West Warwick area, designed specifically for rural
    communities with limited conventional bus provision. However, based on the
    evidence before me, there is no certainty that future occupiers would be able to rely
    upon this service conveniently and consistently to meet their day-to-day travel
    needs.
17. A village shop and public house are located at Shrewley, approximately 1.7 and 1.8
    kilometres from the site respectively. Although a canal towpath, whilst offering a
    lengthy connection, could theoretically be used as an alternative route to Station
    Road for walking or cycling purposes, it is neither surfaced consistently with a hard-
    bound material nor lit. My attention has also been drawn to facilities including a
    shopping village and swimming pool on Dark Lane, approximately 2.2 kilometres
    away. However, owing to the distances involved and the constraints affecting route
    quality and useability, these destinations are unlikely to be accessed on foot or by
    cycle. The nearest school, Ferncumbe Primary School, is approximately 2.3
    kilometres away. Although it would not realistically be accessible on foot, I
    understand that a school minibus currently picks up and drops off pupils on
    Oakdene Crescent.
18. Hatton railway station comprises three platforms and provides rail services to a
    variety of urban destinations including Warwick, Leamington Spa, Stratford-upon-
    Avon, Birmingham and London. In the context of a small rural settlement, this
    represents an important and meaningful form of public transport provision. Even
    so, the frequency of trains to individual destinations remains relatively limited. For
    context, the station does not meet the Framework’s definition of a ‘well-connected
    station’ because fewer than four trains per hour serve it throughout the daytime on
    a typical weekday and fewer than two trains per hour operate in any one direction.
19. Accessibility constraints also arise. To walk the approximately 350 metres between
    the site and the station, users must travel along Station Road, including the bridge
    crossing the railway line, where there are no meaningful dedicated facilities for
    either pedestrians or cyclists. Furthermore, two of the station’s three platforms do
    not benefit from step-free access.
20. The appellant has argued that Station Road is best characterised as a rural shared
    carriageway where pedestrians, cyclists and motor vehicles interact within a low
    traffic environment. Recent traffic surveys undertaken near the railway bridge
    during school termtime recorded 64 two-way vehicle movements during both the
    morning peak (0800-0900) and evening peak (1700-1800), within a total daily flow
    of 653 vehicles. Recorded average vehicle speeds ranged between 23 and
    26 mph, with 85th percentile speeds of around 30 mph. Consequently, and
    consistent with my site observations, it is fair to assert that Station Road
    experiences relatively low traffic volumes travelling at generally moderate speeds.
21. However, it cannot be overlooked that Station Road is principally designed to
    accommodate vehicular movement. The route is narrow and there are a range of
    physical constraints affecting useability, particularly for pedestrians, along the
    section between Oakdene Crescent and the railway station. Slimline grassed
    verges, frequently bounded by hedgerows and other vegetation, provide limited
    opportunities to step clear of the carriageway. Further, forward visibility is restricted
    in places by the road’s curved alignment and undulating topography, and the route
    is also unlit.

22. While recently installed kerbing adjacent to the railway bridge has assisted the
    creation of a narrow informal recessed area across the bridge itself, opportunities to
    leave the carriageway safely in positions where the kerbing is in place at either end
    of the bridge are now particularly constrained. Although I accept that drivers are
    likely to exercise a degree of caution when approaching the bridge, the potential for
    direct conflict between vehicles and pedestrians is readily apparent. Moreover,
    whilst the likelihood of two vehicles passing each other on the bridge
    simultaneously with a pedestrian present is limited, it must be remembered that
    useability constraints apply along almost the entirety of the route from Oakdene
    Crescent to the railway station.
23. While it is relevant that no recorded personal injury collisions have occurred within
    a study area extending approximately 500 metres from the appeal site since the
    introduction of a revised speed limit in 2014, that factor must be treated with
    caution in light of the clear physical constraints evident on the ground.
24. I acknowledge that both Manual for Streets and Manual for Streets 2 emphasise
    that highway design should respond to local character and function, rather than
    impose rigid standards irrespective of context, and that their principles may
    appropriately be applied in this case. I also accept that the absence of continuous
    footways does not automatically render Station Road unsuitable for pedestrian use.
    Nonetheless, having regard to the particular circumstances at play and the
    observed traffic flows and vehicle speeds, Station Road cannot reasonably be
    regarded as providing a safe route for all users, particularly pedestrians.
25. CCTV survey evidence has been submitted to demonstrate pedestrian and cyclist
    use of the section of Station Road containing the railway bridge. Between 0700
    and 1900 on a school termtime weekday, the survey recorded 70 pedestrian
    movements and 13 cyclist movements. Heatmap data generated from a widely
    used fitness tracking application has also been submitted in support of this position.
    During my own site visits, I likewise observed pedestrians and cyclists using the
    bridge.
26. It has been argued that, if a significant highway safety risk existed, pedestrian and
    cycle activity would be expected to be very limited. However, the extent of use
    must be viewed in the context that no alternative crossing of the railway line exists
    within the immediate area to facilitate movement between the northern and
    southern parts of Hatton Station. Consequently, it is likely that at least some
    journeys arise out of necessity rather than choice. In such circumstances, the
    recorded levels of activity do not demonstrate that the route is universally perceived
    as safe.
27. Turning to Policy TR3 of the Framework, it is necessary to make a judgement as to
    whether the proposal could generate a significant amount of movement in the
    context of the area within which it would be situated. Where it would, the policy
    indicates that development should be located in a sustainable location, or one
    capable of being made sustainable, taking account of planned improvements,
    including any to be provided for as part of the development itself.
28. An indicative forecast for vehicle trip generation has been prepared, with the
    proposed 28 dwellings expected to generate around 12 vehicle movements in the
    morning peak hour, nine in the evening peak hour, and around 112 movements per
    day overall. These are relatively modest forecast traffic volumes, and the Highway

     Authority has raised no objection on grounds of traffic generation or the capacity of
     the highway network to accommodate the additional vehicular traffic.
29. Nevertheless, major residential development is proposed at a small rural settlement
    where connectivity constraints are readily pinpointable. Thus, 28 additional
    households, with the potential to produce trips via a range of modes (albeit some of
    sustainable categorisation), could reasonably be expected to generate a significant
    amount of movement when assessed in the context of the host area. I have had
    regard to the appellant’s recent survey evidence in coming to this finding.
    Accordingly, the location should be sustainable, limiting the need to travel,
    particularly by private car, and providing a genuine choice of transport modes.
30. As set out above, Hatton Station contains only a very limited range of services and
    facilities and does not enjoy convenient access, by means other than private
    vehicle, to the majority of facilities available in the wider area. The principal
    exception is the nearest primary school, owing to the availability of a dedicated
    minibus service. In my view, the site’s location does not limit the need to travel.
31. Moreover, future residents would be highly unlikely to walk or cycle beyond Hatton
    Station on a routine basis in order to meet day-to-day needs. This is due both to
    the distances involved and to the constrained useability and, in places, unsafe
    nature of the routes available. In terms of public transport, the nearest bus stops
    are not realistically accessible on foot. Consequently, future residents would be
    heavily reliant upon rail services in order to travel beyond the settlement without
    use of a private vehicle.
32. As previously identified, the railway station provides meaningful public transport
    connectivity to a range of destinations, albeit through a relatively infrequent service
    pattern. However, notwithstanding the station’s proximity to the site, the connecting
    route is subject to significant useability and safety constraints, particularly for
    pedestrians. These shortcomings, when considered alongside accessibility
    limitations at the station itself, undermine any proposition that future residents
    would necessarily enjoy a genuine choice of transport modes.
33. The appellant has indicated a willingness to implement measures intended to
    reinforce the shared-use credentials of Station Road and thus improve its
    useability. Such measures include additional signage and carriageway markings to
    increase driver awareness near the railway bridge, together with coloured surface
    treatments to materials to the western side of the bridge to emphasise its
    suggested informal function as a pedestrian refuge. Also, a modest financial
    contribution towards local road safety initiatives is secured via the legal agreement.
34. However, these measures, whilst aimed at enhancing connectivity in a rural area,
    are relatively minor in nature and, in my judgement, would not substantively
    improve the suitability of Station Road for pedestrians or cyclists. Moreover,
    notwithstanding a suggested potential planning condition to secure a scheme of
    pedestrian connectivity improvements, uncertainty remains regarding the precise
    package of works that could ultimately be delivered, given that any highway
    interventions would require separate Section 278 agreement.
35. The appellant has also expressed a willingness to contribute towards a Traffic
    Regulation Order to introduce a 20 mph speed limit along Station Road. Such a
    measure could have the potential to enhance conditions for pedestrians and
    cyclists. However, there is no certainty that such an amendment would come to

       fruition if the appeal were allowed. Moreover, no legally binding mechanism
       secures its delivery, and any purported change of speed limit would remain subject
       to further discussions with the Highway Authority and approval in conjunction with
       the Section 278 process.
36. Accordingly, notwithstanding the intended formalisation of school minibus stopping
    and turning facilities on Oakdene Crescent and the contribution that is secured
    towards maintaining public rights of way, it has not been demonstrated that
    consequential improvements to sustainable travel opportunities or local connectivity
    would result from the development.
37. Whilst I recognise that a development proposal should only be expected to mitigate
    highway safety impacts arising directly from the scheme, there is clear potential for
    the proposal to exacerbate existing deficiencies within a local network
    characterised by substandard pedestrian infrastructure. Moreover, significant
    residual pedestrian safety concerns would be fairly anticipated to remain if the
    development were to proceed as proposed. This finding derives principally from
    the scale of development and the consequent increase in movements that would
    inevitably be generated.
38. I note here that, whilst successful appeal decisions1 elsewhere have been
    submitted in evidence, each relates to merely a single dwelling such that it is
    difficult to draw direct parallels with the scheme before me.
39. Accordingly, especially as the site’s location fails to limit the need to travel, the
    proposal would not adequately support sustainable patterns of movement and
    would fail to suitably reflect the principles of Policy TR3 when read and considered
    as a whole. The intended provision of electric vehicle charging points, secure cycle
    storage, and Sustainable Welcome Packs does not alter my conclusions in this
    regard. Consequently, the development would not be located in a sustainable
    location as required by Policy GB7(1)(g)(iii) of the Framework.
40. I also note identifiable conflict with Policy TR1 of the Local Plan in so far as it
    requires development proposals to provide safe, suitable and attractive access
    routes and to demonstrate no detriment to highway safety – requirements that are
    not materially inconsistent with the Framework.
       Necessary improvements to local or national infrastructure (Policy GB8(1)(b))
41. The proposal would make proportionate contributions towards a range of
    infrastructure requirements. Indeed, the legal agreement secures, amongst other
    matters, contributions towards education facilities, library services, Public Open
    Space, and the maintenance of public rights of way. However, my findings
    regarding the safety and useability of Station Road, including the identification of
    significant residual pedestrian safety concerns, call into question whether
    improvements to local highway infrastructure necessary to address the impacts
    arising from the proposed 28 dwellings have been adequately secured.
42. Emerging allocation B1/HAT within the ESWLP relates to a potential new
    settlement at Hatton, of which the appeal site forms a small part to its western
    extent. The allocation has emerged as one of two preferred options from an initial
    longlist of 12 potential locations originally consulted upon. It also falls within a

44. I accept that infrastructure of this scale extends beyond what could reasonably be
    required to mitigate the impacts of the appeal proposal alone. Nevertheless, in the
    particular circumstances of this case, necessary local highway infrastructure

    conclusion aligns with the position of the Highway Authority, which considers the
    mitigation measures proposed by the appellant to be inadequate. This is a matter
    of importance, as they are responsible for the safety of road users on the local
    highway network. Accordingly, notwithstanding the appellant’s willingness to fund
    certain improvements, it has not been satisfactorily demonstrated that necessary
    improvements to local infrastructure would be delivered.
     Conclusion on whether or not inappropriate development
45. I have found that the development would not be in a sustainable location and that
    the necessary improvements to local infrastructure required by the Golden Rules
    under Policy GB8 of the Framework would not be secured. Consequently, the
    proposal does not satisfy the exception for development that is not inappropriate in
    the Green Belt that is set out in Category 1(g) of Policy GB7 of the Framework.
46. As confirmed at the Hearing, it is common ground between the parties that no other
    exception within Section 1 of Policy GB7 is applicable. I have no reason to depart
    from that position. Accordingly, the proposal constitutes inappropriate development
    in the Green Belt. I shall consider the implications of this finding when assessing
    whether very special circumstances exist to justify the grant of planning permission.
Number and concentration of affordable dwellings
47. The proposal, whilst for 100% affordable housing, was not submitted on the basis
    of being a rural exception site. Moreover, as confirmed in the signed Statement of
    Common Ground, the scheme does not seek to meet a particular local housing
    need. It instead aims to contribute towards meeting unmet housing need as
    identifiable across the District as a whole. As such, as agreed by the parties at the
    Hearing, the provisions of Policy H3 (Affordable Housing on Rural Exception Sites)
    of the Local Plan are not relevant to the appeal proposal.
48. The Council’s concerns are centred upon the number and concentration of
    affordable units at this particular rural location, which has raised concerns from a
    community cohesion standpoint. However, no associated local planning policy
    conflict has been clearly pinpointed. It is also of relevance that the Framework
    encourages development proposals to either meet or exceed up-to-date
    development plan requirements for the proportion and mix of affordable housing

     tenures relevant to the location. In broad terms, this policy provision is geared
     towards promoting rather than restricting the delivery of affordable homes.
49. In this context, I find that no planning harm would materialise as a consequence of
    the number and concentration of affordable dwellings proposed at this location.
    Nevertheless, when I come on to apportion weight to the social benefits to
    materialise from the delivery of 28 affordable homes, it is relevant for me to factor in
    that the scheme has not been formulated to satisfy any particular local need.
Character and appearance
50. The appeal site is situated beyond the current defined limits of Hatton Station. It is
    clear of built development and well-vegetated, particularly across its eastern portion
    and along its boundaries where various established trees are in existence. Whilst
    the site possesses distinct rural qualities, it is not subject to any formal landscape
    designation and there are adjacent developed influences at play. These include a
    row of residential properties to the west, which back on to the site, and the railway
    line, which runs east-west in proximity to the site’s northern boundary. It is also
    pertinent that the site has a constrained visual envelope, with publicly accessible
    views principally confined to Oakdene Crescent locations.
51. In terms of the on-site layout that is proposed, I do not find that an unduly dense or
    cramped form of development would materialise. Indeed, various gaps of differing
    size and specification between dwellings are proposed and elements of soft
    landscaping would be interspersed with new built form and associated parking to
    assist in softening the appearance of the development. Moreover, the proposed
    layout and dwelling designs would acceptably respect the composition of existing
    neighbouring development on both Oakdene Crescent and Antrobus Close. It is
    also noteworthy that built development would be setback from the site’s eastern
    boundary so as to assist in limiting tree losses.
52. Even so, it is inevitable that a proposal for 28 dwellings rising to two storeys in
    height would have some form of adverse urbanising influence. This is most
    particularly owing to the site’s present verdant and undeveloped composition as
    part of the wider countryside that envelops Hatton Station. However, given the
    edge-of-settlement influences that are apparent as well as the discreet and well-
    enclosed nature of the site, merely limited harm would be caused to the character
    and appearance of the area. There is thus some minor degree of conflict with
    Policies BE1 and NE4 of the Local Plan in so far as these policies require
    development proposals to consider their landscape context and to harmonise with,
    or enhance, the existing settlement in terms of physical form and land use –
    requirements that are not materially inconsistent with the Framework.

Whether very special circumstances apply
55. I have identified that the proposal represents inappropriate development in the
    Green Belt. The Framework confirms that substantial weight should be given to
    harm caused to the Green Belt, including harm to its openness. I note that a
    distinct loss of openness, most especially in a spatial sense, would be associated
    with the development of 28 dwellings upon a presently undeveloped parcel of land
    that extends to approximately 1.17 hectares in area. In addition, limited harm
    would be caused to the character and appearance of the area.
56. In terms of the proposal’s benefits, 28 additional affordable housing units would
    make an effective use of land and be deliverable in the short-term in the context of
    a very significant housing land supply shortfall. Moreover, the latest published
    position is a 1.96 year supply, calculated using the standard method as the
    development plan housing requirement is more than five years old, which
    represents a 3,466 dwelling shortfall.
57. Further, these units would be delivered within a District that constitutes an
    expensive place to live in relative terms and where a clear under-delivery of
    affordable homes against calculated requirements is identifiable over the past five
    years. Further, the proposed housing mix and tenure (75% social rent and 25%
    shared ownership) align with the Council’s requirements and are considered
    acceptable. Thus, even though the new dwellings would not directly respond to
    any particular local affordable housing need, very significant weight is fairly
    attributable to the social benefits that would be brought about by the scheme in a
    housing delivery sense.
58. Some minor additional social benefits would be fairly anticipated as a consequence
    of future occupiers participating in local community activities and groups. Further,
    the proposal would generate economic benefits, including employment during the
    construction phase and increased local expenditure and tax revenues following
    occupation. I attribute considerable weight to these economic benefits given the
    major nature of the development proposal.
59. Furthermore, having regard to conditions that would apply and related monitoring
    provisions secured via the legal agreement, the scheme would deliver a biodiversity
    net-gain of at least 10%. This attracts moderate positive weight as an
    environmental benefit. In addition, whilst the proposed on-site Public Open Space
    would principally serve the needs of future residents, it would be accessible to the
    wider community. As secured through the legal agreement, such provision attracts
    minor positive weight as a social benefit.
60. The scheme’s benefits would, in cumulative terms, be substantial. However, such
    benefits would not clearly outweigh the substantial harm identified to the Green Belt
    (including harm derived from loss of openness), as well as limited harm in a
    character and appearance sense, so as to amount to the very special
    circumstances necessary to justify the development. Accordingly, conflict is
    identifiable with national decision-making policies pertaining to the Green Belt and
    with Policy DS18 of the Local Plan.

62. For the reasons given above, the appeal is dismissed.
