Appeal Ref: 6006986
26 Bendemeer Road, Wandsworth, London SW15 1JU
•    The appeal is made under Section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
     against a refusal to grant planning permission.
•    The appeal is made by Dr Shams against the decision of the Council of the London Borough of
     Wandsworth.
•    The application Ref is 2025/0902.
•    The development proposed is change of use from single dwelling to two self contained flats and
     enlarge existing roof terrace and associated screening.

Decision
1.    The appeal is dismissed.
Procedural Matter
2.    The Council refused the application on the basis that it would result in the loss of a
      family-sized dwelling. It further contended that, if it were accepted that no such
      loss would occur, the proposal would nevertheless conflict with relevant planning
      policies owing to the limited size of the proposed upper flat and the absence of a
      rear garden to serve that flat. The appellant addressed these additional matters
      within their appeal submissions, and there would therefore be no injustice in my
      treating them as an additional main issue when determining the appeal.
Main Issue
3.    The main issues are:
            •    the effect of the development on the availability of homes suitable for
                 families, having particular regard to relevant development plan policies;
                 and
            •    whether the proposal would provide satisfactory living conditions for future
                 occupiers, having regard to the adequacy of internal space and the
                 provision of external space available to the residents of the upper flat.
Reasons
Effect on existing family accommodation
4.    Part A of Policy LP26 of the Wandsworth Local Plan 2023 – 2038 (LP) precludes
      the conversion of any dwelling with 130 square metres or less of floorspace (as
      originally constructed) into flats unless it can be demonstrated that the existing
      dwelling is unsuitable for families. The supporting text to this policy recognises the

      importance of retaining family-sized accommodation in order to meet future
      housing need.
5.    The Council states that the floor plans submitted for two earlier planning
      applications indicate that the original floorspace of the dwelling was 110 square
      metres. The appellant disputes that figure, asserting that the architect who
      prepared those drawings relied on OS data and estate-agent particulars rather
      than a measured survey. They explain that a measured survey was undertaken as
      part of the preparation of the refused application, and that the floorspace of the
      originally constructed dwelling was calculated as 131 square metres. In response,
      the Council maintains that the plans accompanying the previous applications
      correspond with the Planning Officer’s own measurements taken on site and with
      the Council’s GIS mapping. It further argues that the appellant’s challenge to the
      accuracy of those plans potentially calls into question the validity of the two earlier
      planning approvals.
6.    The appellant has provided scaled plans derived from their measured survey to
      substantiate their position. These plans offer a clear basis for establishing the
      original floorspace of the dwelling. By contrast, the Council has not provided any
      further technical evidence beyond the references contained in the officer report
      and its appeal statement. While it relies on the Planning Officer’s site
      measurements and GIS mapping, no scaled plans or contemporaneous survey
      data have been submitted to demonstrate that the earlier application drawings
      accurately reflected the dwelling as originally constructed. In the absence of such
      material, the appellant’s measured survey carries greater evidential weight in
      determining the original size of the dwelling for the purposes of Policy LP26.
7.    For these reasons, the evidence demonstrates that the existing dwelling, as
      originally constructed, exceeded the threshold for a family-sized dwelling and that
      the proposal would not materially diminish the stock of such accommodation. In
      this context, and having regard to all submissions, I find that the development
      complies with Part A of Policy LP26 of the LP.
Living conditions
8.    As I have found that the original dwelling exceeded 130 square metres in
      floorspace, Part B of Policy LP26 applies. That part of the policy states that the
      conversion of a dwelling with more than 130 square metres of floorspace, as
      originally constructed, into flats will only be permitted where, amongst other
      criteria, at least one family-sized dwelling is provided with direct access to a
      dedicated rear garden of at least 15 square metres.
9.    The upper flat would meet the definition of family-sized accommodation and it
      would include a roof terrace on the second floor measuring 15 square metres. I
      acknowledge the appellant’s assertion that the terrace would provide adequate
      amenity space. However, a terrace located on the second floor of a building is
      materially different in character, function and accessibility from a garden situated
      at the rear of a property. The policy requirement is explicit in its reference to a
      “rear garden”, and the distinction is that a garden at ground level provides a type of
      private outdoor space that a roof terrace cannot replicate. On this basis, the
      family-sized accommodation provided by the upper flat would not benefit from a
      rear garden as required by Part B of Policy LP26.

10. Policy LP27 of the LP is also relevant, as it applies to all new residential
    development, including conversions. Amongst other matters, it requires proposals
    for new dwellings to comply with the Nationally Described Space Standard
    (NDSS). The upper flat would be arranged over two storeys and would provide
    three double bedrooms. Under the NDSS, a three-bedroom, two-storey dwelling is
    required to have a minimum floorspace of 102 square metres.
11. There is consensus between the main parties that the floorspace of the upper flat
    amounts to 79 square metres, and I am content to rely on that figure. The
    appellant contends that 79 square metres is sufficient and cannot reasonably be
    regarded as substandard. However, the shortfall relative to the NDSS requirement
    is substantial. A difference of 23 square metres represents a significant reduction
    in the amount of usable living space available to future occupants, and it would
    materially constrain the functionality of the dwelling. Despite the appellant’s
    assertion, the proposal would not provide an adequate level of internal
    accommodation for a three-bedroom property, and the overall area of usable
    floorspace would fall short of what is necessary to support comfortable and
    practical occupation.
12. The appellant also refers to the ground-floor flat as constituting a family-sized
    dwelling. That unit would contain two double bedrooms and would benefit from
    access to a large rear garden. However, it does not meet the definition of a
    family-sized dwelling as set out in the LP, which requires a minimum of three
    bedrooms. I therefore cannot regard it as such for the purposes of the
    development plan. The policy conflict arising from the inadequate size of the only
    proposed family sized dwelling and the absence of a compliant rear garden
    serving that unit is not remedied by the level of accommodation proposed at
    ground floor.
13. While I acknowledge that other London boroughs may adopt different definitions of
    family-sized housing, those approaches do not alter the requirements of the
    development plan for the London Borough of Wandsworth. The proposal must be
    assessed against the policies that apply here.
14. For these reasons, the proposal would not provide satisfactory living conditions for
    future occupiers, having regard to the adequacy of internal space and the
    provision of external space available to the residents of the upper flat.
    Consequently, it conflicts with Part B of Policy LP26 and Policy LP27 of the LP and
    Policy D6 of the London Plan (2021).
Planning Balance
15. The appellant contends that the appeal site occupies a sustainable location and
    that the development would make efficient use of the land by contributing to the
    Borough’s housing supply. I acknowledge these considerations. However, the
    efficient use of an existing residential plot to deliver a single additional dwelling in a
    sustainable location is an uplift in housing provision that amounts to a small-scale
    contribution to the Borough’s overall supply and, in this context, attracts only
    limited weight.
16. Set against these limited benefits is the inadequately sized upper flat and the
    absence of a rear garden to serve that dwelling, resulting in clear conflict with the
    development plan. Policies LP26 and LP27 are consistent with the National
    Planning Policy Framework’s requirement for a high standard of amenity for future

      users of development. The conflict with those policies, and the associated harm
      arising from the proposal, therefore carries significant weight in the overall
      planning balance. In this context, the benefits of the scheme are significantly and
      demonstrably outweighed by the unsatisfactory living conditions for future
      occupiers and the resulting policy conflict.
Conclusion
17. For the reasons given above, the proposal conflicts with the development plan and
    the material considerations do not indicate that the appeal should be decided other
    than in accordance with it. Accordingly, the appeal should be dismissed.
