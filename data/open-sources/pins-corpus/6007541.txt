Site visit made on 4 August 2026
by K Reeves BA (Hons) MA MRTPI
an Inspector appointed by the Secretary of State
Decision date: 09 September 2026


Appeal Ref: 6007541
7 Vicarage Mansions, Queenstown Road, Wandsworth, London SW8 3RZ
•    The appeal is made under Section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
     against a refusal to grant planning permission.
•    The appeal is made by Mr Reeve against the decision of the Council of the London Borough of
     Wandsworth.
•    The application Ref is 2025/3183.
•    The development proposed is alterations including erection of a mansard extension to roof to form
     another level of accommodation.

Decision
1.    The appeal is dismissed.
Main Issue
2.    The main issue is whether the proposal would preserve or enhance the character
      or appearance of the Park Town Estate Conservation Area (CA) and preserve the
      setting of the Grade II listed Church of St Philip.
Reasons
Policy context
3.    The appellant contends that the publication of the 2026 National Planning Policy
      Framework (Framework) materially alters the national approach to heritage and
      height and that, under Annex A, the development plan policies referenced on the
      Council’s decision notice, namely Policies LP1, LP3, LP4 and LP5 of the
      Wandsworth Local Plan 2023 – 2038 (LP), should now attract very limited weight.
4.    However, these policies remain broadly consistent with the Framework’s
      requirements to conserve designated heritage assets, to secure high-quality
      design and to manage building heights through a plan-led spatial strategy. There is
      no substantive basis to regard them as materially inconsistent with national policy,
      and they therefore attract full weight in determining the appeal.
Designated heritage assets
5.    The appeal site lies within the Park Town Estate CA, whose significance derives
      from its planned Victorian composition, classical terraces and the disciplined
      roofscape formed by shallow hipped roofs concealed behind parapets. The corner
      buildings around St Philip Square, including the appeal property, are integral to
      this composition. Their roof profiles, chimney stacks and proportions contribute
      directly to the area’s historic character and to the coherence of the square.

6.    The Grade II listed Church of St Philip stands opposite the site and forms the
      architectural focus of the square. Its tower and spirelet were designed to rise
      above the surrounding terraces, establishing a clear hierarchy between landmark
      and enclosure. The appeal building forms part of this setting, and its existing
      concealed roofline contributes to that intended relationship.
7.    The proposal would involve the near total removal of the original roof and its
      replacement with a taller mansard structure. Even with the set back behind the
      parapet and the use of conservation rooflights, the increased height and altered
      profile would introduce a marked change in the established roof form. Although the
      dormers included in the previously rejected scheme have been removed, this
      change does not alter the fact that the increased height and altered roof profile
      would disrupt the disciplined roofscape of the corner buildings.
8.    The appellant argues that the parapet conceals the proposal in close-range views
      and that only limited or distant glimpses would be possible. Visibility from the
      immediate public realm is restricted, but the roofscape’s contribution to the CA’s
      significance lies in its consistent form, height and massing across the corner
      buildings. The increased height would alter that established relationship, and the
      change in roof form would be appreciable in the context of the CA’s overall
      composition even if public views are limited.
9.    The increased height would also affect the setting of the Grade II listed church.
      The existing building sits visually below the church’s tower, reinforcing the
      intended hierarchy between landmark and enclosure. The proposed mansard
      would raise the roofline and introduce a more assertive presence on the corner,
      reducing the clarity of that hierarchy. Seasonal tree cover provides partial
      screening, but the increased height would still diminish appreciation of the church’s
      upper elements in views where the relationship between the two buildings is
      experienced.
10. The proposal would create a five-storey building. Policy LP4 of the LP defines
    such development as mid-rise and restricts mid-rise buildings to designated zones.
    The appeal site lies outside those zones, and the increased height would therefore
    breach the spatial strategy. While the appellant refers to the Framework’s support
    for upward extensions, increased densities and taller forms at street corners, these
    provisions do not displace the development plan’s locational approach to height.
11. The appellant refers to Policies L2 and L3 of the Framework, arguing that national
    policy encourages upward extensions, increased densities and taller forms at
    street corners. However, Policy L2(i) supports upward development only where it
    would not cause substantial harm to the character of a building or its surroundings.
    Here the increased height would disrupt the disciplined roofscape that contributes
    to the CA’s significance and weaken the intended hierarchy between the appeal
    building and the listed church. Limited visibility from the closest street-level
    positions does not alter the underlying impact on the square’s established
    composition or on the appreciation of the listed building’s setting.
12. I have also had regard to the appellant’s references to other mansard
    developments and to other appeal decisions, but those examples differ materially
    in their architectural context and do not alter my findings on the effect of the
    proposal on the CA or the listed building’s setting.

13. Having regard to the above, the proposal would cause harm to the significance of
    the CA and the setting of the listed building. The Framework establishes that any
    harm to a designated heritage asset is a matter of considerable importance and
    weight, and requires that harm to be weighed against the public benefits of the
    proposal.
14. The appellant advances a range of benefits, including improved thermal
    performance, weathering, drainage and long-term durability, resolving historic
    water ingress, reducing pressure on the drainage system and providing additional
    naturally lit accommodation. However, the appellant has not demonstrated that the
    proposal is necessary to improve insulation, weathering or drainage. Further, the
    accommodation benefits are private in nature and do not amount to public benefits
    of meaningful scale.
15. The appellant suggests that glimpsed visibility should carry less weight than the
    benefits of additional floorspace, given the Framework’s direction to give
    substantial weight to better use of underutilised buildings. However, substantial
    weight applies only where the criteria in Policy L2 of the Framework are met and
    where no conflict arises with the conservation of designated heritage assets. The
    proposal does not meet those criteria, and the Framework requires great weight to
    be given to heritage conservation irrespective of the degree of harm. The statutory
    duties under Sections 66 and 72 of the Planning (Listed Buildings and
    Conservation Areas) Act 1990 reinforce this requirement.
16. When weighing the great weight to be given to the conservation of the designated
    heritage assets against the collective weight to be attributed to the benefits, the
    overall benefit would not be sufficient to outweigh the identified harm.
17. For these reasons, I conclude that the proposal would fail to preserve or enhance
    the character or appearance of the Park Town Estate CA and would also fail to
    preserve the setting of the Grade II listed church. It therefore conflicts with Policies
    LP1, LP3 and LP5 of the LP, which together require that development sustains
    and enhances the character and significance of heritage assets and responds
    positively to the established form and pattern of the area. The proposal also
    conflicts with Policy LP4 of the LP, which requires that mid-rise buildings are
    located only within the designated zones identified in the spatial strategy.
Conclusion
18. For the reasons given above, the proposal conflicts with the development plan and
    the material considerations do not indicate that the appeal should be decided other
    than in accordance with it. Accordingly, the appeal is dismissed.
