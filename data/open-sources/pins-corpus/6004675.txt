Reasons
Appeal A - Whether the development proposed by the application falls within the scope
of Section 73 of the Act - Appeal A
13. The scope of S73 of the Act is not limited to minor material amendments. However,
    the Courts4 have established that an application under S73 may not be used to
    obtain a permission that requires a variation to the terms of the ‘operative’ part of
    the original planning permission - which includes the description of development. It
    therefore follows that the removal and replacement of a plans condition which
    would authorise development outside of or in conflict with the operative part of the
    original planning permission, would be outside of the scope of S73.
14. Amongst other things, the plans subject of this appeal include the proposed
    formation of a single-storey link structure between the Farmhouse and the building
    identified as the ‘cottage’. They also include demolition and alterations to part of the
    side wall of the Farmhouse to enable access to be provided to the link, as well as
    associated demolition and alteration of some other parts of the Farmhouse to
    enable the provision of and access to a proposed first-floor level rear extension.
15. The original planning permission contains a detailed description of the development
    authorised by it. Significantly, it does not include a single-storey link between the

Appeal B
Significance
17. According to their official list entries, the Farmhouse and the Barn date from the
    16th Century, the Granary dates from the 17th Century, and all of these buildings
    were built as timber-framed structures. Nevertheless, compelling evidence has
    been provided indicating that the main block and the east ranges of the Farmhouse
    were likely to have been built at different times, with the east range predating the
    main block.
18. Although the buildings have undoubtedly been altered since their original
    construction, and they vary considerably in terms of their states of repair, they
    continue to retain much of their early timber framing, as well as large expanses of
    old tiled roofs. In addition, the Farmhouse retains its early brick-built chimney
    stacks, which include a large and imposing stack to the rear of the house.
    Furthermore, and even if the 20th century additions and alterations to the
    Farmhouse have not made a meaningful contribution to its significance, they have
    not prevented the earlier form and plan of this building from being readily
    appreciable.
19. Consequently, the previously identified early architectural features make a
    substantial contribution towards the historic and architectural interest of each of the
    Listed Buildings and to their significance.
20. The evidence indicates that in the 18th or 19th Centuries, the east range of the
    Farmhouse is likely to have been refaced and underbuilt, and a chimney breast and
    stack in the west wall of the east range added. Although the first-floor level chimney
    breast has subsequently been blocked up, these and other historic changes and
    additions to the building from the 18th and 19th Centuries, reflect the ongoing
    adaptations of the Farmhouse and make a positive contribution to its evidential and
    historic interest.
21. As part of a former farmstead, the Barn and the Granary would have historically
    had a close functional relationship with the Farmhouse. Consequently, and despite
    their different appearances, forms, and scales, all of the Listed Buildings - as well
    as other historic ancillary structures within their curtilages, gain some significance
    from their collective value as part of a former farmstead.
Effects of the works

22. The Council’s concern centres on perceived harm to the Listed Buildings from the
    works associated with the formation of a link structure between the Farmhouse and
    the ‘cottage’ and a highly glazed first-floor corridor to the rear of the Farmhouse.
    Having regard to the evidence before me, I am satisfied that the other proposed
    works, which are unrelated to these elements, would preserve the Listed Buildings,
    their settings, and any features of special architectural or historic interest which
    they possess, and that they would not cause harm to their significance.
23. In respect of the Farmhouse, the provision of the first-floor rear corridor/link would
    require the partial removal of a historic chimney breast in the west wall of the east
    range to provide access into bedroom 5. It would also require the demolition of a
    section of the rear wall of the building, as well as the demolition of what is likely to
    be an early rear dormer window - which was observed to have at least a partially
    peg-tiled roof, to provide access from the landing at the top of the stairwell. In
    addition, the provision of the link structure between the Farmhouse and the
    neighbouring cottage would require the removal of sections of brickwork in the side
    wall of the East range of the Farmhouse, around an existing window.
24. The evidence indicates that the section of wall that would be demolished under the
    dormer window is likely to date to the 20th century. On that basis, the associated
    removal of 20th century fabric to enable access to the first-floor rear corridor/link
    would not be materially harmful to the significance of the Farmhouse. However, the
    construction of the first-floor rear corridor/link and the link between the Farmhouse
    and the cottage would also necessitate losses to parts of the building’s historic
    fabric that make a positive contribution to its significance.
25. Moreover, the reflective surface of the glass and the dark bronze coloured
    framework of the highly-glazed proposed first-floor rear corridor/link to the rear of
    the Farmhouse, would draw the observer’s eye to this new part of the building and
    away from parts of the chimney stacks that would be enclosed by it. Even though
    these chimney stacks, which are a key architectural feature of the building, could
    continue to be discerned to a degree, such works would harmfully erode the
    legibility of the composition of the rear elevation of the Farmhouse.
26. Furthermore, insufficient information has been provided to enable me to understand
    how the framework and glazing associated with the first-floor rear corridor/link
    would adjoin historic parts of the walls and roof of the building, which are key
    architectural features that contribute greatly to its significance. Given the
    importance of these parts of the building, it is essential that full details of how the
    corridor/link would adjoin the building be provided, to allow the effects of the
    associated works to be fully assessed.
27. For these reasons, and even if the extent of the losses of the Farmhouse’s historic
    fabric would be modest in relation to the overall extent of the building, these losses
    would erode its architectural and historic interest.
28. Because the structure that would link the Farmhouse and the Cottage would be of
    low height and ‘light weight’ appearance, it would read as a clearly subservient
    addition to both buildings. That being the case, it would not erode the primacy of
    the main Farmhouse building at the heart of the former farmstead, or the separate
    identities of the buildings around it. Consequently, the development would not
    erode the collective group value of the Listed Buildings, or the contribution that
    each of the Listed Buildings make to the others significance.

29. Nevertheless, and although the scheme would not cause harm to the significance
    of the Granary or the Barn, for the reasons previously given, it would cause harm to
    the significance of the Farmhouse.
Public benefits / Heritage balance
30. In line with policy HE6: 1. of the National Planning Policy Framework (the
    Framework), substantial weight is given to the conservation of the Listed Buildings.
31. The harm that would be caused to the significance of the Farmhouse would be to a
    lower level within this category. This is because the scheme would result in the loss
    of small parts of its historic fabric that make a meaningful contribution to its
    significance. It is also because the works would harmfully reduce the legibility of
    some of its key historic architectural features.
32. In accordance with policy HE6: 3, of the Framework, the harm that would be
    caused to the significance of the Farmhouse is attributed considerable importance
    and weight.
33. Framework Policy HE6: 4 then indicates that any harm to the heritage asset and its
    significance should be weighed against any public benefits arising from the
    proposal.
34. The implementation of the appeal scheme would undoubtedly facilitate the re-use
    of the Farmhouse as a residential dwelling. However, I have no reason to doubt
    that the building could be brought back into use with less harmful works being
    undertaken. As such, modest weight is accorded to this public benefit.
35. Subject to the proposed works being carried out to the Barn and to the Granary, the
    scheme would also contribute towards facilitating the re-use and preservation of
    these buildings, which would attract substantial weight. However, in the absence of
    a planning obligation or any suggested condition wording requiring a programme of
    works for the entire scheme to be submitted to and approved in writing by the
    Council, I cannot be certain that the public benefits that would flow from the works
    to the Barn and the Granary would result. Therefore, only a small amount of weight
    can be attributed to the potential public benefits arising from the works to the
    Granary and the Barn.
36. Economic benefits would arise during the preparation for and implementation of the
    works. However, because the scheme relates to a single house and associated
    outbuildings, the associated public benefits would be small and very little weight is
    attributed to them.
37. The alterations to the residential dwelling and its enlargement would enhance the
    living conditions available to future occupiers of the property. However, this is a
    private rather than a public benefit as set out in the Government’s Planning
    Practice Guidance5.
38. Consequently, and even though the completion of the works would preserve the
    Grade II Listed Buildings at the Barn and the Granary, their settings and any
    features of special architectural or historic interest which they possess, the works
    would not preserve the Grade II Listed Building at the Farmhouse or any features of
    special architectural or historic interest which it possesses. That being the case,

Other Matters
39. The appeal site is within the zone of influence of the Burnham Beeches Special
    Area of Conservation (the SAC), which is a habitat site afforded protection under
    the Habitat Regulations, and which is subject to recreational and air quality
    pressures.
40. If I had otherwise been minded to allow either of the appeals, it would have been
    necessary to establish whether, without mitigation, the scheme would have a likely
    significant effect on the integrity of this habitat site – either on its own or in
    combination with other projects. Depending on the findings on this matter, it could
    then have been necessary to undertake an Appropriate Assessment. However, I
    have not needed to pursue this issue because a finding that the proposal would not
    have an adverse effect on the integrity of the SAC, either with or without mitigation,
    would, at best, be a neutral matter.
41. I appreciate that there is an extant listed building consent for an alternative scheme
    on the site subject of this appeal, and accept that there is a more than theoretical
    possibility that the approved scheme could be implemented. However, while
    sharing many similarities with the previously approved scheme, the proposal
    subject of appeal B includes additional elements and more demolition than the
    fallback scheme. Furthermore, although the proposal would not require either the
    subdivision of what would become the master bedroom, or the removal of a section
    of the building’s historic timber framing to accommodate an opening to provide first-
    floor corridor access to bedroom 3, the nature and extent of the proposed works
    subject of appeal B would introduce additional harm which would not occur if the
    fallback scheme were implemented. That being the case, the fallback scheme does
    not weigh in favour of Appeal B.
Conclusions
42. For the reasons given, appeals A and B should be dismissed and the applications
    for planning permission and listed building consent should be refused.
