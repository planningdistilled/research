 Appeal Decision
 Site visit made on 3 September 2026
 by D Cleary MTCP MRTPI
 an Inspector appointed by the Secretary of State
 Decision date: 17 September 2026


 Appeal Ref: 6013434
 1 Baskerville Road, Wandsworth, London SW18 3RJ
 •   The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
     against a refusal to grant planning permission.
 •   The appeal is made by Mr Luke Stovell against the decision of the Council of the London Borough of
     Wandsworth.
 •   The application Ref is 2026/0479.
 •   The development proposed is described as Alterations including excavation to extend existing
     basement floorspace including formation of front and rear lightwells; demolition and replacement of
     side extension at ground floor level; alterations to rear ground floor elevation; addition of side bay
     window at first floor level; and replacement side dormer window at roof level.

Decision
1. The appeal is dismissed.
Preliminary Matters
2. The appeal includes amended plans for my consideration. However, neither the
   Council nor third parties have had the opportunity to comment on these plans.
   Therefore, I have considered the appeal based on the plans which were before the
   Council when they made their decision.
3. During the course of the appeal a new National Planning Policy Framework (the
   Framework) was published on the 17 August 2026. Whilst the format has altered,
   any considerations that are material to this decision have not fundamentally
   changed. I am satisfied that this has not prejudiced any party and I have had regard
   to the latest version of the Framework in reaching my decision.
Main Issues
4. The main issues are the effect of the development on:
     a) the character and appearance of the building and whether the development
        would preserve or enhance the character or appearance of the Wandsworth
        Common Conservation Area; and,
     b) the living conditions of the occupiers of neighbouring properties with particular
        regard to loss of privacy.

Reasons
Character and Appearance
5. The appeal site lies within the Wandsworth Common Conservation Area (the
   WCCA). Under Section 72(1) of the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation
   Areas) Act 1990, special attention must be paid to preserving or enhancing its
   character or appearance. The Framework, at HE9, advises that development in
   conservation areas should retain and conserve buildings and other features which
   make a positive contribution to the character or appearance of the conservation
   area, and consider the area’s special architectural or historic interest in the design
   of development.
6. The WCCA is a large area and comprises the open space of Wandsworth Common
   and its surrounding streets. The Wandsworth Common Conservation Area
   Appraisal provides a good understanding of the WCCA and its special interest. The
   appeal site lies within a group of streets known as the Toast Rack. The Toast Rack
   forms a highly distinctive late-Victorian residential enclave whose significance lies
   in its tight grid of substantial red-brick semi-detached houses and unified
   architectural detailing. This strong uniformity, combined with its intimate relationship
   with the open landscape of Wandsworth Common, gives the area a coherent
   historic character and a clear sense of planned suburban development that
   contributes materially to the WCCA’s overall architectural and townscape interest.
7. The dwelling forms one half of a substantial late-Victorian red-brick semi-detached
   pair. The pair are characterised by steeply pitched roofs with prominent gables,
   two-storey bays, and consistent detailing to openings that emphasises its verticality
   and rhythm. Together, the pair presents a strong, unified architectural composition
   with matching massing and materials. The existing single-storey side extension is
   of limited architectural quality and slightly detracts from the clarity of the original
   composition. Nonetheless, its set back and subservience to the original dwelling,
   together with partial screening by the boundary wall, means it does not undermine
   the overall positive contribution the dwelling and its pair make to the WCCA.
8. Single storey extensions of varying design are commonplace along Baskerville
   Road. However, the original alignment and rhythm of first floor openings remain
   largely unaltered. The proposed single-storey rear extension would be modest in
   height and depth, appearing visually subservient, and would be similar in scale to
   other rear extensions along the street. Its overall height would be set below the sill
   level of the first-floor windows. As such, the continuity of first-floor openings, which
   forms an important part of the architectural rhythm of the rear elevation, would not
   be disrupted.
9. The replacement single-storey side extension would occupy a similar footprint to
   the existing structure, but would be positioned slightly closer to the street.
   Nevertheless, it would remain set behind the two-storey principal façade and would
   read as a clearly subordinate element. Its proportions, simple form and coherent
   detailing, including the alignment of openings, would represent an enhancement
   over the existing addition, appearing more visually cohesive and reinforcing the
   architectural hierarchy of the host building. These qualities would allow the side
   extension to relate more comfortably to the balanced composition of the
   semi-detached pair.

10. The first-floor side extension would take the form of a modest flat-roofed bay
    positioned above the replacement single-storey side addition. Its restrained scale,
    set back from the principal façade, alignment of openings and the use of
    sympathetic architectural detailing would ensure that it integrates with the
    established composition of the dwelling. Although projecting slightly from the side
    wall, its position, scale and design would ensure that it appears as a clearly
    subordinate and harmonious addition. The replacement dormer, positioned
    adjacent to the eaves, would retain clear roofscape around its upper and side
    edges. Its siting and modest proportions would prevent it from appearing as an
    unduly prominent addition to the roof.
11. The combined effect of the first-floor side extension and replacement dormer would
    result in a well-proportioned and coherent flank elevation. Their scale and detailing
    would preserve the legibility of the original dwelling and its semi-detached pair,
    avoiding any erosion of the balanced composition and architectural rhythm that
    contribute to the significance of the Toast Rack, and the wider WCCA
12. Notwithstanding the above, the appeal scheme also includes the introduction of a
    rooftop lantern which would project above the ridgeline of the main roof. This would
    introduce an incongruous feature which would visually imbalance the roofscape of
    the host dwelling and its semi-detached neighbour. Consequently, I find that this
    would be a harmful addition which would erode important aspects that contribute
    towards the significance of the WCCA. However, it is a very minor component of
    the overall development proposed and, if I were minded to allow the appeal, I
    consider that this harmful element could be removed by way of condition.
13. Therefore, for the reasons set out above, and subject to the necessary removal of
    the rooftop lantern, the development would not have a harmful effect on the
    character and appearance of the building, and would preserve the character and
    appearance of the WCCA. The development complies with Policies LP1, LP3 and
    LP5 of the Wandsworth Local Plan 2023-2038 (the WLP). Together, amongst other
    things, these seek to ensure that development proposals are of a high-quality
    design, which conserve or enhance the historic environment.
Living Conditions
14. The existing balcony at the rear is modest in size and its limited depth restricts
    meaningful occupation. Its position set away from the shared boundary with No. 2
    Baskerville Road (No.2) also limits the prospect of intrusive overlooking. The
    proposal includes a substantially larger terrace, projecting further from the rear
    elevation and extending up to the shared boundary with No. 2. As a result, the
    terrace would function as a more usable outdoor space and would materially
    increase opportunities for overlooking of the neighbouring garden.
15. The enlarged terrace, by projecting further from the rear elevation and extending up
    to the shared boundary, would enable direct and elevated views into the
    neighbouring garden. This would result in intrusive overlooking beyond that which
    currently occurs resulting in a loss of privacy within the rear garden space. The
    terrace would also facilitate unobstructed and direct views towards first-floor
    windows at the rear of No.2. Given the terrace’s elevated position and the
    alignment between the two properties, this would result in a significant loss of
    privacy for the neighbouring occupiers.

16. While the appellant suggests that privacy screening could mitigate these effects,
    the proximity of the terrace to the boundary means that any screen of sufficient
    height would be likely to appear visually intrusive and could introduce additional
    issues relating to light and outlook. I therefore cannot be certain that such a
    measure would adequately address the harm. In any event, such a screen along
    the boundary would not address the resultant overlooking into the garden space of
    No.2.
17. I recognise that terraces are a characteristic feature along Baskerville Road and
    that a degree of mutual overlooking is inherent to the pattern of development. I also
    acknowledge that planning permission has been granted for some larger terraces in
    the wider area. However, the evidence before me is limited, and I cannot be certain
    of the specific design, context or circumstances that led to those approvals. In this
    case, the terrace would facilitate direct and close-range views into the neighbouring
    garden and towards first-floor windows, resulting in a materially greater degree of
    overlooking to an extent that would be unacceptably harmful. The presence of other
    terraces therefore does not alter my conclusions and, in any event, I have
    considered this appeal on its individual merits.
18. Therefore, for the reasons outlined above, the development would have an
    unacceptable effect on the living conditions of the occupiers of neighbouring
    properties with particular regard to loss of privacy. The development conflicts with
    Policy LP2 of the WLP. This, amongst other things, this requires that development
    proposals do not adversely impact the amenity of existing occupiers by avoiding
    unacceptable levels of overlooking.
Other matters
19. I note the concerns raised by No.1 Routh Road with regard to overlooking. The
    Council is satisfied that there would be sufficient spacing between new and altered
    openings facing towards that property to ensure that living conditions would not be
    adversely affected. Based on my observations on site, I see no reason to disagree
    with the conclusions of the Council on this matter.
Conclusion
20. For the reasons given above the appeal should be dismissed.
