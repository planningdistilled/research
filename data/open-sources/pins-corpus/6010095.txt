•    The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
     against a refusal to grant planning permission under section 73 of the Town and Country Planning
     Act 1990 (as amended) for the development of land without complying with conditions subject to
     which a previous planning permission was granted.
•    The appeal is made by Mr Gareth Langmead against the decision of Trafford Metropolitan Borough
     Council.
•    The application Ref is 118677/VAR/26.
•    The application sought planning permission for part retrospective application for the change of use
     from Class E (Cafe) to Sui Generis (Drinking Establishment), erection of front and side retractable
     folding arm awnings, alterations to the frontage to also include new bifold doors, timber fence to side
     and introduction of a cycle store to the rear, without complying with a condition attached to planning
     permission Ref 116380/FUL/25 dated 24 October 2025.
•    The condition in dispute is No 9 which states that: Within 6 months from the date of this planning
     permission being granted, an Acoustic Verification Report (AVR) shall be prepared by a suitably
     qualified acoustician and submitted to, and approved in writing by the Local Planning Authority.

     This AVR shall demonstrate that sufficient sound insulation treatments have been provided to the
     separating ceiling and floor between the ground floor drinking establishment hereby permitted, and
     the adjoining first floor residential unit, having regard to the proposed specifications referred to within
     section 13 and Appendix F to the supporting Noise Impact Assessment (NIA) prepared by Noise
     Assessments Ltd ref. NALPRO100325.01, dated 18/09/25.

     The AVR shall describe the provided sound insulation treatments, including supporting technical and
     photographic evidence, and detail the methodology and results of sound insulation testing and
     calculations to demonstrate that the ground floor operational internal noise level described in section
     12 to the NIA, will not exceed the operational sound target level of NR20 (noise rating curve) within
     each habitable room on the first floor. The sound insulation treatments referred to within the
     approved AVR shall be kept in good order for the lifetime of the development.

•    The reason given for the condition is: In the interests of residential amenity having regard to Policy
     L7 of the Trafford Core Strategy and the National Planning Policy Framework.

Decision
1.     The appeal is dismissed.
Background and Main Issue
2.     Planning permission has been previously granted for the change of use of the
       appeal building, from a café to a drinking establishment, including further
       alterations, subject to conditions. One of these conditions was Condition 9, which
       required the submission of an Acoustic Verification Report (AVR) to be prepared
       and submitted to the Council, which demonstrates that sufficient sound insulation
       treatments have been provided to the separating ceiling and floor between the
       appeal site, and the adjoining first floor residential unit above it. This was required

       within six months of the date of planning permission being granted. This
       conditional requirement has not been carried out.
3.     It follows that a Section 73 application to vary the condition in question was
       submitted to the Council, with alternative suggested wording proposed. This
       alternative wording sought to remove the six-month time limit on Condition 9, and
       replace it with a stipulation that required any operator of the appeal site which had
       live music playing beyond 10:30pm, or the volume of any music exceeded 66
       decibels, would require the submission of an AVR, with sufficient sound insulation
       treatments being shown to have been carried out. This application is the subject of
       this appeal.
4.     The main issue therefore is whether the variation of Condition 9 would protect the
       living conditions of neighbouring residential occupiers, with regard to noise and
       disturbance.
Reasons
5.     The revised wording of Condition 9 as suggested by the appellant, promotes a
       particular focus on live music only, and the volume of such music exceeding 66
       decibels, or being played beyond 10:30pm. This revised wording therefore would
       only focus on the noise created by music, not any other noise. Noise is not just
       confined to music. This is a bar. It is a place where people gather, the evidence of
       which suggests that there is room for 80 patrons. This is not a small number of
       individuals. This is acknowledged in the submitted Noise Management Plan
       (prepared by Noise Assessments Ltd, dated 18 September 2025), which notes that
       in addition to music, noise would be generated from the front outdoor smoking
       area, and crucially, the noise from patrons raised voices. In combination, this
       would be capable of being heard by residents in the flat above, even if music was
       limited to 66 decibels.
6.     The application was accompanied by a Noise Impact Assessment prepared by
       Noise Assessments Ltd. Importantly, the assessment acknowledges that, when
       accounting for the anticipated increase in patron numbers and higher music levels,
       the resultant internal noise level is likely to be in the region of 83 decibels. The
       appellant's own evidence therefore indicates that the expected internal noise
       environment would exceed the 66-decibel limit proposed within the revised
       wording of Condition 9. While it may be possible in principle to utilise a volume
       limiter to restrict live music to a specified decibel level, no comparable mechanism
       exists to effectively control the noise generated by up to 80 customers within a bar
       environment. Patron conversations, raised voices and general activity are inherent
       features of such a use and can contribute significantly to overall noise levels.
7.     Consequently, whilst the appellant may contend that live music would not exceed
       66 decibels and would cease by 10.30pm, these measures would address only
       one aspect of the noise generated by the operation. Music is not the sole source of
       noise associated with the premises, and the evidence indicates that overall noise
       levels during opening hours would remain elevated. This conclusion is supported
       by the appellant's own Noise Impact Assessment, which identifies an anticipated
       internal noise level of approximately 83 decibels. As such, the proposed revision to
       Condition 9 would not adequately control all sources of noise arising from the use
       of the premises.

8.     I am drawn to the mitigation measures proposed at section 4 of the Noise
       Management Plan. However, none of the proposed measures adequately address
       the principal issue of the overall noise levels anticipated to arise from the operation
       of the premises. Given the close proximity of the residential flat above the
       premises, I am not persuaded that the proposed mitigation measures would be
       sufficient to prevent unacceptable levels of noise disturbance. During periods of
       high occupancy, the noise generated by the operation is likely to reach a level that
       would adversely affect the living conditions of the occupants of the flat above,
       particularly in terms of residential amenity and the potential for noise nuisance and
       disturbance.
9.     The appellant submits that no formal noise complaints have been made in relation
       to the premises. However, the absence of formal complaints does not, in itself,
       demonstrate that the current situation is satisfactory or that there would be no
       adverse impact arising from the proposed development. There may be a variety of
       reasons why individuals have not lodged complaints, including a reluctance to
       identify themselves or engage in a formal complaints process.
10. In any event, the assessment of the proposal must be based on the likely effects of
    the development, informed by the available technical evidence, rather than solely
    on whether complaints have been received. The planning system seeks to ensure
    that an appropriate standard of living conditions is secured for existing and future
    occupiers. In this case, that requires consideration of whether acceptable noise
    levels can be achieved and maintained through suitable mitigation measures. It
    would not be appropriate to permit a development that is likely to expose residents
    to unacceptable levels of noise, only to address those impacts retrospectively once
    harm has occurred and demonstrated through a complaint. The objective should
    be to prevent such harm from arising in the first place, rather than relying on a
    reactive approach after residential amenity has already been adversely affected.
11. The appellant has also criticised the Council for not undertaking specific on-site
    noise testing. However, the burden of demonstrating the acceptability of the
    proposal rests with the appellant. This is particularly relevant where the appellant's
    own evidence indicates that the operation of the venue is likely to generate
    significant levels of noise.
12. Both parties have referred to the 'Agent of Change' principle in their submissions.
    The appellant contends that this principle has not been applied proportionately and
    points to the appeal site's location within an established mixed-use commercial
    parade. Whilst that is accepted, my observations during the site visit were that the
    surrounding commercial uses comprise, amongst others, a beauty salon, retail
    premises, a dental practice and a medical centre. These are not uses typically
    associated with significant levels of noise generation, nor do they generally
    operate during late evening hours.
13. The appellant further argues that the original planning permission accepted the
    principle of the premises operating as a drinking establishment. However, planning
    permission was granted subject to several planning conditions, including Condition
    9, which was imposed specifically to ensure that the use would not result in
    unacceptable impacts on neighbouring occupiers. The acceptability of the use was
    therefore not unconditional. Rather, it was dependent upon compliance with
    conditions, one of which was imposed to protect the living conditions of nearby
    occupiers.

14. The appellant states that cost estimates have been obtained for the acoustic works
    necessary to comply with Condition 9 and contends that these costs would be
    disproportionate. However, those cost estimates have not been provided for
    consideration. As set out elsewhere in this decision, the appellant's case for
    amending Condition 9 focuses predominantly on controlling noise arising from live
    music. However, this approach fails to address the wider range of noise
    associated with the operation of the premises, most notably that generated by
    patrons. The evidence indicates that customer activity, including conversations,
    raised voices and general movement within and around the venue, would
    contribute significantly to the overall noise environment. This is the critical failing of
    this appeal.
15. The appellant contends that several factors weigh strongly in favour of allowing the
    appeal. In addition, several representations have been submitted in support of
    both the application and the appeal, highlighting the perceived benefits of the
    premises to the local community. Whilst I have had regard to these
    representations and acknowledge the value that the premises may provide to local
    patrons, such considerations do not negate the requirement to safeguard the living
    conditions of nearby residents. The existence of community support does not
    diminish the need to ensure that development can operate without causing
    unacceptable harm to residential amenity. Consequently, although the benefits
    identified by supporters of the proposal attract some limited, yet positive weight in
    favour of the appeal, they do not outweigh the concerns relating to noise and
    disturbance.
16. To conclude, the proposed variation of Condition 9 would not protect the living
    conditions of neighbouring residential occupiers, with regard to noise and
    disturbance. As such, it would conflict with Policy L7 of the Trafford Local Plan:
    Core Strategy 2012 and Policy JP-P1 of the Places for Everyone Joint
    Development Plan Document 2024. These policies seek for the amenity of
    residents to be protected. Particularly of note is that development must be
    compatible with the surrounding area and not prejudice the amenity of the
    occupants of adjacent properties, with particular regard given to noise and
    disturbance, amongst other things.
Conclusion
17. For the reasons given above and in taking all material considerations into account,
    the appeal should be dismissed.
