 Appeal Decision
 Site visit made on 3 September 2026
 by D Cleary MTCP MRTPI
 an Inspector appointed by the Secretary of State
 Decision date: 29 September 2026


 Appeal Ref: 6009988
 56 Burlington Road, New Malden, Kingston Upon Thames KT3 4NU
 •   The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
     against a refusal to grant planning permission.
 •   The appeal is made by Barry Doherty of Odyssey Developments (UK) Ltd against the decision of the
     Council of the Royal Borough of Kingston Upon Thames.
 •   The application Ref is 25/02055/FUL.
 •   The development proposed is described as the demolition of existing building to allow the erection of
     a 4 storey building to accommodate a replacement commercial unit and 7 flats.

Decision
1. The appeal is dismissed.
Procedural Matters
2. On 17 August 2026, the Government revised the National Planning Policy
   Framework (the Framework) and this post-dated the planning application and the
   appeal. The main parties were asked to comment on the relevance of the revised
   Framework to the appeal, and I have taken into account the comments received in
   coming to my decision.
Main Issues
3. The main issues are the effect of the development on:
     a) the living conditions of the occupiers of neighbouring properties with particular
        regard to daylight;
     b) the living conditions of future occupiers of the development with particular
        regard to external living space; and,
     c) highway safety, having regard to parking provision.
Reasons
Living Conditions - Neighbours
4. Adjacent to the appeal site is a block of residential flats known as 52-54 Burlington
   Road (Nos. 52-54). There are a number of windows within the flank elevation of
   Nos. 52-54 which face directly towards the appeal site at very close distance. This
   includes a ground-floor window serving a bedroom (referred to as W15). It is
   understood that this is the sole opening to that room. As existing, this window faces
   towards a boundary fence and the existing two-storey building. To the front of the

     building is an open forecourt. The open forecourt means that, as existing, the
     bedroom served by W15 is likely to receive a reasonable level of daylight.
5. The appeal scheme proposes a three-storey apartment building with further
   accommodation within a steeply pitched roof space. This would be positioned
   directly on the boundary line and replacing the existing fence and building. Given its
   height and massing, the building would appear visually prominent in views from
   W15. The scale and proximity of the structure, combined with the ground-floor
   position of W15, means that the development would result in a significant reduction
   in daylight to the bedroom.
6. The application was supported by a Daylight & Sunlight Report (DSR) 1. The DSR
   provides an assessment of the effects of the development on neighbouring
   properties informed by BRE guidance2. This includes Vertical Sky Component
   (VSC) and No-Skyline (NSL) tests for daylight, and the Annual Probable Sunlight
   Hours (APSH) test for sunlight.
7. In this case the DSR confirms that W15 would experience a 93.7% reduction in
   VSC, and a 100% loss under the NSL test. These significantly exceed the BRE
   guidance. As such, the figures presented in the DSR reaffirm the concerns relating
   to daylight for the room served by W15. The effect on this window would not be
   marginal. The magnitude in the loss of daylight for the bedroom would be
   substantial and would lead to a gloomy and oppressive internal environment for
   occupants of that property. When considering the effect on daylight to a habitable
   room, there is no compelling evidence before me to suggest that a bedroom should
   be treated as a less sensitive space.
8. The Council also raise concern with the effect on three flank-facing windows within
   Nos. 52-54 at second floor level serving an open-plan kitchen/dining room. These
   would also face the appeal development at very close proximity. The submitted
   evidence indicates that these windows would fall below the accepted BRE standard
   for VSC. However, the room is also served by two further openings to the front and
   rear, and the effect of the development on those windows would remain within
   acceptable tolerances. As such, I do not find the impact on daylight to this room to
   be as severe and would, on balance, be acceptable.
9. I have considered the mirror-image test submitted by the appellant. However, this
   does not demonstrate that the impact of the appeal scheme would be acceptable.
   At most, it illustrates the inherent sensitivity of W15 due to its position. It does not
   follow that the degree of harm arising from the proposed massing should be
   regarded as tolerable. In any event, there is no compelling evidence that the appeal
   scheme represents the only feasible redevelopment of the site; alternative forms of
   development could reasonably achieve a lesser impact on this window
10. The appellant also relies on precedent of the relationship between a bedroom
    window within No.50 Burlington Road (No.50) and the recently constructed Nos.
    52-54. I do not have full details of that approval or any evidence on the effect of
    Nos. 52-54 on daylight/sunlight to neighbouring rooms. Nonetheless, while I note
    that the separation between No.50 and Nos. 52-54 would be similar to the appeal

11. Taking all evidence into account, I am not persuaded that the appellant’s
    assessment demonstrates that the impacts would be acceptable. Although there is
    broad compliance across the wider building, the severity of the loss to W15 would
    be substantial. The proposal would result in a marked and harmful reduction in
    daylight to a habitable bedroom.
12. For the above reasons, the development would have an unacceptable effect on the
    living conditions of neighbouring properties with particular regard to loss of daylight.
    The development therefore conflicts with Policy DM10 of the Royal Borough of
    Kingston Upon Thames Local Development Framework Core Strategy 2012 (the
    CS) and Policy D3 of the London Plan 2021 (the LP). Together, amongst other
    things, these require development to have regard to the amenities of neighbours in
    terms of daylight and achieve comfortable indoor environments.
Living Conditions – Future occupiers
13. The Residential Design Supplementary Planning Document 2013 (the SPD)
    provides guidance on private and communal amenity space for residential
    developments. Policy Guidance 13 (PG13) sets out expectations for private
    amenity space, while Policy Guidance 14 (PG14) states that all new flat
    developments should provide communal amenity space in addition to the private
    requirements of PG13. PG14 suggests that 50sqm of communal amenity space
    should be provided, plus any shortfall arising from PG13.
14. Flats 1–5 would each be provided with private external spaces ranging between
    6sqm and 12sqm. Some of these fall below the suggested 10sqm standard
    (excluding any additional quantum for occupancy). Flats 6 and 7 would not benefit
    from any private external space.
15. Neither party has provided a detailed breakdown of the total communal amenity
    space required. The Council suggests a shortfall of 28sqm, while the appellant
    considers that the proposed play space (22sqm) should be included within the
    communal provision calculation. On that basis, they consider that a total of 45sqm
    of communal amenity space would be delivered.
16. This falls below the suggested 50sqm identified in the SPD, excluding any
    additional shortfall that may arise. Nonetheless, I am mindful that the SPD is
    guidance rather than policy. The play space and communal amenity space are
    located adjacent to one another and are not fully segregated. They would therefore
    function as a single usable area, and there is nothing before me to suggest that the
    play space could not reasonably be included within the communal calculation.

17. The space provided would be sufficient in size and proportion to accommodate the
    essential day-to-day activities expected for future occupiers. While there would
    likely be a shortfall in provision when assessed against the SPD, the shortfall would
    not be so significant to result in a poor external living environment for future
    occupiers of the development.
18. I am therefore satisfied that the development would not lead to a substandard living
    environment for future occupiers with regard to external living space. As such, the
    development complies with Policy DM10 of the CS and D6 of the LP. Together,
    amongst other things, these require development to deliver adequate private and
    communal living space for future occupants.
Parking Provision
19. The appeal scheme does not propose any off-street parking. Burlington Road is
    subject to parking restrictions, and nearby residential streets fall within a Controlled
    Parking Zone (CPZ) where parking permits are in operation. These streets appear
    to be well saturated. If permits were issued to future occupiers of the development,
    this would place additional pressure on on-street parking, potentially leading to
    operational difficulties and highway safety concerns. Although the site is in a
    sustainable location with good access to public transport, there can be no certainty
    that future occupiers would not own cars. Without appropriate control, the
    development could therefore lead to parking issues to the detriment of the local
    highway network.
20. The appellant has submitted a signed Unilateral Undertaking (UU) confirming that
    future occupiers would be prevented from obtaining parking permits. In principle,
    this would mitigate the potential effects of local parking pressure arising from the
    development.
21. The UU includes provision for a Management Monitoring Fee (MMF) payable to the
    Council. However, the MMF is undefined, and there is no evidence that any fee has
    been paid. Monitoring is essential to ensure the obligation operates effectively.
    Without a specified amount, the undertaking is incomplete and the obligation
    cannot be enforced.
22. As a result, the submitted UU cannot be relied upon to prevent future occupiers
    from obtaining parking permits. In the absence of a complete and enforceable
    obligation, the development would give rise to unacceptable pressure on on-street
    parking. Accordingly, the scheme conflicts with Policy T6 of the LP and Policy
    DM10 of the CS, which together seek to manage parking demand and safeguard
    local highway conditions.
Other matters
23. The Local Planning Authority is currently unable to demonstrate a five-year supply
    of deliverable housing sites. Consequently, the delivery of seven new flats would
    contribute to addressing this shortfall, a matter to which I attach substantial weight.
    As the site lies within the defined settlement boundary, Policy S4(1) of the
    Framework is relevant. This requires that proposals be approved unless their
    benefits are substantially outweighed by adverse effects when assessed against
    national decision-making policies.

24. Turning to the adverse effects, the evidence demonstrates that the massing of the
    proposed block would result in a severe and significant reduction in daylight to a
    habitable bedroom window of the neighbouring property. The magnitude of this loss
    goes well beyond minor urban friction and represents a profound deterioration in
    living conditions. The proposal therefore conflicts with DM10 of the CS and D3 of
    the LP, along with the Framework’s decision-making policies (L2 and P3), which
    seek to ensure acceptable living standards in terms of daylight and sunlight.
25. In applying the planning balance required by Policy S4, I have given substantial
    weight to the delivery of seven new homes in an area with an acknowledged
    housing shortfall. However, the benefits of additional housing cannot outweigh the
    consequences of creating an unacceptably compromised living environment for an
    existing resident.
26. Given the extraordinary magnitude of daylight loss to the affected window, I find
    that the severe harm to residential amenity represents a compelling and overriding
    counterweight. In this case, the adverse effects substantially outweigh the benefits
    when assessed against national decision-making policies as a whole. The proposal
    therefore fails the test in Policy S4.
Conclusion
27. For the reasons given above the appeal should be dismissed.
