Marina Court, 34 Banks Road, Poole BH13 7QE
•    The appeal is made under Section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
     against a failure to give notice within the prescribed period of a decision on an application for
     planning permission.
•    The appeal is made by Mr S Whitworth against Bournemouth Christchurch and Poole Council.
•    The application Ref is P/25/03296/FUL.
•    The development proposed is demolish garage to create a vehicular access and erect a detached
     bungalow.

Decision
1.    The appeal is allowed and planning permission is granted for demolish garage to
      create a vehicular access and erect a detached bungalow at Marina Court, 34
      Banks Road, Poole, BH13 7QE in accordance with the terms of the application,
      Ref P/25/03296/FUL, subject to the conditions in the attached schedule.
Application for costs
2.    An application for costs was made by Mr S Whitworth against Bournemouth
      Christchurch and Poole Council. This application is the subject of a separate
      decision.
Preliminary Matters
3.    The appeal arises from the Council’s failure to reach a decision within the statutory
      timeframe. In its statement, the Council indicates that, had it been able to
      determine the application, it would have issued a refusal. The putative reasons for
      refusal concern character and appearance of the area, living conditions for future
      occupiers, flood risk and the mitigation for European designated sites.
Main Issues
4.    The main issues are:
           •    the effect of the proposed development on the character and appearance of
                the area;
           •    whether the proposal would provide satisfactory living conditions for future
                occupiers of the development, having regard to light, outlook and the
                usability of external space;
           •    whether the proposed development is acceptable in flood risk terms; and

Reasons
Character and appearance
5.    The appeal site occupies a position behind the Marina Court garages. The
      garages form a continuous low structure that screens the land from Banks Road,
      while the dwellings on Salter Road and Panorama Road restrict views from those
      streets. These elements combine to create a pocket of land that is already visually
      enclosed from public vantage points, meaning that the proposed bungalow within it
      would have a limited influence on the public realm.
6.    The wider townscape displays considerable variety. The local area contains flats,
      bungalows, two-storey dwellings and examples of backland development,
      reflecting a pattern of incremental subdivision rather than a planned or uniform
      layout. In this context, the appeal site does not present as an anomaly. Its irregular
      geometry is consistent with the fragmented pattern of land parcels created
      historically around Marina Court, and the proposal would sit comfortably within that
      established form rather than appearing as an incongruous departure.
7.    The small size and sloping form of the site are evident, but they do not prevent the
      land from accommodating a modest building. Several nearby plots are smaller,
      and many exhibit higher built-form ratios. The proposed footprint would sit
      comfortably within the site and would not exceed the scale of development typical
      of the immediate area. As a result, the building would not appear compressed or
      contrived within its boundaries, and its proportions would remain in keeping with
      the prevailing grain of development.
8.    The height and massing of the proposal are modest. The flat roof would sit below
      the eaves of dwellings on Salter Road and below the ridge heights on Panorama
      Road. Although retaining structures would be visible from the boatyard, that
      environment is private and commercial rather than a sensitive public vantage
      point. From some surrounding residential properties, the development would be
      visible. However, its modest height and massing mean that, even where seen, it
      would not appear intrusive or disrupt the established pattern of spacing or building
      hierarchy.
9.    The proposed layout responds to the site’s geometry rather than imposing artificial
      regularity. The dwelling would occupy the widest part of the plot, and the external
      spaces would be arranged logically around it. This approach results in a coherent
      form that respects the constraints of the land. The development would therefore
      integrate into the existing townscape without eroding its character or
      spaciousness, and its modest scale would ensure that the sense of enclosure
      already present on the site is not materially intensified.
10. For these reasons, the proposal would not cause material harm to the character
    and appearance of the area. Consequently, it accords with Policies PP27 and
    PP28 of the Poole Local Plan (2018) (LP) and Policy SAND5 of the Sandbanks
    Peninsula Neighbourhood Plan (2023) (NP), which require development to respect
    the prevailing pattern of built form, respond appropriately to site context and
    maintain the established qualities of the local townscape.

Living conditions
11. The appellant has submitted a daylight and overshadowing assessment that
    models daylight distribution within the proposed rooms and the extent of
    overshadowing across the garden. It concludes that all habitable rooms would
    achieve the required target illuminance for over half of their floor area. Although
    the Council and interested parties have questioned aspects of the assessment,
    including its scope, methodology and the absence of a separate sunlight analysis,
    none has provided any technical evidence to dispute or contradict the appellant’s
    modelling. The submitted assessment is based on recognised methodology and is
    sufficiently robust to inform my judgement.
12. The internal layout of the dwelling would consist of habitable rooms arranged
    around a central circulation space. The modelling demonstrates that these rooms
    would receive adequate levels of diffuse daylight. With respect to sunlight, the
    absence of direct sunlight to all habitable rooms does not, in itself, render the
    accommodation unacceptable. Many acceptable dwellings in constrained urban
    environments rely primarily on daylight rather than direct sunlight, and national
    guidance does not require every habitable room to receive direct sun. In this case,
    the evidence before me indicates that the internal environment would be
    comfortably lit and would not appear unduly dim.
13. Outlook from the principal living spaces would be towards the garden. The
    boundary fencing is necessary to secure privacy, but its height is typical of
    residential treatment in Sandbanks and would not create a sense of enclosure that
    would materially harm amenity.
14. In terms of the garden, the development plan does not prescribe minimum garden
    sizes or shapes. It requires only that acceptable living conditions be achieved.
    Although the garden is irregular in shape, its usable area is sufficient for a
    two-bedroom bungalow, and the relationship between the dwelling and its
    boundaries would remain proportionate and comfortable.
15. The overshadowing analysis within the appellant’s submitted assessment indicates
    that more than half of the garden would receive over eight hours of direct sun at
    the equinox. This demonstrates that the external space would be adequate and
    functional, with sufficient sunlight for sitting-out, planting and general domestic
    purposes. The proximity of the garage blocks would not materially impede daylight
    or outlook. Their low height and separation would ensure that the dwelling retains
    adequate access to natural light, and the external environment would not be
    overshadowed to an unacceptable degree.
16. For the reasons above, and on the evidence before me, the dwelling would provide
    satisfactory living conditions for future occupiers, having regard to light, outlook
    and the usability of external space. It therefore complies with Policies PP27 and
    PP37 of the LP and the National Planning Policy Framework (the Framework),
    insofar as they require development to secure appropriate levels of amenity for
    residents.
Flood risk
17. The appeal site lies within Flood Zone 1 and is not identified as being at risk of
    tidal or fluvial flooding under present-day conditions. The Council’s Strategic Flood
    Risk Assessment provides the broader context for understanding how future tidal

      and surface-water conditions may affect the Sandbanks Peninsula, including
      locations where access and egress could be constrained in extreme scenarios.
      Updated climate-change mapping shows that only a narrow strip at the rear of the
      garden would fall within a modelled future flood-risk area, while the dwelling itself
      and the access leading to it lie wholly outside that extent.
18. Policy PP38 of the LP requires a sequential test only where new residential
    development would fall within the Council’s future flood-risk zones. As the future
    affected land is confined to a small part of the garden and does not include any
    built development or the means of access in that area, the Council accepts that a
    sequential test is not required. This establishes that the proposal would be located
    in the least vulnerable part of the site, and there is nothing before me to identify
    any site-specific concern regarding safe access or egress either now or in the
    future.
19. The proposal incorporates proportionate sustainable-drainage measures, including
    a soakaway, silt trap and permeable surfacing, ensuring that surface water is
    managed within the site. As such, the development would not displace floodwater,
    and given its modest scale, it would not materially affect the peninsula’s wider
    flood-risk profile.
20. The Council also raises a peninsula-wide concern. Its technical note applies
    projected tidal levels to existing land heights along Banks Road and indicates that
    the main access route, at around 1.8m AOD, could be inundated by approximately
    200mm during Mean High Water Spring Tide by 2125, with greater depths
    possible during more extreme tidal events. The Council suggests that such
    conditions may become routine rather than exceptional, and that movement to and
    from Sandbanks could be constrained with increasing frequency. The note further
    explains that, although the Shoreline Management Plan identifies a “hold the line”
    position for navigation and property defence, no funded scheme exists for Banks
    Road and any future works would rely on private investment. This evidence
    highlights potential long-term challenges for access to the peninsula as a whole,
    particularly during high-tide conditions and in the absence of confirmed future
    defences.
21. The Council acknowledges that it does not hold detailed tidal modelling for
    Sandbanks and that further clarification of the projected effects is awaited, but the
    available broad projections can still legitimately inform strategic consideration of
    peninsula-wide conditions. Even so, the peninsula-isolation scenario is not a
    mapped constraint and my attention has not been drawn to any adopted policy that
    would prevent development on land that is not itself at risk of flooding.
22. The Framework requires that residual flood risk can be safely managed and that
    safe access and escape routes are available where relevant. These considerations
    fall within Policies F5 and CC3. Policy F5 requires that development be located
    where flood risk is minimised or can be safely managed, including in relation to
    future flood conditions arising from climate-change projections, and that access
    and egress remain safe for the lifetime of the development. Policy CC3 requires
    proposals to take into account the current and potential impacts of climate change,
    ensuring that vulnerability to future tidal or surface-water conditions is avoided or
    can be managed safely.

23. The peninsula-isolation scenario is presented as a potential future condition rather
    than a defined policy requirement or mapped constraint. While the Council’s
    projections may indicate that access to the wider peninsula could become more
    difficult over time, they do not establish that development on land outside the
    modelled flood extent would expose occupiers to unsafe conditions. On the
    evidence before me, the peninsula-wide concern does not amount to a conflict with
    Policy PP38 or the relevant provisions of the Framework.
24. The Council refers to an appeal decision at Banks Road in support of its concerns.
    In that case, the Inspector found the location unsuitable because both the building
    and its only escape route lay within the modelled future flood-risk zone, and
    because no safe means of evacuation could be demonstrated. The Inspector also
    observed that, in extreme tidal conditions, parts of the peninsula could become
    isolated. That decision illustrates how future flood-risk considerations may apply
    where a development and its access fall within the modelled flood extent. The
    circumstances of the present appeal differ materially as the dwelling and its access
    lie outside the modelled future flood-risk area, and the Council’s own mapping
    does not identify any point at which the proposed development or its means of
    access would be affected. The Banks Road decision is therefore not a directly
    comparable scenario and does not alter my judgement on the appeal.
25. Drawing these matters together, the Council’s evidence identifies potential
    long-term challenges for the Sandbanks Peninsula, particularly in relation to Banks
    Road. Those challenges are relevant to understanding the broader context in
    which development proposals must be assessed. However, they do not
    demonstrate that this particular dwelling, or its means of access, would be subject
    to unsafe flood conditions as a result of the development proposed, nor do they
    establish any conflict with the development plan. On the information before me, the
    proposal would be acceptable in flood-risk terms and would accord with Policy
    PP38 of the LP and the relevant provisions of the Framework, which require
    development to be safe, resilient and appropriately drained.
European sites
26. The appeal site lies within the zones of influence for the Dorset Heathlands and
    Poole Harbour, each designated for the international importance of their habitats
    and species. Under Regulation 63 of the Conservation of Habitats and Species
    Regulations 2017 (the Habitats Regulations), the competent authority must be
    certain, beyond reasonable scientific doubt, that a proposal would not adversely
    affect the integrity of any such site, either alone or in combination with other plans
    or projects. That duty applies irrespective of the scale of the development.
27. The Dorset Heathlands SPA comprise an extensive mosaic of lowland heath,
    including dry and wet heath, bog, valley mire and acid grassland. These habitats
    support qualifying bird species such as nightjar, Dartford warbler and woodlark and
    are among the rarest and most sensitive in Europe. They are particularly
    vulnerable to increased recreational pressure, trampling, nutrient enrichment and
    fire risk, all of which arise cumulatively from additional residential development
    within the 400m–5km zone.
28. The Dorset Heathlands Planning Framework Supplementary Planning Document
    identifies these pressures and establishes a strategic mitigation approach,



https://www.gov.uk/planning-inspectorate   5
Appeal Decision 6003539


      including Strategic Access Management and Monitoring (SAMM), which must be
      secured before permission is granted.
29. Poole Harbour is designated as a SPA and Ramsar site for its extensive intertidal
    mudflats, saltmarsh and shallow coastal waters, which support internationally
    important populations of wintering and passage waterbirds including avocet, black-
    tailed godwit, shelduck and little egret. The Harbour is also highly sensitive to
    nutrient loading, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus, which can lead to
    eutrophication and degrade the ecological functioning of the site. Recreational
    disturbance from shoreline activity and water-based recreation is an additional
    recognised pressure.
30. Natural England’s advice confirms that even small-scale residential development
    contributes cumulatively to these impacts and therefore requires mitigation through
    SAMM and other strategic measures.
31. A completed Unilateral Undertaking (UU) has been submitted. It secures the
    Dorset Heathlands SAMMS and the Poole Harbour SAMMS contributions, both
    index-linked and accompanied by the required administrative fees. The Council’s
    solicitor has confirmed receipt of the payment. The UU is enforceable and meets
    the tests in Regulation 122 of the Community Infrastructure Levy Regulations
    2010. I am therefore satisfied that the UU secures adequate mitigation to ensure
    that the proposal would not have a likely significant effect on the Dorset
    Heathlands SPA, or the Poole Harbour SPA and Ramsar site, either alone or in
    combination with other plans or projects.
32. Accordingly, the proposal meets the requirements of Regulation 63 of the Habitats
    Regulations and complies with Policies PP32 and PP39 of the LP, which require
    development to secure appropriate mitigation for recreational and ecological
    pressures so that internationally designated sites are protected and their integrity
    is not adversely affected.
Other Matters
33. Interested parties raise concerns about the access arrangements, focusing
    specifically on the new section of access proposed alongside the retained
    garages. They state that this route would narrow to approximately 2.51 metres and
    argue that such a width would be inadequate for parking or manoeuvring. Although
    the access would be narrow at this point, it would serve a single dwelling and there
    would therefore be no conflict arising from vehicles attempting to pass each other.
    On the evidence before me, the proposed access would operate safely and would
    not give rise to highway safety concerns.
34. Pedestrian access is available along the existing route from the road to the
    garages. Although there would be no dedicated pedestrian path to the site, the
    access already serves residents who walk to and from Marina Court. The modest
    increase in pedestrian activity and vehicle movements associated with one
    additional bungalow would not create an unacceptable risk, and the arrangement
    would remain safe for day-to-day use.
35. The single-storey form of the proposal, together with the proposed boundary
    fencing and the separation distances to neighbouring properties, would be
    sufficient to avoid harmful intervisibility. Interested parties have suggested that car
    headlights would shine into bedrooms at The Peninsula. However, the alignment

      of the access, the position of the dwelling and the intervening boundary treatments
      mean that headlight glare would not be directed towards neighbouring windows in
      a meaningful way. I am satisfied that the development would not result in
      unacceptable overlooking or light intrusion, and that the arrangement would not
      give rise to any planning harm in this respect.
36. Noise and disturbance associated with the use of the access would be modest.
    The proposal would not intensify movements to a degree that would materially
    affect neighbouring living conditions, and the pattern of activity would remain
    consistent with that already experienced at Marina Court.
37. Interested parties raise concerns about biodiversity and soft landscaping, including
    the loss of what they describe as valued green space and the absence of
    increased planting. The site is small and largely enclosed. The proposal includes
    planting and biodiversity net-gain measures proportionate to its scale. The NP
    encourages increased soft landscaping where possible, but it does not prohibit
    development on small plots, and the measures proposed are adequate to ensure
    that the scheme would not harm local biodiversity.
38. Concerns about design quality, sustainability and energy efficiency relate to
    matters that can be addressed through conditions or through subsequent
    building-regulation processes. The absence of solar panels or heat pumps is not a
    reason to withhold permission, and these matters do not weigh against the
    proposal.
39. Concerns about refuse collection on slopes and structural requirements for
    retaining walls relate primarily to building regulation matters and private legal
    responsibilities rather than planning considerations. These matters do not alter the
    planning judgment.
40. Interested parties also raise concerns about drawing accuracy, levels and
    illustrative perspectives. While some drawings are marked “not to scale”, the
    physical inspection of the site provides a reliable basis for assessing the proposal.
    Planning decisions are made on the substance of the development rather than on
    disputed illustrative material, and nothing before me indicates that the drawings
    are so inaccurate as to undermine the assessment.
Conditions
41. The Council has suggested a suite of conditions, and I have amended the wording
    where necessary to ensure precision and enforceability. A standard
    commencement condition is imposed to provide certainty, and a plans condition is
    necessary to define the development and ensure that it proceeds in accordance
    with the approved drawings.
42. To secure biodiversity benefits proportionate to the scale of the development, a
    Biodiversity Enhancement Plan is required before any works of site preparation
    begin. This will ensure that measures such as hedgehog passes and bee bricks
    are incorporated and subsequently maintained. A drainage condition is necessary
    to ensure that the development does not increase flood risk and that foul and
    surface water drainage infrastructure functions effectively for the lifetime of the
    scheme.

43. Details and samples of external materials should be approved before construction
    above damp-course level, in order to secure a satisfactory appearance. Measures
    to provide at least 10% of the predicted future energy use from on-site renewable
    sources should also be agreed and installed prior to occupation, ensuring that the
    development meets the LP’s sustainability requirements.
44. The implementation of landscaping works in accordance with the submitted 30
    year Landscape Management Plan and associated drawing, with ongoing
    management thereafter, is necessary to ensure that the development is softened
    and assimilated into the surrounding residential development. The access and
    parking areas should be laid out in accordance with the approved plans before the
    dwelling is occupied, and retained for their intended purpose, to ensure safe and
    convenient access.
45. It is also necessary to provide secure and covered cycle parking for at least two
    bicycles to support sustainable travel. Any external lighting, including lighting along
    the access route, should be approved before installation and thereafter retained in
    accordance with the approved details, to ensure that lighting does not harm
    residential amenity or biodiversity.
46. Finally, permitted development rights for extensions, roof alterations, outbuildings
    and new openings shall be removed. Given the nature and context of the site,
    such works could have harmful effects on the character of the area, neighbouring
    living conditions or the functioning of the access. It is therefore necessary to retain
    control over future alterations to the building and development within its curtilage.
47. The Council also suggested a condition requiring the installation of a permanent
    vehicle-proof barrier in the entrance to the site access, intended to prevent
    vehicles from using the access as a turning area or obstructing it. The purpose of
    the condition is therefore to control behaviour by third parties rather than to
    mitigate a planning impact arising from the development itself. Any unauthorised
    obstruction or misuse would be a civil matter between the future occupiers and the
    third party. As such, the suggested condition is not necessary to make the
    development acceptable in planning terms, and I have not included it.
Conclusion
48. For the reasons given above, the appeal should be allowed.
