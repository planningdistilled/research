•   The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
    against a refusal to grant planning permission.
•   The appeal is made by Mr Colin Straker (MS2 Property Group Ltd) against the decision of
    North Yorkshire Council.
•   The application Ref is 26/00313/FUL.
•   The development proposed was originally described as “Sui Generis planning application -
    conversion of C3 dwelling house to 7 bed, 9 person C4 HMO”.

Decision
1. The appeal is dismissed.
Preliminary Matters
2. The appeal was submitted in a different name to that given on the planning
   application. Following clarification, it has been confirmed that the appeal should
   proceed in the name of Mr Colin Straker of MS2 Property Group Ltd, and I have
   determined it on that basis.
3. A revised National Planning Policy Framework (the Framework) was published on
   17 August 2026. The appellant has provided additional comments concerning the
   relevance of the revised Framework, and I have taken them into account in
   determining the appeal.
4. The proposal as determined by the Council comprised a 7-bedroom, 9-person
   House in Multiple Occupation (HMO). The appellant subsequently prepared revised
   floor plans, identified as Revision C, showing a 7-bedroom, 7-person HMO, and
   requested that the appeal be determined on that basis. The revisions relate solely
   to the internal arrangement of the accommodation. They do not materially alter the
   nature of the development or affect the external appearance of the building.
   Furthermore, the evidence indicates that the revised layout was considered by the
   Council's Private Housing Standards Team prior to the appeal being submitted. In
   these circumstances, I am satisfied that no party would be prejudiced by my
   consideration of the revised plans. Accordingly, I have determined the appeal on
   the basis of the Revision C plans.
Main Issues
5. The main issues are:
    •    The effect of the development on the living conditions of neighbouring occupiers,
         with particular regard to the relationship between the appeal property and
         neighbouring dwellings, and the resulting activity and disturbance associated
         with the proposed use; and

Reasons
Living conditions
6. Policy DEC4 of the Scarborough Local Plan (2017) (the SLP) seeks to protect the
   living conditions of neighbouring occupiers by ensuring that development does not
   give rise to unacceptable disturbance from noise and other activities. This policy is
   broadly consistent with the Framework’s policies relating to the provision of healthy
   living conditions and quality of life, and I therefore afford the policy full weight.
7. The appeal property is a mid-terraced dwelling within a closely developed
   residential street and has only a modest enclosed rear yard. Although the revised
   plans reduce the proposed occupancy from nine persons to seven, the proposal
   would nevertheless involve occupation by seven unrelated individuals, each with
   their own patterns of movement and daily activity.
8. The Council's HMO Supplementary Planning Document (2015) (SPD) includes
   criteria intended to avoid excessive concentrations of HMOs. Whilst the SPD does
   not form part of the development plan, it provides relevant guidance when
   assessing the likely effect of the proposal on neighbouring living conditions.
9. The evidence before me indicates that the proposal would not exceed the SPD's
   10% threshold for HMOs within a 100-metre radius. However, existing HMOs at 20,
   24 and 44 Gladstone Street mean that the proposal would result in four HMOs
   within a 200-metre street length and would also leave No 42 positioned between
   the appeal property and the existing HMO at No 44. The proposal would therefore
   conflict with the SPD’s guidance relating to localised concentrations of HMOs and
   the “sandwiching” of a dwelling between two HMOs. In this case, the significance of
   that conflict lies in the likely effect on the living conditions of neighbouring
   occupiers, particularly the occupiers of No 42.
10. I have had regard to the appellant’s detailed management plan, which includes
    tenant vetting procedures, inspections, cleaning arrangements, neighbour liaison
    measures and procedures intended to address anti-social behaviour. However, the
    concern is not that future occupiers would behave unreasonably, but that
    occupation by seven unrelated individuals within a constrained terraced property
    would be likely to result in a materially greater level of day-to-day activity than
    would typically arise from occupation as a dwellinghouse.
11. The HMO use, with seven separate individuals sharing the use of the small rear
    yard for refuse disposal, cycle parking and general domestic purposes, would likely
    result in a more intensive use of the outside space than would be the case with a
    single family occupying the property. Taken together with the frequency of arrivals
    and departures, visitors and general comings and goings associated with seven
    separately occupied bedrooms, neighbouring occupiers would be likely to
    experience a noticeable increase in activity, noise and disturbance.
12. Those effects would be experienced most acutely by the occupiers of No 42, who
    would be exposed to activity associated with HMOs on both sides of their property.
    Given the close physical relationship between the terraced properties and their

     small rear yards, such activity would be readily perceptible to the neighbouring
     occupiers. Consequently, the occupiers of No 42 would be subjected to the
     cumulative effects of intensified occupation from both neighbouring properties,
     rather than from a single adjoining HMO. The resulting increase in activity and
     disturbance would materially diminish the quiet enjoyment of No 42 and would
     harm the living conditions of its occupiers.
13. I have carefully considered the appellant's position that any adverse effects could
    be adequately controlled through planning conditions. I have also noted that the
    Council's Environmental Protection officers raised no objection in principle and
    considered that matters relating to sound insulation and internal noise transmission
    could be addressed through conditions. However, the principal concern in this case
    relates to external activity associated with occupation of the property, including
    movements to and from the building and use of the rear yard, which internal sound
    insulation measures would not effectively address. In addition, whilst conditions
    could secure implementation of the submitted management plan, this would not
    realistically prevent or materially reduce the ordinary domestic activities that would
    give rise to the harm identified. Furthermore, notwithstanding the revisions made to
    the appeal scheme, the submitted plans show several bedrooms of a size capable
    of accommodating more than one occupant. In those circumstances, it would be
    difficult to ascertain through routine observation whether occupation remained
    within any imposed maximum occupancy limit. Consequently, any condition
    intended to restrict occupation to seven persons would be difficult to monitor and
    enforce effectively in practice. I am therefore not persuaded that the imposition of
    conditions would overcome the identified harm to neighbouring living conditions.
14. For these reasons, I conclude that the development would result in unacceptable
    harm to the living conditions of neighbouring occupiers through increased activity,
    noise and disturbance, particularly for the occupiers of 42 Gladstone Street, who
    would be exposed to the cumulative effects of intensified occupation from both
    neighbouring properties. Accordingly, the proposal would conflict with Policy DEC4
    of the SLP.
Parking
15. The appeal site contains no off-street parking and is located on a street subject to
    parking controls. The evidence before me, including representations from local
    residents, indicates that on-street parking within the surrounding area is limited and
    heavily used. That is consistent with my own observations of the area.
16. The appellant relies on experience from other HMOs in their portfolio and contends
    that car ownership amongst occupants would be low. However, limited evidence
    has been provided to demonstrate that those properties occupy comparable
    locations, or that similarly low levels of car ownership would be likely to arise in this
    case. Consequently, I attach limited weight to that evidence.
17. The plans refer to secure and covered cycle storage within the rear yard. However,
    there is little detail as to how those facilities would be enclosed or covered in
    practice. Moreover, only five cycle spaces are shown for a seven-bedroom HMO
    and limited information has been provided regarding their practical operation within
    the constrained rear yard. Taken together, the evidence does not satisfactorily
    demonstrate that cycling would provide a realistic substitute for private car use for
    all occupiers. Whilst the appellant intends to discourage private vehicle ownership

     through tenancy arrangements, there is no planning mechanism before me that
     would prevent future occupiers from using private vehicles and obtaining parking
     permits. Thus, in the absence of convincing evidence to the contrary, I cannot
     reasonably conclude that the development would operate with exceptionally low
     levels of car ownership or use.
18. In these circumstances, it is reasonable to expect that the development would
    generate additional parking demand. In a location where available parking is
    already limited and heavily used, that demand would likely exacerbate parking
    stress within the surrounding area.
19. I acknowledge that the site occupies an accessible location close to services,
    facilities and public transport. Nevertheless, Policy DEC1 requires development to
    provide suitable parking arrangements, including for cyclists. For the reasons
    above, I am not satisfied that the proposal demonstrates suitable parking
    arrangements for vehicles or cycles, or that it would not add to existing parking
    stress within the area.
20. Thus, the proposal would conflict with Policy DEC1 of the SLP. This policy is
    broadly consistent with the Framework’s policies relating to suitable and
    sustainable transport arrangements, and I therefore afford the policy full weight.
Other Matters
21. The appellant points out that the property could potentially be occupied as a
    six-person HMO without the need for planning permission. I consider that to
    represent a realistic fallback position and I have taken it into account. Whilst such
    occupation could result in some increase in activity and demand for on-street
    parking, the appeal proposal would nevertheless represent a more intensive form of
    occupation. On the evidence before me, I am not satisfied that the impacts of the
    appeal proposal would be equivalent or less than those likely to arise from the
    fallback position. Accordingly, the fallback position attracts limited weight and is
    insufficient to alter my findings on the main issues.
Planning Balance and Conclusion
22. Policy S3 of the Framework states that decisions on development proposals should
    apply a presumption in favour of sustainable development. In this case, the
    development would be located within a settlement, where Policy S4 of the
    Framework states that development proposals should be approved unless the
    benefits of doing so would be substantially outweighed by adverse effects when
    assessed against the national decision-making policies of the Framework.
23. The proposal would make use of an existing building and would contribute, albeit
    modestly, to housing supply and choice within a location close to services, facilities
    and employment opportunities. These are benefits which attract weight in favour of
    the proposal and align with the Framework’s policies relating to the effective use of
    land and buildings, the delivery and choice of homes, and development in
    accessible locations. However, the development is modest in scale and, whilst the
    appellant indicates that the accommodation would be targeted towards working
    professionals and key workers, limited evidence has been provided to demonstrate
    a specific unmet need for that form of accommodation or the extent to which the
    proposal would address it. Furthermore, I have attached weight to the realistic
    fallback position that the property could operate as a six-person HMO without

     planning permission, meaning that some of the accommodation and
     housing-choice benefits could arise irrespective of the appeal proposal.
     Accordingly, I attach moderate weight to the benefits of the proposal.
24. Against those benefits, I have found that the development would result in
    unacceptable harm to the living conditions of neighbouring occupiers, particularly at
    No 42, and that suitable parking arrangements have not been demonstrated. These
    adverse effects are inconsistent with the Framework's policies relating to the
    creation of well-designed places, the provision of healthy living conditions and
    quality of life, and the provision of suitable and sustainable transport arrangements.
    I attach significant weight to the identified harm to neighbouring living conditions
    and moderate weight to the parking harm.
25. Drawing these matters together, and having regard to the Framework's national
    decision-making policies as a whole, I find that the adverse effects of the proposal
    would substantially outweigh its benefits. Consequently, the proposal fails to satisfy
    Policy S4 of the Framework.
26. Furthermore, for the reasons given above, the proposal would conflict with
    Policies DEC1 and DEC4 of the SLP and therefore with the development plan
    taken as a whole. There are no material considerations, including the Framework
    and the fallback position, that indicate a decision should be made other than in
    accordance with the development plan. Therefore, the appeal is dismissed.
