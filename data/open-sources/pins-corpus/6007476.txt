Appeal Decision
Site visit made on 14 August 2026
by Elaine Moulton BA (Hons) BPl MRTPI
an Inspector appointed by the Secretary of State
Decision date: 22 September 2026


Appeal Ref: 6007476
Low Flatt Farm, Heads Lane, Bolsterstone, Sheffield S36 3ZF
•    The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
     against a grant of planning permission subject to conditions.
•    The appeal is made by Mr P Justice against the decision of Peak District National Park Authority.
•    The application Ref NP/S/1025/1079 was approved on 10 February 2026 and planning permission
     was granted subject to conditions.
•    The development permitted is described as ‘S.73 application for the variation of conditions 2 and 3
     on NP/S/1024/1107’.
•    The condition in dispute is No 3 which states that:
     Notwithstanding the approved plans, no planning permission is hereby granted for the ground floor
     window opening (or any associated alterations) to the south elevation of the barn which shall be
     omitted from the scheme.
•    The reason given for the condition is:
     The proposed ground floor window would have an unacceptable harmful impact upon the
     significance of the property as a non-designated heritage asset. This harm is given great weight
     within the National Park and is not outweighed by any public benefits of allowing the proposal.


Decision
1.     The appeal is dismissed.
Preliminary Matter
2.     A new National Planning Policy Framework (the Framework) was published on
       17 August 2026 during the appeal process. However, as the policy content of the
       Framework, insofar as it relates to this case, has not significantly changed, there is
       no requirement for me to seek further submissions from the main parties. I am
       satisfied no party would be prejudiced if I determine the appeal accordingly.
Background and Main Issue
3.     Planning permission was granted in 2025, subject to conditions, for the conversion
       of the appeal building, a barn, to a residential annex. A subsequent application
       was submitted under section 73 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (the
       Act) seeking to vary condition 2 of the planning permission, which listed the
       approved plans. The application proposed alterations to an existing first-floor
       window and the insertion of a new ground-floor window, both on the south
       elevation. The Authority accepted the alterations to the first-floor window and
       approved the revised plans. However, it imposed a new condition, condition 3,
       preventing the insertion of the ground-floor window shown on those plans on the
       basis that it would harm a non-designated heritage asset. The appeal seeks the
       removal of this condition.

4.     The main issue is the effect of removing condition 3, and the resulting insertion of
       a ground-floor window in the south elevation, on the character and appearance of
       the barn and on the cultural heritage of the Peak District National Park (PDNP).
Reasons
5.     The appeal building is a traditional gritstone barn with a slate roof dating from at
       least the 19th century. It is attached to a farmhouse, forming a L-shape around a
       yard. The evidence before me suggests that the building has been adapted over
       time; however, those alterations have been consistent with its continued
       agricultural use.
6.     The appeal site is within the Peak District National Park (PDNP), a Protected
       Landscape. Policy N4 of the Framework states that development proposals within
       Protected Landscapes should be limited in scale and extent and sensitively
       located and designed to avoid harm to their statutory purposes and special
       qualities. It further makes clear that substantial weight should be placed on the
       importance of conserving and enhancing the natural beauty of these areas, and to
       conserving and enhancing wildlife and cultural heritage National Parks.
7.     The Authority considers that the appeal building is a non-designated heritage
       asset (NDHA), a matter which is not disputed by the appellant. The evidence
       before me supports that conclusion and, in reaching my decision, I have treated it
       as such. The barn is of moderate local significance as a NDHA, in part, derived
       from its traditional agricultural architecture, its surviving fabric, and its functional
       and visual relationship with the farmhouse, all of which contribute to the cultural
       heritage of the PDNP.
8.     The removal of the condition would permit the insertion of the opening within the
       south elevation to serve the lounge included within the permitted residential
       conversion. Although designed with a vertical emphasis, the opening would appear
       at odds with the building’s historic agricultural character. It neither reflects the
       barn’s former agricultural function nor respects the size, form or positioning of the
       existing openings within the barn.
9.     Consequently, whilst the ratio of solid to void within the elevation would remain
       relatively high, and the building’s relationship to the farmhouse would be largely
       unchanged, the proposed opening would erode the building’s agricultural identity
       and diminish the legibility of its historic use. This would result in harm to the
       significance of the NDHA, although such harm would be limited in extent given the
       small scale of the proposed alterations. The proposed shutters could lessen the
       domestic appearance of the opening when closed, however, they would not alter
       its form, position or visual impact. Moreover, given its elevated position above the
       adjoining ground level, they would be unlikely to be used regularly. Therefore, they
       would not overcome the harm, even if appropriately designed.
10. I acknowledge that the opening would have a limited effect on the wider landscape
    quality of the PDNP, as the south gable is not visible from Heads Lane and
    visibility from other public views is limited due to the distance from which it is seen
    and the intervening topography. Nevertheless, the importance of conserving
    cultural heritage is given substantial weight in the Framework, and, for the above
    reasons, the proposal would not achieve that.

11. I accept the importance of natural light and sunlight to health and wellbeing and
    acknowledge that, without the proposed new opening, the lounge would rely upon
    two relatively modest windows. Consequently, it would receive limited natural light.
    However, there is insufficient evidence before me to demonstrate that the
    approved layout has been dictated by the constraints of the historic building, rather
    than the building being altered to accommodate a preferred layout. Nor is there
    persuasive evidence that the proposed opening is necessary to secure an
    appropriate and viable conversion. Consequently, the proposal conflicts with SPD1
    guidance, which seeks to ensure that new uses respond to the character, form and
    function of historic buildings.
12. The appellant has referred me to an example of a barn conversion included within
    the SPD, where new openings have been accepted. However, the cited example
    relates to a different building type and context and, therefore, does not alter my
    assessment that the proposed opening would harm the agricultural character of
    this barn and the significance of the NDHA even though it is more prominent in
    public views than the appeal building.
13. Policy HE7.2 of the Framework indicates that where a development proposal
    would harm the significance of a NDHA, that harm should be weighed against the
    benefits of the proposal and a balanced judgement made, having regard to the
    scale of any harm or loss and the significance of the NDHA.
14. The proposal is associated with a new use for the barn and thereby would
    contribute towards securing the building’s ongoing use. However, notwithstanding
    the appellant’s assertion that the investment required to convert the building would
    not be undertaken if the resulting accommodation failed to meet their needs, there
    is no compelling evidence that it is currently at risk and requires immediate
    investment. Therefore, it has not been demonstrated that the future conservation
    of the building depends upon the proposed opening. Nor has it been demonstrated
    that less harmful alternatives have been adequately explored.
15. Consequently, the benefits of the proposal attract only limited weight. Such
    benefits do not outweigh the identified harm to an NDHA, of moderate local
    significance. Furthermore, it would conflict with the Framework which states that
    substantial weight should be placed on the importance of conserving the cultural
    heritage of the PDNP.
16. I therefore find that the insertion of the ground-floor window in the south elevation
    would result in harm to the character and appearance of the barn, a NDHA, and to
    the cultural heritage of the PDNP. The condition is therefore reasonable and
    necessary in order to avoid such harm.
Conclusion
17. For the reasons set out above, I conclude that the disputed condition is reasonable
    and necessary, and as such the appeal should be dismissed.
