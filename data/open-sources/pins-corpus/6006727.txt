Appeal Ref: 6006727
271 Forest Road, Walthamstow, Waltham Forest E17 6HD
•   The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended) (the
    Act) against a refusal to grant planning permission.
•   The appeal is made by Mr Lingamoorthy Iyar Loganatha Sarma against the decision of the Council of
    London Borough of Waltham Forest.
•   The application Ref is 252833.
•   The development proposed is originally described as “retrospective application for retention of rear
    extension extended to approved extension (15 years old). Altered roof form (eave height not
    changed) and replacement of external timber cladding with brick at the existing place of worship”.

Decision
1. The appeal is dismissed.
Preliminary Matters
2. The appellant has submitted another appeal concerning an enforcement notice
   issued against the development. That appeal is the subject of a separate decision.
3. An additional Shadow Study has been submitted with the appeal. In deciding
   whether to accept this document, I have had regard to the Procedural Guide:
   Planning Appeals – England (2026), which states that the appeal process should
   not be used to evolve a scheme and that what is considered at appeal should be
   essentially the same proposal as that which was before the Council and interested
   parties at the application stage. In this instance, the Shadow Study provides
   supplementary information relevant to one of the main issues, rather than
   constituting a design evolution, and it does not materially alter the scheme. The
   Council and interested parties have had the opportunity to comment on the
   additional information as part of the appeal process. I am therefore satisfied that no
   party would be prejudiced by its acceptance, and I have determined the appeal on
   this basis.
4. The application form indicates that the development has already been carried out.
   During my site visit, I saw that the rear extension had been substantially completed.
   However, the development does not appear to correspond fully with the submitted
   plans. There is also a dispute between the parties regarding the height of the
   extension. The appellant also states that the flues and extraction system shown on
   the plans would be subject to a separate application for a lawful development
   certificate and do not form part of this application. For clarity and the avoidance of
   doubt, my assessment is based on the plans before me, rather than what has been
   constructed to date. Any works carried out that are not in accordance with those
   plans would be a matter between the Council and the appellant.

5. I have been advised that the appeal property benefits from a planning permission
   granted for a single-storey rear extension in the same location. Although only
   limited information, including plans and drawings of the previous approval, has
   been provided, there is no dispute that the extension has not been built fully in
   accordance with the previous approval, including in respect of its depth.
6. The appellant also asserts that a single-storey extension has been erected on site
   for over 15 years, and has submitted historic aerial photographs, an affidavit, a
   statutory declaration and witness statements in support of this claim. It is clear that
   a rear extension broadly resembling that shown on the submitted “pre-existing”
   plans existed before the development. However, it is not within my remit, in an
   appeal under section 78 of the Act, to determine whether or not that “pre-existing”
   extension is lawful. It is open to the appellant to apply to the Council for a separate
   lawful development certificate, regardless of the outcome of this appeal.
7. Prior to the determination of this appeal, a revised version of the National Planning
   Policy Framework (the Framework) was published on 17 August 2026. The
   changes made to the Framework, insofar as they relate to the substantive matters
   in this appeal, do not fundamentally alter the relevant policy position. I am therefore
   satisfied that neither party would be prejudiced by my consideration of the revised
   version of the Framework in reaching my decision. References hereafter are to the
   revised version of the Framework.
Main Issues
8. The main issues are the effects of the development on:
          •    the character and appearance of the host building and surrounding area,
               including its effect on the significance of the host building as a non-
               designated heritage asset; and
          •    the living conditions of the neighbouring occupiers at 7 and 8 Rennes Road
               and 269 and 273 Forest Road, with regard to outlook, daylight and sunlight.
Reasons
Character and appearance
9. The appeal property is a three-storey terraced building that is currently in use as a
   Hindu Temple. The extension occupies the entire rear garden and is built up to the
   rear boundary wall. Its arched roof runs almost the full depth of the extension and
   rises to approximately the height of the first-floor rear window of the existing
   outrigger, appearing noticeably higher than the surrounding boundary walls. The
   external materials used generally match those of the host building, and the arched
   roof form is set in from the eaves with sloping sides. Nevertheless, the overall
   depth of the rear extension, together with its tall roof form, creates a boxy extension
   of substantial mass that appears as an incongruous addition which dominates the
   rear elevation of the host building.
10. While the extension is screened from public views from the street, and other
    properties in the terrace have rear additions of varied built forms, I did not observe
    any rear extensions of comparable depth and height during my site visit. The
    extension therefore appears as an incongruous addition to the host property at
    odds with the established pattern of development in the wider terrace.

11. The appellant contends that the development replaces an existing single-storey
    rear extension of comparable scale and form, which had existed for over 15 years.
    Historic photographs provided by the appellant show that a single-storey rear
    extension existed before the development. However, it appears to have had a roof
    form with greater set-ins from the eaves and side boundaries. Irrespective of the
    lawfulness of the previous extension, the current scheme presents a significant
    increase in massing.
12. The Council has confirmed that the appeal property is included on its published
    Local Heritage List (2024). The Local Heritage List entry identifies that the building
    has clear architectural and cultural interest, with a temple frontage to Forest Road
    featuring classical decoration and a depiction of the goddess above the entrance. It
    is therefore considered as a non-designated heritage asset (NDHA).
13. Policy HE7 of the Framework states that where a development proposal would
    harm the significance of a NDHA, this should be weighed against the benefits of the
    proposal and a balanced judgement made, having regard to the scale of any harm
    or loss and the significance of the NDHA.
14. The development does not alter the principal elevation, such that the architectural
    features of the temple frontage from which the significance of the NDHA primarily
    derives are preserved. Although the rear extension changes the building form and
    the overall appearance of the property when viewed from the rear, this rear view is
    experienced separately from the principal frontage. Its effect on the host building is
    therefore not at a level that would harm the overall integrity of the NDHA or erode
    its architectural and cultural interest. Consequently, I find that the development has
    no harmful effect on the significance of the NDHA.
15. Overall, although there is no harm to the significance of the NDHA, the
    development has an unacceptable effect on the character and appearance of the
    host building and surrounding area. Accordingly, it conflicts with the relevant
    provisions of Policy 53 of the Waltham Forest Local Plan Part 1: Shaping the
    Borough 2020-2035 (adopted in 2024) (the LP), which seeks, amongst other things,
    to deliver development of high-quality design that reinforces and/or enhances local
    character and distinctiveness, and responds appropriately to its context in terms of
    scale, height and massing.
Living conditions
16. Although the development replaces a previous single-storey rear extension, the
    current roof form is larger and sits closer to its eaves and shared side boundaries
    with 7 and 8 Rennes Road (Nos 7 and 8) and 273 Forest Road (No 273).
    Consequently, when viewed from these neighbouring properties, its substantial built
    form appears bulky and visually dominant, with limited visual relief. Given the
    narrow depth of the adjacent rear gardens of Nos 7 and 8, the close proximity of
    the large extension, the protruding roof form and the tall rear wall along their shared
    boundaries, the sense of enclosure experienced by these occupiers in their
    gardens is exacerbated. The development is unduly overbearing and oppressive for
    the occupiers of Nos 7 and 8 and unacceptably affects their outlook, to the
    detriment of their living conditions.

17. The separation distance, oblique viewing angle and sloped roof pitch recessed from
    the eaves provide some reduction in bulk when viewed from the first-floor rear
    windows of 269 Forest Road (No 269), where these occupiers continue to enjoy an
    overall adequate level of outlook over their rear side space and garden. While the
    development appears more prominent and overbearing when seen from the closest
    first-floor window on the outrigger of No 273, due to its closer proximity to the
    shared boundary, the roof of the extension does not exceed the full height of this
    window and its primary direct views into the rear gardens remain largely unaffected.
    Although the tall roof form is inevitably visible from this window in sideward views, it
    is not of a scale that would restrict the outlook of the occupiers of No 273 to a
    harmful level.
18. Nos 269 and 273 have ground-floor commercial units and there is no evidence
    before me to suggest that their rear gardens are used as amenity spaces for the
    occupiers of the upper-floor flats. In any event, due to its position to the side of the
    development, the rear garden of No 269 is relatively generous and open, and the
    development does not significantly increase the sense of enclosure to an
    unacceptable level. The rear space of No 273 currently has limited open views
    towards the appeal property, and thus it is unlikely to experience a material loss of
    outlook as a result of the development.
19. Regarding daylight and sunlight, the model used in the submitted Shadow Study
    does not appear to have taken into account the existing buildings along Forest
    Road, including the host building, or other boundary treatments within the vicinity of
    the site. Although the study does not quantify the level of change, such as by
    percentage, it nevertheless indicates that the shadows projected onto neighbouring
    gardens, in particular Nos 7 and 8, would be greater on selected dates when
    compared with the previously built extension. However, it does not explain or justify
    why that increased overshadowing would be acceptable. Given these concerns
    regarding its accuracy, together with the absence of any justification for the
    increased overshadowing shown, the Shadow Study fails to adequately
    demonstrate that the level of change or loss in sunlight received by the
    neighbouring rear gardens as a result of the development would be acceptable.
20. Moreover, given its depth and height relative to other rear additions within the wider
    terrace, its position to the south of Nos 7 and 8, and its proximity to the closest first-
    floor window of No 273, the development has the potential to affect the daylight and
    sunlight received by these neighbouring properties. In the absence of clear
    evidence to demonstrate otherwise, I am not satisfied that the development would
    not result in a material loss of daylight and sunlight for neighbouring occupiers.
21. Given the reasons above, the development has a harmful effect on the living
    conditions of the neighbouring occupiers, with regard to outlook, daylight and
    sunlight. Accordingly, it would therefore conflict with Policy 57 of the LP, which
    seeks, amongst other considerations, to ensure that development respects the
    amenity of neighbours by avoiding harmful impacts arising from enclosure, and loss
    of outlook, daylight and sunlight.
Other Matters
22. The appellant is concerned that photographs submitted with neighbours’
    representations would not constitute reliable evidence. My assessment has been
    made having regard to all the submitted evidence and my own observations on site.

23. With regard to noise and disturbance arising from the construction and operation of
    the temple, the Council has raised no objection. Any construction-related disruption
    would, in all likelihood, be temporary and limited in duration. As the development
    relates to an existing temple, and there is no substantive evidence before me to
    indicate that unacceptable noise or disturbance has arisen, or would arise, as a
    result of the extension, I have no reason to conclude that it would cause
    unacceptable harm in this respect.
24. Disputes about the boundary wall with neighbours are private matters between the
    relevant parties and are not within my jurisdiction. Allegations of discrimination and
    harassment are also not for me to determine in this appeal. Accordingly, these
    matters have not had any material bearing on my assessment of the planning
    issues of the appeal.
Planning Balance
25. I have found that the development causes harm to the character and appearance of
    the host building and the surrounding area. It also causes harm to the living
    conditions of the neighbouring occupiers.
26. I have had due regard to the Public Sector Equality Duty (PSED) in section 149 of
    the Equality Act 2010. The PSED requires decision-makers, in exercising their
    functions, to have due regard to the need to eliminate unlawful discrimination,
    harassment and victimisation, and to advance equality of opportunity, and to foster
    good relations between people who share a protected characteristic and people
    who do not share it. The appellant is a member of the Hindu community, and the
    host building is currently in use as a Hindu Temple. Religion or belief is a protected
    characteristic. The PSED is therefore engaged, but it does not follow that the
    appeal must succeed on that basis.
27. It is a positive consideration that the existing place of worship provides a
    community facility for a particular religious group in the area, and that the
    development provides additional space for its religious activities. I also recognise
    that, as an extension to a community facility, the development has the potential to
    support social cohesion, cultural diversity and community life, albeit to a modest
    degree given its relatively small scale.
28. Nevertheless, the place of worship has operated for a number of years, and there is
    no substantive evidence before me to indicate that the existing use could not
    continue without the extension, or that there are no alternative ways to extend or
    modernise the property without causing harm. It therefore follows that the
    circumstances of the Hindu community at this Hindu Temple cannot be a decisive
    consideration in this case. Therefore, I find that dismissing the appeal would not
    amount to unlawful discrimination, and a refusal of planning permission for this
    unacceptable development would be essential to advance equality of opportunity
    and foster good relations between people who share a protected characteristic and
    people who do not share it.
29. Similarly, I have considered the rights of the appellant under Article 9 of the Human
    Rights Act 1998 (the HRA), which affords the right to freedom of thought,
    conscience and religion. It is a qualified right, and interference may be justified
    where that is lawful and in the public interest. The concept of proportionality is
    crucial.

30. Dismissing the appeal would interfere with the appellant’s rights under the HRA.
    However, the interference would be in accordance with the law and in pursuance of
    well-established and legitimate aims in the public interest, namely protecting the
    character and appearance of the area and the living conditions of neighbouring
    occupiers. I therefore find in this case that the interference would be necessary and
    proportionate, and it would not amount to a violation of the appellant’s rights.
Conclusion
31. The development conflicts with the development plan when read as a whole and
    that there are no material considerations that indicate that the development should
    be determined other than in accordance with it. Therefore, the appeal should be
    dismissed.
