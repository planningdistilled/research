Appeal Decision
Site visit held on 29 June 2026
by M Clowes BA (Hons) MCD PG CERT (Arch Con) MRTPI
an Inspector appointed by the Secretary of State
Decision date: 17 August 2026


Appeal Ref: 6009542
Redundant Barn, Kings Hill, Brassington DE4 4NT
• The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
  against a refusal to grant planning permission.
• The appeal is made by Mr and Mrs D Webster against the decision of Derbyshire Dales District
  Council.
• The application Ref is 25/00700/FUL.
• The development proposed is demolition of redundant building and erection of new dwelling.

Decision
1.     The appeal is dismissed.
Preliminary Matters
2.     A Bat Roost Appraisal Survey Report was submitted with the appellant’s final
       comments. This document was not part of the original planning submission, and
       the Council has not had opportunity to consider it. Whilst understanding that such
       surveys can only be carried out at certain times of the year, the late submission of
       new evidence would be prejudicial to the interests of the Council and other
       interested parties. There is no clear exceptional reason why it could not have been
       provided earlier in the appeal process, and as a result, it has not been taken into
       account in my decision.
Main Issues
3.     The main issues in this appeal are;
       i)     the effect of the proposal on the significance of a non-designated heritage
              asset (NDHA) and whether the proposal would preserve or enhance the
              character or appearance of the Brassington Conservation Area (BCA).
       ii)    highway safety, with particular regard to intensification of use and visibility
              splays; and
       iii)   the effect of the proposed development upon protected species.
Reasons
The Effect on Heritage Assets
Significance
4.     The significance of the BCA lies in its layered architectural history. It is an intact
       example of an evolved historic townscape that has grown organically from
       medieval times, but particularly during the 17th and 18th centuries, with a strong

Appeal Decision 6009542


       association with lead mining and agriculture. Small farms and smallholdings are
       juxtaposed with grander houses and landmark buildings. Streets often exhibit a
       strong sense of enclosure, and the consistent use of limestone is an important
       feature. It has a good survival of original farm groups and a rich architectural
       quality. Sitting under a limestone plateau and on rising land above gently
       undulating pasture, it has a strong visual and cultural association with its rural
       landscape setting.
5.     The Council identifies the barns within the appeal site as a NDHA, and the
       appellant does not dispute that the buildings possess some local historic interest.
       While there is no statutory listing process for NDHA’s, the Planning Practice
       Guidance explains that they are buildings, sites or areas identified by plan making
       bodies as having heritage significance meriting consideration in planning
       decisions, but which do not meet the criteria for designated heritage assets.
6.     Based on the evidence before me, including my observations during my visit, I
       consider that the appeal buildings should be regarded as a NDHA. The appellant
       suggests that the building type is not rare, has been altered and suffers partial
       structural decline and is functionally obsolete, such that it should be considered a
       heritage asset of relatively modest significance. The submitted Heritage Statement
       similarly concludes that the building possesses archaeological, architectural and
       historic interest, but assesses it as being of only medium importance as a NDHA,
       and potentially of low importance owing to alteration, deterioration and its current
       condition. I disagree.
7.     The appeal site comprises 19th-century former agricultural buildings. Significance
       derives principally from their evidential, historical and aesthetic value as a
       surviving component of Brassington’s agricultural past, illustrating former farming
       practices and the village’s historic relationship with agriculture. The buildings also
       contribute to the character and appearance of the BCA through their traditional
       form, materials and roadside position. In particular, their alignment closely follows
       the curve of Kings Hill and contributes to the enclosed character of the lane, a
       positive characteristic specifically identified within the BCAA. Together with the
       associated historic plot, the buildings form part of the surviving agricultural
       character of this part of the village and contribute to the understanding and
       appreciation of Brassington’s historic development.
8.     Despite some alteration, the buildings have retained much of their traditional
       character and appearance, particularly the larger barn. Whilst the Heritage
       Statement refers to deterioration, a collapsed roof section, decaying timber
       elements and some evidence of movement, it does not conclude that the buildings
       are structurally unsound or incapable of repair. Nor does it contain a structural
       assessment demonstrating that demolition is the only reasonable option available.
9.     Due to their age, architectural form and position, I find that the appeal buildings
       possess appreciable heritage significance as a NDHA and make an important and
       positive contribution to the character and appearance of the BCA.
Impact of the development
10. The proposal seeks to demolish the existing barns to accommodate vehicular
    parking for a 2-storey dwelling that would be set back into the plot away from the
    road frontage. Whilst some of the supporting documentation describes the
    retention of part of the smaller barn as a carport/store, the proposal would

https://www.gov.uk/planning-inspectorate   2
Appeal Decision 6009542


       nevertheless result in the loss of the principal barn and the substantial loss of the
       historic farm group. The demolition of the barns would result in an almost total loss
       of the physical fabric and remove a traditional pattern of development along the
       road frontage of Kings Hill. None of the significance derived from their surviving
       historic fabric, agricultural form, evidential value or contribution to the
       understanding of the historic development of Brassington would be preserved.
11. The BCAA identifies the loss of agricultural character as a threat to the BCA and
    repeatedly emphasises the importance of surviving farm buildings and agricultural
    structures to the area’s character and significance. The loss of a range of
    traditional agricultural barns would therefore contribute to the further harmful
    erosion of the BCA’s agricultural character. While the loss would only be
    perceptible in localised views, the proposal could not be said to preserve or
    enhance the character or appearance of the BCA as a result.
12. The Heritage Statement suggests that, in the absence of the proposed
    development, the barns would continue to deteriorate and that a viable use for the
    buildings in their current form is unlikely to be found. However, no viability
    assessment, marketing evidence, structural appraisal or options analysis has been
    provided to support that conclusion. Consequently, there is little evidence to
    demonstrate that the retention, repair or alternative reuse of the building has been
    adequately explored.
13. I further note that the Georgian Group, while referring to a previous planning
    proposal for the conversion of the buildings to a dwelling, suggests that it was
    refused only on the basis of highway safety concerns and not because the building
    itself was unsuitable for conversion to a new use. The lack of an identified
    alternative use does not demonstrate that one does not exist, nor does it justify the
    demolition of a heritage asset without robust evidence that retention and reuse
    options have been fully explored. It is not for the Council to find a realistic viable
    alternative future for the retained building. It may, however be, that the benefits of
    restoring and re-using the NDHA need to be balanced against any planning harms
    arising from alternative proposals.
14. The BCAA identifies the loss of open land, crofts, paddocks and gardens through
    infill development as a negative factor within the BCA and emphasises the
    importance of retaining remaining open spaces and views that maintain the
    connection between the village and its rural setting, a matter that I have identified
    above as contributing to the significance of the BCA.
15. The proposed introduction of a new dwelling within the undeveloped plot to the
    rear of the existing barns, even if well designed and constructed from appropriate
    materials, would result in the development of land that has historically remained
    open and undeveloped. Its siting behind a frontage parking and garden area would
    give rise to a suburban character that would appear at odds with the historic
    pattern of development in this part of the village. The proposal would therefore
    result in an incongruous form of development that would further diminish the
    characteristics that contribute to the significance of the BCA. The demolition of the
    barns and erection of the dwelling would be permanent, such that the harm would
    endure for the lifetime of the development.
16. The appellant argues that planning policy should not be interpreted in a manner
    that sterilises constrained rural sites and that a contradiction arises because


https://www.gov.uk/planning-inspectorate   3
Appeal Decision 6009542


       retention of the barns constrains the ability to improve access arrangements. I do
       not agree. National and local planning policy do not seek to prevent the beneficial
       use, adaptation or reuse of historic rural sites. Rather, they seek to ensure that
       change is undertaken in a manner that conserves heritage significance. The
       existence of site constraints does not, in itself, justify the loss of a heritage asset.
       Nor does the fact that demolition may facilitate an alternative form of development
       demonstrate that such demolition is acceptable. The proposal would result in the
       loss of the majority of the heritage asset and therefore cannot reasonably be
       described as a sensitive or proportionate response to the site's constraints.
17. The deliberate or continuing neglect of a heritage asset cannot reasonably justify
    development that would otherwise result in unacceptable harm to its significance.
    The possibility that a building may continue to deteriorate if permission is refused
    is not, in itself, a convincing justification for its demolition.
18. The submitted Heritage Statement accepts that the proposal would result in a
    major impact upon the significance of the NDHA through the loss of historic fabric.
    Whilst it suggests this harm is justified by securing a viable long-term use, I am not
    persuaded for the reasons set out above, that the evidence adequately
    demonstrates that demolition is necessary to secure the future of the site.
Conclusion – First Main Issue
19. I find that the proposal would result in harm of very considerable magnitude to the
    significance of the NDHA from its near total loss. The proposal would also fail to
    preserve the character and appearance of the BCA, through the erosion of
    agricultural character, the loss of an important roadside historic building, and the
    introduction of domestic development onto a historically undeveloped plot.
20. The proposal would conflict with Policies S4, PD1, and PD2 of the adopted
    Derbyshire Dales Local Plan (DDLP) 2017. Together these policies seek to protect
    and where possible, enhance the landscape’s intrinsic character and
    distinctiveness, including conserving heritage assets in a manner appropriate to
    their significance.
Public Benefits and Heritage Balance
21. Paragraph 216 of the Framework states that the effect of an application on the
    significance of a NDHA should be taken into account in determining the proposal.
    In respect of proposals that directly affect NDHA’s, a balanced judgement is
    required having regard to the scale of any harm or loss and the significance of the
    heritage asset. In this case, the proposal would result in the near-total loss of the
    NDHA and therefore harm of very considerable magnitude. I attach substantial
    weight to the loss of the NDHA, having regard to both its significance and the
    extent of its demolition.
22. Paragraph 212 of the Framework advises that when considering the impact of
    development on the significance of designated heritage assets such as the BCA,
    great weight should be given to the assets’ conservation. That is irrespective of the
    magnitude of that harm.
23. The degree of harm to the significance of the BCA would in terms of the
    Framework, be towards the middle of the spectrum of less than substantial harm.
    However, this harm should not be equated with a less than substantial objection

Appeal Decision 6009542


         and is of considerable importance and weight. Under such circumstances,
         paragraph 215 advises that this harm should be weighed against the public
         benefits of the proposal.
24. I have considered the appellant's case that the development would deliver a new
    dwelling, securing a viable use of the appeal site. In this regard, it would make a
    contribution to local housing land supply which would in turn contribute to the
    social and economic objectives of the Framework through the construction and
    occupation of the dwelling. The re-use of salvaged local materials whilst
    commendable, would be at the expense of the almost total loss of a NDHA, for
    which it has not been demonstrated that repair and reuse would not be feasible.
25. Notwithstanding the above, there is no evidence before me that the Council cannot
    currently demonstrate a 5-year supply of deliverable housing sites, or that there is
    an identified unmet need for self-build or custom-build housing which this proposal
    would address. It is unclear whether the appellant is on the Council’s self and
    custom build housing register, but in any case, there is no mechanism such as a
    S106 legal agreement1 before me, to secure the dwelling for self-build purposes.
26. Even if I could accept that the proposal would result in an improved vehicular
    access, it would be at the expense of the removal of the NDHA. Taken together,
    the benefits of this modest-scaled proposal attract no more than limited weight.
27. The limited public benefits arising from the proposal would not outweigh the harm
    of very considerable magnitude arising from the near-total loss of the NDHA. Nor
    would they outweigh the less than substantial harm to the significance of the BCA.
    There would be conflict with paragraph 206 of the Framework, as the harm
    identified does not have clear and convincing justification.
Highway Safety
28. Kings Hill is a narrow, predominantly single track historic lane with no footway
    provision. Signage at its junction with Dale End identifies the route as unsuitable
    for motor vehicles. However, the presence of car parking areas and driveways
    indicate that it is used by vehicles to gain access to properties. During my visit I
    observed that the narrow width of the road, its undulating nature and the presence
    of boundary walls and buildings, vehicle speeds are lower than the prevailing
    speed limit. This is corroborated by the appellant’s speed survey.
29. Although the barns may not have been used for intensive agricultural activity for
    some time, that is their lawful use. I have not been presented with any compelling
    evidence regarding the level of traffic movements that could lawfully arise from the
    existing use. The proposal relates to a single dwelling and therefore is likely to
    generate a modest number of vehicular movements. Whilst I am not persuaded
    that the increase in vehicle movements associated with a single dwelling would
    amount to a significant intensification in highway terms, the proposal would
    introduce a permanent residential use and therefore more regular day-to-day
    vehicle movements than presently occur.
30. The Highway Authority states that notwithstanding the visibility splays shown on
    the submitted plans, properly constructed splays would achieve only 2m to the
    north and 13.5m to the south, a substantial shortfall below the required splays,

         even when adjusted to reflect prevailing speed limits. This is due to the proposed
         splay to the north relying on passing over an adjacent access, on land outside of

31. Whilst the appellant disputes the Highway Authority's assessment and considers
    that the visibility splay can be achieved within highway land or land under their
    control, I have not been provided with definitive highway boundary information or
    other objective evidence that would justify departing from the professional advice
    of the Highway Authority on this matter.
32. The relevant consideration is whether the proposal would provide a safe and
    suitable access for the development, rather than whether it would represent an
    improvement over the existing situation. I cannot be satisfied that the visibility
    splays relied upon by the appellant could be secured and maintained for the
    lifetime of the development. The proposal therefore fails to demonstrate that safe
    and suitable access could be achieved.
33. I recognise that traffic speeds are low and that many rural and historic lanes
    function safely with visibility below modern standards. Nevertheless, the issue is
    not whether some visibility can be achieved, but whether emerging vehicles would
    have an adequate opportunity to see and be seen by approaching traffic. Visibility
    of only 2m to the north would be wholly inadequate, even on a lightly trafficked
    road where vehicle speeds are below the speed limit. In these circumstances,
    even a modest increase in vehicular activity would give rise to concerns in relation
    to highway safety. Accordingly, whilst I do not attach substantial weight to the
    alleged significant intensification of use, this does not alter my overall conclusion
    on the effect of the proposal on highway safety.
34. I find that the proposal would cause harm to highway safety. It would therefore
    conflict with Policies S4 and HC19 of the DDLP which amongst other things, seek
    to ensure safe accesses for all users.
Protected Species
35. The existing barn is of traditional construction, and it possesses crevices within the
    stonework that could provide roosting opportunities for bats. It is in a rural location
    with mature trees nearby, which may provide suitable commuting and foraging
    habitat. In these circumstances, I find that the building has the potential to support
    bats. The proposed demolition of the barns would therefore have the potential to
    affect a protected species should they be present.
36. Bats are protected by law2 and their presence is a material consideration where a
    development proposal is being considered that would be likely to result in harm to
    the species or its habitat. The fact that ecological information was not required at
    validation stage does not prevent such information from being sought where
    concerns arise during the consideration of the application. I acknowledge that the
    appellant was unaware of the Council’s concerns in this regard prior to the issuing
    of the decision. Nonetheless, as the decision-maker, I must be satisfied that
    sufficient information is available to assess the implications of the proposal for
    protected species.

37. In this case, a protected species survey was not provided during the determination
    of the application to establish whether bats or other protected species are present
    within the building proposed for demolition. The appellant considers that the
    requirement for a survey could be addressed through the imposition of planning
    conditions.
38. Circular 06/20053 states that it is essential that the presence or otherwise of
    protected species, and the extent that they may be affected by the proposed
    development, is established before planning permission is granted. Otherwise, all
    relevant material considerations may not have been addressed in the decision-
    making process. In the absence of any ecological survey evidence, I cannot
    determine whether protected species are present, whether the proposal would
    adversely affect them, whether mitigation would be necessary, or whether any
    such mitigation would be possible and effective. Moreover, the circular advises
    that ecological surveys should only be left to a planning condition in exceptional
    circumstances, which have not been advanced here.
39. For the above reasons, I cannot be satisfied that the proposal would not adversely
    affect protected species or their habitats. The proposal would therefore conflict with
    Policy PD3 of the DDLP which seeks to protect and prevent harm to biodiversity
    interests, an objective shared by paragraph 187 of the Framework.
Other Matters
40. Even if I could have accepted the late evidence regarding bats, it would not have
    affected my findings on the other main issues and would not therefore have altered
    the outcome of the appeal.
Conclusion
41. The proposal would conflict with the development plan and there are no
    considerations including the approach of the Framework which indicate that a
    decision should be made otherwise than in accordance with it. Accordingly, the
    appeal is dismissed.
