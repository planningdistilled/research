Appeal Decision

an Inspector appointed by the Secretary of State

Appeal Ref: 6010288
St Albans House, 98 East Hill, Dartford, Kent DA1 1SB
•      The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
       against a refusal to grant planning permission.
•      The appeal is made by Mr Santokh Lart against the decision of Dartford Borough Council.
•      The application Ref is DA/26/00051/FUL.
•      The development proposed is the provision of a vehicular access onto A226 (including vehicle
       crossover), provision of paved parking area and partial removal of boundary wall.

Decision
1.       The appeal is dismissed.
Preliminary Matters
2.       An amended drawing1 was submitted with the appeal, revising the layout of the
         driveway in response to the Council’s concerns on the visibility splays and swept
         paths. In deciding whether to accept this plan, I have had regard to the Procedural
         Guide2, which states that the appeal process should not be used to evolve a
         scheme and that what is considered at appeal should be essentially the same
         proposal as that which was before the Council and interested parties at the
         application stage.
3.       In this instance, the amended drawing provides supplementary information
         relevant to one of the main issues and does not materially alter the scheme. The
         Council and interested parties have had the opportunity to comment on it during
         the appeal process. I am therefore satisfied that no party would be prejudiced by
         my acceptance of the amended drawing, and I have determined the appeal on that
         basis.
4.       The application form indicates that the proposed works have already commenced
         but have not been completed. From my observations during my site visit, part of
         the boundary wall had been removed and a substantial part of the site had been
         covered by hardstanding. However, no vehicular crossover had been constructed
         across the highway. The surfacing materials used for the hardstanding also
         differed from those shown on the proposed plans. For the avoidance of doubt, my
         assessment is based on the proposal shown on the plans before me, rather than
         what has been constructed to date. Any works undertaken that are not in
         accordance with those proposed plans would be a matter between the Council and
         the appellant.

5.     Prior to the determination of this appeal, a revised version of the National Planning
       Policy Framework (the Framework) was published on 17 August 2026. The
       changes made to the Framework, insofar as they relate to the substantive matters
       in this appeal, do not fundamentally alter the relevant policy position. I am
       therefore satisfied that no party would be prejudiced by my consideration of the
       revised version of the Framework in reaching my decision. References hereafter
       are to the revised version of the Framework.
Main Issues
6.     The main issues are the effect of the proposal on:
           •    the character and appearance of the surrounding area; and
           •    highway safety.
Reasons
Character and appearance
7.     The appeal property and the neighbouring dwellings at 100, 102 and 104 East Hill
       have front gardens enclosed by boundary treatments that include brick walls,
       hedges and timber fences. These front gardens and low-rise boundary treatments
       provide a clear separation between the dwellings and the highway and contribute
       positively to the open character of the street scene. The appeal property occupies
       a particularly large and prominent corner plot, with a generous grassed front
       garden enclosed by a low boundary wall. This makes a notable contribution to the
       openness of the area.
8.     With the exception of Nos 106 and 108 East Hill, the former of which appears to
       have been a public house, the dwellings on this side of East Hill up to its junction
       with Brandon Road generally retain their front gardens without extensive areas of
       hardstanding used for parking. The semi-detached houses on the opposite side of
       the road are of a contrasting built form, and the front gardens of many of those
       properties have been paved to provide parking spaces.
9.     The proposal would involve paving a substantial part of the grassed front garden
       with a permeable paving grid to form a vehicular access and parking area for two
       vehicles, retaining only a small part of the existing grassed area. While the paving
       grid is intended to allow grass to grow through its cells, its use by vehicles would
       be likely to wear away the grass over time, reducing the verdant appearance of the
       frontage. In any event, a substantial part of the garden would become a parking
       area and would be occupied by two parked vehicles when in use. The loss of a
       large part of the open grassed frontage and part of the low boundary wall would
       weaken the visual demarcation between the highway and the dwelling. The
       hardstanding and parked vehicles would also introduce visual clutter that would
       erode the established open character of the street scene. This effect would be
       particularly apparent because of the site’s prominent corner position.

10. The appellant refers to the varied pattern of frontage treatments in the area,
    including alterations to boundary walls, areas of hardstanding and vehicular
    accesses. There is no clear evidence before me regarding the planning status or
    history of the nearby parking areas, including that at No 108. Nevertheless, the
    dwellings in the area, particularly those opposite the appeal site, vary in their
    design and character, and their frontages differ in depth and width. They are
    therefore not directly comparable with the appeal property, which has a wider and
    deeper frontage and occupies a prominent corner plot. Consequently, the existing
    parking areas elsewhere attract limited weight in support of the proposal.
11. Moreover, the parking areas I saw nearby, including at No 108 and on the opposite
    side of East Hill, do not contribute positively to the character of the area. Their
    existence only serves to confirm that such hardstanding parking areas along the
    frontages have a harmful effect on the street scene. In any event, notwithstanding
    any precedent arising from the loss of residential front gardens to parking areas
    elsewhere, the proposed development on its own would cause a significant level of
    harm to the street scene.
12. The appellant suggests that imposing planning conditions relating to materials,
    surfacing, landscaping and boundary treatment could safeguard the appearance of
    the site. Soft landscaping and suitable boundary treatments could provide some
    screening and greenery, helping to retain a degree of the site’s existing garden
    character. However, no details have been provided to demonstrate how such
    measures could be incorporated while also maintaining the visibility splays
    required for the access. In the absence of such details, I cannot be satisfied that
    an acceptable scheme could be secured without materially altering the nature of
    the proposal before me. Consequently, it cannot be concluded that the harm I
    have identified would be adequately addressed, and conditions requiring such
    details would therefore be inappropriate.
13. I conclude that the proposal would be harmful to the character and appearance of
    the surrounding area. Accordingly, it would conflict with Policy M1 of The Dartford
    Plan – Dartford Borough’s Local Plan to 2037 (2024) (the LP), which requires,
    amongst other things, development to respond to, reinforce and enhance positive
    characteristics of its locality.
14. The proposal would also be contrary to Policy DP3 of the Framework, which
    concerns the key principles for well-designed places and states, amongst other
    matters, that development proposals should respond to their context so that they
    integrate with and enhance their surroundings.
Highway safety
15. The appeal site is located at the junction of East Hill (A226) and St Albans Road.
    East Hill is a classified route connecting Dartford and Greenhithe and serves a
    range of nearby destinations, including a hospital, community facilities and local
    shops. The site is close to pedestrian crossing facilities, a bus stop and junction
    traffic controls, while St Albans Road provides access to a vehicle-repair workshop
    and is situated near a nursery and primary school. Taken together, these
    characteristics indicate that the surrounding highway network experiences
    significant levels of pedestrian and vehicular activity.

16. The County Highway Authority has raised concerns about pedestrian visibility
    splays, vehicle manoeuvring space and surfacing materials. Although the
    amended drawing shows a revised visibility splay within the appeal site, the splay
    on the side nearest No 100 conflicts with the vehicle-tracking path shown on that
    side and does not therefore appear to have been accurately represented.
17. Furthermore, while the tracking diagram indicates that a vehicle could enter and
    leave the parking spaces in a forward gear, the vehicles shown are substantially
    shorter than the 4.8-metre-long car required by the County Highway Authority for
    assessment purposes. Consequently, the submitted evidence does not
    demonstrate that a longer vehicle could enter and leave the site in a forward gear.
    A longer vehicle could therefore require reversing movements either into or out of
    the site and across the footway. Given the proximity of the access to the junction,
    the pedestrian crossing and the bus stop, such movements would give rise to an
    increased risk of conflict with pedestrians and other highway users. There is no
    planning control that could reasonably restrict the length of vehicles using the
    parking spaces. In the absence of a tracking diagram for an appropriately sized
    vehicle, or any justification for assessing the proposal using shorter vehicles, I
    have no substantive evidence that would lead me to depart from the County
    Highway Authority’s position. The proposal has therefore not been shown to
    provide safe vehicular access and egress.
18. During my site visit, which took place on a weekday afternoon during the school
    holiday period, I observed a significant level of traffic past the site and through the
    junction. At times, vehicles queued in front of the appeal site while waiting at the
    traffic signals near Nos 106 and 108. Although a site visit provides only a snapshot
    of prevailing traffic conditions, my observations reinforce my concerns regarding
    the potential conflict between vehicles using the proposed access and other
    highway users.
19. The appellant states that, as a former driving instructor, they have consistently
    been able to enter and leave the site in a forward gear. However, the appellant’s
    personal experience does not demonstrate that other drivers, using vehicles of
    different sizes, could undertake the necessary manoeuvres safely. This is
    particularly so given the shortcomings in the submitted tracking and visibility
    information. The appellant also refers to neighbouring properties with more
    constrained accesses and driveways. However, any shortcomings in existing
    arrangements do not justify the introduction of a further access where its safe
    operation has not been demonstrated.
20. The appellant maintains that technical details could be secured by condition if the
    appeal were allowed. A condition could potentially secure details of surfacing
    materials. However, the evidence before me does not demonstrate that a 4.8-
    metre-long vehicle could manoeuvre safely within the site while maintaining
    adequate pedestrian visibility splays. If the access had to be relocated or more of
    the boundary wall removed to achieve an acceptable arrangement, that would
    materially alter the design shown on the plans. A condition leaving the position and
    fundamental design of the access to be resolved following the grant of permission
    would undermine the precision and enforceability of the planning permission, and
    such a condition would therefore not be reasonable.

21. For the reasons set out above, it has not been demonstrated that the proposal
    would have an acceptable effect on highway safety. Accordingly, it would conflict
    with Policy M16 of the LP, which seeks, amongst other things, to minimise adverse
    effects from the travel and movement issues associated with any development.
    The policy also states that development will be supported where the layout and
    siting of all forms of access is acceptable in terms of highway safety and free flow
    of traffic.
Planning Balance and Conclusion
22. Policy S3 of the Framework requires decisions on development proposals to apply
    a presumption in favour of sustainable development. Policy S4 states that
    development proposals within settlements should be approved unless the benefits
    of doing so would be substantially outweighed by adverse effects when assessed
    against the national decision-making policies of the Framework.
23. The proposal would provide two off-street parking spaces, which would help to
    meet residential parking demand and could potentially lead to a reduction in
    demand for on-street parking in the area. There would also be benefits arising
    from the provision of an electric vehicle charging point. However, as these benefits
    would apply to a single residential property, they would be limited in scale. These
    benefits therefore attract modest weight.
24. Against these benefits, the proposal would be harmful to the character and
    appearance of the area. It would conflict with Policy DP3(1) of the Framework,
    without clear justification. That policy states that development proposals should
    respond to their context (the history, character and features of their site and its
    setting), so that they integrate with and enhance their surroundings. Policy DP3(3)
    states that development proposals should be refused if, without clear justification,
    they conflict with Policy DP3(1). This harm therefore weighs substantially against
    the proposal.
25. Furthermore, it has not been demonstrated that the proposal would have an
    acceptable effect on highway safety. It would conflict with Policy TR4(1)(c)(i) of the
    Framework, which states that development proposals should employ measures to
    minimise the scope for conflict between pedestrians and vehicles. It would also
    conflict with Policy TR6(4) of the Framework, which states that development
    proposals should be refused if they would have an unacceptable impact on
    highway safety. This consideration also weighs substantially against the proposal.
26. Taking these considerations together, the benefits of granting planning permission
    would be substantially outweighed by the adverse effects when assessed against
    the national decision-making policies of the Framework. As such, Policy S4 does
    not indicate that planning permission should be granted in this instance.
27. For the reasons given above, I conclude that the proposal would conflict with the
    development plan when read as a whole, and the material considerations in favour
    of the proposal are not of sufficient weight to indicate that the decision should be
    made otherwise than in accordance with the development plan. Therefore, the
    appeal should be dismissed.
