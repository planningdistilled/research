Appeal Decision
Site visit made on 28 July 2026
by E Fawcett BA (Jt Hons) MSc MRTPI
an Inspector appointed by the Secretary of State
Decision date: 30 September 2026


Appeal Ref: 6009649
Land on the East Side of Hole House Lane, Tosside BB7 4TS
•    The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
     against a refusal to grant planning permission.
•    The appeal is made by Mr D Cowking against the decision of Ribble Valley Borough Council.
•    The application Ref is 3/2025/0686.
•    The development proposed is change of use of land from agriculture to mixed use agriculture and
     retail (siting of a food truck) with associated parking.

Decision
1. The appeal is dismissed.
Preliminary Matters
2.     An amended site plan was submitted as part of the appeal which identifies visibility
       splays at the site’s access. I am satisfied that the amendments to the site plan do
       not represent a substantial change to the scheme determined by the Council. The
       Council and interested parties have had an opportunity to comment on the revised
       plan during the appeal, and the Council refers to this amendment, and
       representations from the Local Highway Authority (LHA) in its statement. Given
       this and the limited nature of the change, I am satisfied that no party would be
       prejudiced by accepting the amended plan.
3.     The Government published a revised National Planning Policy Framework
       (the Framework) on 17 August 2026. The main parties were provided with an
       opportunity to comment. I have had regard to the 2026 version of the Framework
       in my decision. References hereafter are to the August 2026 version.
Main Issues
4.     The main issues are the effect of the proposed development on:
           •    the character and appearance of the area, having particular regard to the
                Forest of Bowland National Landscape; and
           •    highway safety, with particular regard to visibility at the site’s access.
Reasons
Character and Appearance
5.     The appeal site is located within the Forest of Bowland National Landscape
       (FBNL). The statutory purpose of National Landscapes is to conserve and
       enhance the natural beauty of the area. Section 85 of the Countryside and Rights

       of Way Act 2000, as amended by section 245 of the Levelling-up and
       Regeneration Act 2023, places a duty on relevant authorities to seek to further this
       purpose. Similarly, Policy N4 of the Framework requires substantial weight to be
       placed on the importance of conserving and enhancing the natural beauty in these
       areas.
6.     This part of the FBNL is characterised by undulating pastoral land on soft rolling
       hills often enclosed by a distinctive network of dry-stone walls which contribute to
       the landscape pattern. It has a strong sense of openness with buildings often
       limited to isolated tightly grouped farmsteads.
7.     The landscape character changes a short distance to the north of the site, where
       Gisburn Forest, the largest block of woodland in the FBNL, becomes a dominant
       feature. In contrast to the open rolling agricultural landscape, the dense
       commercial forestry creates a much stronger sense of enclosure. The forest also
       represents an important recreational resource, supporting an extensive network of
       public rights of way and cycle routes that attract visitors to the area.
8.     The appeal site comprises an irregularly shaped parcel of agricultural land
       containing a mixture of hardstanding and low-level vegetation, with the land rising
       gradually away from the highway. A post-and-wire fence separates the site from
       the adjoining field, which continues to slope upwards to the east, beyond the site
       boundary, while a stone wall forms most of the highway frontage.
9.     Although a substantial proportion of the appeal site is hard surfaced and enclosed
       by post and wire fencing, these features are generally inconspicuous in public
       views. The hardstanding is most noticeable at its access point, although I
       observed at my site visit that its appearance was softened by vegetation growing
       through it. Otherwise, the boundary wall and intervening vegetation largely screen
       this from the highway, whilst the visually lightweight fencing enables the site to be
       read as part of the adjoining field rather than as a separate or developed parcel of
       land, particularly in views from the south. Consequently, despite its altered surface
       treatment, the site appears closely associated with the surrounding pastoral
       landscape and contributes positively to the open and undeveloped character that
       forms part of the special qualities of the FBNL.
10. The proposal would introduce a food truck and associated customer parking.
    Owing to the site’s rising landform and the open character of much of the
    surrounding countryside, customer vehicles would be visible from sections of the
    highway both in close and more distant views. Although existing roadside
    vegetation would likely provide some screening in views close to the site, when
    approaching from the south, views of the associated vehicles would be gained
    across the wider pastoral landscape. Whilst many such views may be transient for
    passing motorists, they would nevertheless be experienced over a relatively
    lengthy stretch of road. Moreover, pedestrians and cyclists, who are likely to travel
    more slowly and are generally more sensitive visual receptors, would experience
    these changes for a longer duration.
11. Although no permanent buildings are proposed, and neither the food truck nor
    customer vehicles would remain on the land continuously, their regular presence
    would materially alter the site’s appearance and character. The proposal would
    introduce an overtly commercial activity, parked vehicles and associated
    movement into an area that presently reads as part of the wider agricultural

       landscape. In doing so, it would diminish the site's open, rural character and
       weaken the contribution it makes to the natural beauty of the FBNL.
12. The proposal would not be viewed in association with an existing farmstead, group
    of buildings or established commercial use. Instead, the food truck and parked
    vehicles would appear detached from the built form typically found in this part of
    the FBNL, resulting in a discordant and incongruous feature within the open
    countryside.
13. Whilst Gisburn Forest restricts longer-distance views from the north, the proposal
    would nonetheless be visible from the highway at the edge of the forested area. In
    this location, the site is experienced within the context of the wider open landscape
    rather than the enclosed character of the forest itself. Consequently, the presence
    of vehicles and commercial activity would appear visually jarring when set against
    the expansive pastoral backdrop. The nearby public car park does not alter this
    conclusion, as it is located within the woodland where the enclosed character of
    the forest substantially limits its visual influence, in marked contrast to the
    openness of the appeal site and its surroundings.
14. It is intended that the food truck would be removed from the site daily, and the
    appellant anticipates that it would only be present during periods of favourable
    weather. However, the visual effects of the proposal would arise primarily during
    daylight hours when the use is operational and when landscape receptors are
    most likely to experience it. Consequently, removing the truck outside operating
    hours would do little to reduce the identified harm.
15. Similarly, although customer vehicles would only be present intermittently, they
    would nevertheless contribute to the harmful visual effects whenever the site is in
    use. Restricting or preventing the use during the winter months would reduce the
    duration of the harm, particularly when vegetation provides less screening.
    However, the proposal would introduce visually intrusive vehicles and commercial
    activity into an exposed part of the FBNL over a significant proportion of the year.
    Consequently, the identified harm to its character and appearance would remain.
16. Furthermore, whilst a planning condition could potentially control the colour and
    finish of the food truck, there would be no realistic mechanism to control the
    colour, size or type of customers’ vehicles. Some vehicles, particularly larger vans,
    could appear especially intrusive within this exposed rural setting. Nor am I
    satisfied that the number of vehicles using the site could be controlled effectively
    through planning conditions. The proposed change of use would permit vehicle
    parking across the site and, given its proximity to a popular recreational
    destination, visitor demand could fluctuate considerably, particularly at weekends
    and during holiday periods.
17. The submitted Landscape and Visual Impact Appraisal identifies further mitigation
    measures, including siting the food truck away from the highway and the provision
    of additional landscaping. Whilst the revised siting may reduce visibility in some
    nearer views, the rising topography of the site means that this could increase the
    prominence of the food truck, particularly in views from the south. In addition,
    whilst supplementary planting may filter or interrupt some views of vehicles
    associated with the use, little substantive evidence has been provided to
    demonstrate that such measures would satisfactorily mitigate the identified
    landscape and visual harm. Indeed, substantial landscaping in this location could

       itself appear at odds with the prevailing openness of the landscape and thereby
       erode one of its defining characteristics.
18. Whilst the existing surfacing on the site is proposed to be retained for the
    proposed use, works to improve the site access would exacerbate the identified
    harm. To provide a safe access arrangement, the first 5 metres of the access
    would require surfacing with a bound material and sections of the boundary wall
    and roadside vegetation would need to be reduced to achieve the necessary
    visibility splays. Whilst details of this surfacing material have not been provided, it
    is likely to appear more formal and visually prominent than the existing surface
    treatment. The removal of sections of wall and vegetation would also diminish a
    traditional field boundary feature and increase public views into the site.
    Collectively, these works would further urbanise the site's appearance and
    reinforce the harmful effects arising from the proposed use.
19. Even if ancillary features, including external seating, could be restricted through
    use of a planning condition, the proposal would still result in harm arising from the
    food truck itself, the associated customer parking, the access alterations, and the
    general commercial activity associated with the use.
20. Overall, the proposed development would result in a harmful impact on the open
    agricultural character and appearance of the site and its surroundings. Whilst the
    associated truck and customer vehicles would not be on the site permanently, and
    most elements of the proposal are reversible, the use would nevertheless result in
    a form of development that would appear incongruous, detached from existing
    buildings or similar uses within this open landscape. Consequently, the proposed
    development would fail to conserve the natural beauty of the FBNL and would
    therefore conflict with the statutory purpose of National Landscapes.
21. I therefore conclude that the proposed development would cause harm to the
    character and appearance of the area, having particular regard to the FBNL.
    It therefore conflicts with Key Statement EN2 and Policies DMG1, DMG2 and
    DMB3 of the Ribble Valley Core Strategy 2008 – 2028 (2014) (the Core Strategy).
    Collectively these seek to ensure, amongst other things, that development within
    the FBNL contributes to the conservation and natural beauty of the area, is in
    keeping with the character of the landscape and its special qualities, does not
    undermine the visual amenities of the area and is sympathetic to existing land
    uses in terms of a proposal’s nature and features.
22. Whilst Core Strategy Policy DMG3 is also referenced within the Council’s refusal
    reason in respect of this main issue, as this relates to transport and mobility and
    does not cover matters in respect of landscape or visual effects of development,
    I find no conflict with it in respect of this main issue.
Highway Safety
23. As part of the appeal submission, the appellant provided an amended plan
    identifying visibility splays at the site’s access, together with a traffic survey.
    Following consultation with the Local Highway Authority (LHA), the Council has
    confirmed that this information satisfactorily addresses its final reason for refusal,
    subject to the imposition of several conditions.
24. The appellant disputes the necessity of a condition requiring the provision of a
    visibility splay measuring 2.4 metres by 43 metres to the south of the site’s access.

       They contend that the access would be used only intermittently for the proposed
       development, and it would not result in a severe impact on highway safety.
       Achieving the visibility splay would require part of the dry-stone wall to be reduced
       in height and some vegetation along the highway boundary to be removed.
25. The appellant’s evidence demonstrates that vehicle speeds are significantly lower
    than the posted speed limit. This justifies a reduction in the required visibility
    distances from those that would ordinarily be expected based on the speed limit
    alone. However, the achievable visibility splay of 2.4 metres by 16.5 metres to the
    south falls significantly short of the 43 metres sought by the LHA, in accordance
    with recognised highway design guidance. Whilst the traffic survey recorded low
    2-way traffic flows, and there is no evidence of recorded collisions within the
    previous 5 years, the safety of all highway users, including pedestrians and
    cyclists, must also be taken into account. Although the proposed use would be
    unlikely to generate a significant number of vehicle movements on a daily basis, it
    would nevertheless result in a material increase in vehicles using the access.
    On the basis of the evidence before me, I cannot therefore be satisfied that the
    visibility splays shown on the amended plan would provide a safe and suitable
    access for all highway users.
26. Nevertheless, the evidence does not indicate that the visibility splay sought by the
    LHA could not be achieved. Accordingly, its provision could be secured by a
    planning condition. Subject to such a condition, I am satisfied that the proposal
    would provide a safe and suitable means of access to serve the development.
27. The LHA has also stated that the site would benefit from marked out parking bays
    and a defined turning area. However, given that the parking area would be used
    only in connection with the food truck and not for long-term parking, there is no
    substantive evidence before me to demonstrate that such measures are necessary
    in the interests of highway safety.
28. I therefore conclude that the proposed development would not cause harm to
    highway safety with particular regard to visibility at the site’s access. It would
    therefore comply with Policies DMG1 and DMG3 of the Core Strategy, in respect
    of this main issue. Collectively these policies, amongst other things, seek to
    ensure that safe access can be provided, suitable to the scale and type of traffic to
    be generated.
Other Considerations
29. The Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (England)
    Order 2015 (as amended) (GPDO) allows for certain temporary uses of land for up
    to 28 days in a calendar year. This permitted development right could be exercised
    in respect of the proposed use, and there is a greater than a theoretical possibility
    that this might take place. However, even if the use was not operated during the
    winter months, the proposed development would still significantly exceed the
    number of days allowed by the GPDO. The fallback position would therefore be
    significantly less harmful than the appeal proposal. I therefore afford this only a
    small amount of weight.

Planning Balance and Conclusion
30. The proposed development would cause harm to the character and appearance of
    the FBNL. Whilst the Local Plan contains policies that provide support for farm
    diversification and proposals that strengthen the rural economy, the proposal
    would conflict with the development plan taken as a whole.
31. Policy S5 of the Framework sets out that only certain forms of development should
    be approved outside settlements. This includes development for rural businesses,
    including tourism, where a location outside settlements is shown to be necessary,
    and the redevelopment of previously developed land. As the proposal would
    support an existing farm enterprise, together with an existing leisure and tourism
    attraction close to the site, and would re-use an area of hardstanding, the principle
    of development in this location attracts support from the Framework.
32. The policy goes on to state that such development should be approved unless the
    benefits of doing so would be substantially outweighed by any adverse effects,
    when assessed against the national decision-making policies in the Framework.
33. Policy E2 of the Framework states that substantial weight should be given to
    economic benefits of proposals for commercial development which allow
    businesses to invest, expand and/or adapt. Policy E4 supports the diversification
    of agricultural businesses and facilities to support rural leisure and tourism. The
    proposal would provide some economic benefits to the farm enterprise, and the
    rural economy, and support tourism in the area, although these would be limited by
    the small scale of the proposed development. However, Policy E4 also states that,
    in meeting business needs in rural areas, the siting and design of the development
    should be appropriate to the character of its surroundings.
34. In supporting the farm enterprise, the proposed development would provide some
    limited support for associated environmental land management practices.
    The proposal also includes additional landscaping which would provide some
    environmental benefits, including for biodiversity and to the character and
    appearance of the landscape, to which Policy N2 of the Framework gives general
    support. However, as set out above, this would not adequately mitigate the harm
    identified, and it could in itself cause harm to the open character of the area.
    I therefore afford these benefits a small amount of weight.
35. However, the proposal would also cause harm to the special qualities of the FBNL.
    Whilst the scale of the development would be relatively modest, for the reasons
    set out above, it would not be sensitively located. Furthermore, Policy N4 of the
    Framework places substantial weight on the importance of conserving and
    enhancing the natural beauty of Protected Landscapes. The proposal would also
    conflict with Policy DP3 which indicates that proposals should be refused if,
    without clear justification, they fail to respond to the character and features of their
    site and its setting, and consequently fail to integrate with and enhance their
    surroundings.
36. Taken together, the benefits of the proposal would be substantially outweighed by
    its adverse effects when assessed against the national decision-making policies of
    the Framework. As such, Policy S5 of the Framework does not point to permission
    being granted in this instance.
