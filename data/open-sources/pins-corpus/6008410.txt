Appeal Ref: 6008410
14 Elm Grove, Hartlepool, TS26 8LZ
•    The appeal is made under section 78 of the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 (as amended)
     against a refusal to grant planning permission.
•    The appeal is made by Active Care Group against the decision of Hartlepool Borough Council.
•    The application Ref is H/2025/0256.
•    The development proposed is for the change of use from residential dwelling (C3 Use Class) to
     supported living residential accommodation (C2 Use Class) for up to 5 children with
     learning difficulties and complex care needs, including garage conversion, car parking, single storey
     in-fill extension and canopy.

Decision
1.     The appeal is dismissed.
Preliminary Matters
2.     A revised version of the National Planning Policy Framework (the Framework) was
       published with immediate effect on 17 August 2026. Policies within the revised
       Framework are material considerations which should be taken into account in
       decision making. The main parties have been given an opportunity to comment on
       the new Framework and I have taken into account any subsequent comments
       received in arriving at my decision.
3.     In accordance with Article 3(1) of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of
       the Child, I have treated the best interests of children as a primary consideration
       throughout my assessment. I have also had regard to Article 19 of the United
       Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, Article 8(1) of the
       Human Rights Act 1998, and the Public Sector Equality Duty under section 149 of
       the Equality Act 2010.
Main Issues
4.     The main issues are:

       •        the effect of the proposed use on the character of the residential area;
       •        the effect of the proposal on the living conditions of neighbouring occupiers,
                with particular regard to noise and disturbance; and
       •        whether there are other considerations, including the provision of
                accommodation and care for disabled children, which outweigh any harm.

Reasons
Residential character
5.     The appeal site comprises a large, detached seven-bedroom dwelling on a
       spacious plot within an established residential area. The property is set back from
       Elm Grove behind a front garden, with extensive private gardens and a side
       driveway providing vehicular access. The proposal seeks a change of use to
       supported living residential accommodation for up to five children, alongside a
       modest extension, garage conversion, internal alterations, and an eight-space off-
       street parking area. Future residents would occupy the property as their home with
       dedicated care and support. Day-to-day activities for residents would reflect typical
       residential living, including travel to school or college, shopping, leisure, and
       routine visits by healthcare professionals and family.
6.     Policy RC21 of the Hartlepool Local Plan (2018) requires commercial development
       within residential areas to avoid significant detrimental effects on neighbouring
       amenity, including from excessive traffic generation, to be compatible with the
       character and amenity of the surrounding area, and to provide appropriate parking
       arrangements. A children's care home does not, however, sit comfortably within
       the categories of development expressly identified by the policy, namely industrial,
       business, leisure, retail and other commercial uses. Nevertheless, the concerns
       addressed by RC21, particularly those relating to operational activity, residential
       living conditions and compatibility with local character, are relevant to the appeal
       proposal. I have therefore had regard to the policy insofar as it is relevant to the
       particular circumstances of this case.
7.     In any event, similar considerations arise under Local Plan Policy QP4, which is
       plainly applicable to the appeal proposal. Amongst other things, QP4 seeks to
       ensure that development respects its surroundings and does not adversely affect
       the amenity of occupiers of nearby properties through general disturbance. In that
       regard, the key question is not whether the proposal falls within a particular
       category of commercial development, but whether the scale and pattern of activity
       associated with the use would be compatible with the character of the area and the
       living conditions of neighbouring occupiers.
8.     The proposed physical alterations are limited and would preserve the property’s
       domestic appearance. The extension, garage conversion, and ancillary works
       would remain subordinate to the main dwelling, with no external signage or
       commercial paraphernalia. The parking area would be contained within the
       existing curtilage using the established driveway. Consequently, the development
       would retain the dwelling's domestic character and would not appear out of place
       within the street scene.
9.     The appeal site lies adjacent to Oval Grange. Whilst that development does not
       take vehicular access from Elm Grove, it comprises a substantial flatted residential
       development with communal parking areas and garages that are readily apparent
       from the surrounding streets. The wider area is therefore not characterised
       exclusively by detached or low-density residential development. Moreover, the
       appeal site is reasonably well connected to local services and the town centre by
       public transport, pedestrian and vehicular routes. Consequently, vehicle
       movements, parking activity and the routine comings and goings associated with
       residential occupation already form part of the character of the area.

10. The appellant's evidence indicates that staffing levels for a home accommodating
    up to five children would vary throughout the day, with up to 12 staff present during
    weekday daytime periods and at shift changeovers, reducing to around 7 staff
    during much of the day and 5 staff overnight. Staff shifts operate between 08:45
    and 21:00 for day staff and between 20:45 and 09:00 for night staff, with
    handovers occurring twice daily. Whilst the appellant states that approximately
    50% of staff travel by public transport, the operation of the home would
    nevertheless generate regular vehicle movements associated with staff arrivals
    and departures, together with movements arising from residents travelling to and
    from education, appointments and community activities, as well as visitors and
    routine servicing requirements.
11. However, the 50% expectation relies largely on operational aspirations,
    recruitment intentions and travel patterns associated with the operator's existing
    facilities. No bus timetables, employment strategy, green travel policy, travel plan,
    monitoring framework, or other mechanism has been provided to demonstrate that
    such travel patterns would be achievable at this site or likely to be maintained over
    time. Therefore, whilst some staff may reasonably be expected to travel on foot, by
    public transport or by other non-car modes, there is no certainty that the level of
    non-car travel anticipated by the appellant would occur throughout the lifetime of
    the development.
12. The evidence indicates that activity at the site would not be evenly distributed
    throughout the day. Rather, staffing arrangements would give rise to recurring
    peaks of activity at predictable times associated with shift changeovers. These
    periods would result in a concentration of vehicular and pedestrian movements
    over relatively short periods as staff arrive, depart and manoeuvre vehicles within
    the site.
13. Nevertheless, even allowing for the possibility that the level of non-car travel
    anticipated by the appellant may not be achieved, I am not persuaded that the
    overall number of vehicle movements likely to be generated by the proposal would
    be excessive in the context of the surrounding residential area or materially out of
    keeping with its character. In reaching that view, I note that the Highway Authority
    has raised no objection to the proposal. Furthermore, journeys associated with
    staff, visitors and residents would be distributed across a number of surrounding
    streets and routes rather than being concentrated along a single route into and out
    of Elm Grove.
14. Residents would also travel to education, community activities, family visits,
    shopping and occasional appointments. Whilst these journeys would extend
    beyond the principal handover periods, they would arise from the day-to-day care
    and support needs of the resident children and, viewed in that context, would not
    be unusual within a residential area. The property would not operate as a base for
    other facilities, nor would it host corporate meetings, staff training or wider
    management functions. Any office, staff and activity spaces would be ancillary to
    the care of the resident children and the operation of the home. Although the
    proposal would generate a greater frequency of activity than a single household,
    that activity would arise from the provision of care and accommodation to children
    living at the property and would therefore remain fundamentally residential in
    character.

15. I therefore conclude that the proposed use would not have a harmful effect on the
    character of the residential area. Insofar as they relate to the compatibility of the
    use with its residential surroundings, I find that the proposal would not conflict with
    those aspects of Local Plan Policies RC21 and QP4. However, those findings do
    not determine the separate question of the proposal's effect on the living
    conditions of neighbouring occupiers, which I consider below.
Living conditions
16. My findings in relation to the character of the area do not determine the effect of
    the proposal on the living conditions of neighbouring occupiers. A use may be
    compatible with the character of a residential area whilst nevertheless giving rise
    to unacceptable effects on the living conditions of occupiers who share a
    particularly close relationship with the site. In this case, whilst I have found that the
    overall level of activity generated by the proposal would not be excessive in the
    context of the wider area and would remain residential in character, the proposal
    would not operate in the same manner as either a seven-bedroom dwelling
    occupied by a single household or a 6-person HMO.
17. The activity associated with those uses would ordinarily arise from the routines of
    residents themselves and would tend to occur in a more dispersed and less
    structured manner. By contrast, the proposed children's home would generate
    additional movements associated with staffing requirements, visitors, residents'
    activities and the day-to-day operation of the facility. It would also introduce regular
    and predictable peaks of arrivals and departures linked to shift changes.
18. Properties within Oval Grange and on the opposite side of Elm Grove would be
    less sensitive to activity associated with the proposal, owing to their greater
    separation distances and the intervening highway. However, the position of No 12
    is materially different from Oval Grange and other homes on Elm Grove. It shares
    a close physical relationship with the appeal site, with the two driveways running
    side-by-side for much of their length. Whilst sections of higher fencing, walling and
    the respective garages provide screening further into the site, the frontage is more
    open, comprising slatted timber fencing between brick piers. As a result, the
    occupiers of No 12 would experience the activity associated with vehicle
    movements more directly than occupiers elsewhere.
19. The principal windows of No 12 face Elm Grove and would clearly allow views of
    vehicles arriving at and departing from the appeal site. However, the side elevation
    facing the appeal site also contains large ground-floor windows and glazed
    elements associated with the entrance porch, together with two small, high-level
    first-floor windows. All of these openings are located close to the shared driveway
    boundary and in close proximity to the area through which vehicles would enter
    and leave the site. The intervening boundary treatment is not continuously solid
    and, given the slight difference in levels between the respective driveways, I find it
    likely that views of vehicle movements and associated activity, together with the
    noise arising from them, would be perceptible through and above sections of the
    boundary treatment within the home, front garden and side of the dwelling.
20. Regardless of whether vehicles approach from the east or west, they would turn
    into and out of the appeal site's driveway immediately adjacent to No 12. Such
    activity would be funnelled into the driveway and is therefore materially different
    from the more dispersed background movement evident elsewhere within the

       locality. Arrivals, departures and staff shift changeovers would therefore be
       experienced at close quarters and would result in periods of concentrated activity.
       The principal periods of activity would occur during the morning and evening, when
       residents could reasonably expect a greater degree of quiet enjoyment within their
       home, whether preparing for the day, working from home, relaxing during the
       evening or resting.
21. The garage serving No 12 and the built form of the appeal property provide a
    degree of physical separation between the respective garden areas and the
    proposed parking area. The proposal would not give rise to harmful effects in
    terms of overlooking, loss of privacy, loss of outlook, overshadowing, or
    overbearing impacts. However, the vehicle manoeuvring, engine noise, door
    closures, headlights and associated comings and goings would be readily
    perceptible from within the dwelling. The proposal would introduce a recurring and
    concentrated source of vehicle movements, noise and activity immediately
    alongside the dwelling, where occupiers would ordinarily expect a greater freedom
    from disturbance.
22. The appellant places considerable reliance on the absence of objection from the
    Council's Public Protection Team and the lack of technical evidence demonstrating
    that the proposal would result in noise exposure reaching the Significant Observed
    Adverse Effect Level (SOAEL). However, national policy requires decision-makers
    not only to avoid noise giving rise to a SOAEL, but also to mitigate and reduce
    potential adverse impacts to a minimum. The policy objective is therefore broader
    than simply demonstrating whether a particular noise threshold would be
    exceeded. Moreover, my concerns do not arise solely from noise measured in
    technical terms. Rather, they relate to the effect of the proposed activity on the
    living conditions of neighbouring occupiers, having regard to the frequency, timing
    and nature of the proposed comings and goings in close proximity to No 12.
23. I have already found that there is no certainty that the modal shift anticipated by
    the appellant would be achieved or maintained over time. Whilst the peak periods
    of activity associated with staff changeovers would be limited in duration, they
    would occur routinely and repeatedly throughout the week. Consequently, the
    effect on neighbouring occupiers would arise not simply from the intensity of
    activity at any one time, but from its regular and foreseeable recurrence, which
    would make it a persistent feature of the living environment of No 12.
24. As such, I find that the ability of the occupiers of No 12 to enjoy normal domestic
    activities, including relaxing within habitable rooms and opening windows for
    ventilation, would be adversely affected by the regular presence of activity
    immediately adjacent to the property. Taken together, the proposal would give rise
    to a level of noise, disturbance and activity that would materially exceed that
    typically associated with either a single dwellinghouse or a small HMO. The
    resulting activity would not be occasional or sporadic in nature, but would instead
    comprise a recurring and concentrated source of disturbance arising from the
    regular pattern of arrivals, departures and associated movements. Consequently, I
    find that the proposal would result in an unacceptably intrusive living environment
    for the occupiers of No 12.
25. Consequently, for the reasons set out above, I find that the proposal would be
    harmful to the living conditions of the occupiers of No 12. Insofar as they are
    relevant to this issue, Policy RC21(1) seeks to ensure that development does not

       result in significant detrimental effects on the amenities of adjoining or nearby
       occupiers by reason of noise or excessive traffic generation, whilst Policy QP4(9)
       seeks to ensure that development does not negatively affect neighbouring
       occupiers through general disturbance. For the reasons set out above, I find that
       the proposal would conflict with those policies.
Other Considerations
26. The Council's reason for refusal refers to paragraphs 135(f) and 198(a) of the
    2024 Framework in support of its position that the proposal would adversely affect
    the character of the area and the living conditions of neighbouring occupiers. The
    Council has subsequently reviewed the revised Framework and concludes that it
    does not materially alter its position. However, beyond a brief reference to
    Framework Policy HO1(2)(f), the Council has not identified any specific national
    decision-making policies within the Framework with which it considers the proposal
    would conflict.
27. The appellant's case is essentially that the Framework places greater emphasis on
    meeting the accommodation needs of vulnerable groups. They point to Framework
    Policy HO1(2)(f) which identifies looked-after children and those requiring
    specialist community-based accommodation as groups whose housing and
    accommodation needs should be considered through the planning system.
    However, this is a plan-making rather than a decision-making policy, and
    Framework Paragraph 8 states that plan-making policies should not be used in the
    determination of individual development proposals.
28. Of greater relevance, the appellant places significant reliance on Framework
    Policy HC4. It is argued that the appeal proposal falls within the scope of that
    policy because it would provide social care infrastructure, which the Framework
    glossary expressly identifies as a form of public service infrastructure. The
    proposal would provide accommodation and care for children with learning
    disabilities and complex care needs, thereby making a demonstrable contribution
    towards promoting health and wellbeing, reducing health inequalities, and
    supporting the social inclusion of a particularly vulnerable group. I therefore agree
    that the proposal attracts substantial weight under Framework Policy HC4. The
    appellant also relies on Framework Policy DP3 concerning well-designed places,
    submitting that the proposal would remain residential in character and provide a
    domestic environment rather than an institutional setting.
29. Framework Policies S4 and HC4 provide support for the proposal as development
    within a settlement that would deliver a valuable community facility. However,
    Policy DP3(3) indicates that permission should be refused where a proposal
    conflicts with DP3(1) or relevant aspects of DP3(2). As identified under the main
    issues, I consider that the proposal would fail to respond appropriately to its
    context because the proposed access arrangements would not operate
    comfortably alongside the neighbouring residential property at No 12. It would
    therefore conflict with the liveability, movement and built form principles of
    Framework Policy DP3. This conflict, together with the identified conflict with
    development plan policies, weighs substantially against the proposal in the overall
    planning balance.
30. In relation to the UNCRC and the UNCRPD, I have treated the best interests of
    children, including disabled children, as a primary consideration throughout my

       assessment. The evidence indicates that the proposal is intended to provide a
       more domestic alternative to the operator's existing accommodation at Norton
       Court. However, the evidence before me does not identify any particular child or
       children who would occupy the appeal property, nor does it establish their
       individual circumstances, educational arrangements or support needs.
       Consequently, I am unable to conclude that dismissal of the appeal would have
       direct adverse consequences for any identified individual, or that the particular
       needs of any identified child require this specific appeal site to be developed in the
       manner proposed.
31. Furthermore, the rights engaged under the HRA, together with the duties arising
    under the PSED, are not absolute and may be restricted where justified and
    proportionate in pursuit of legitimate planning objectives. The concept of
    proportionality is therefore central to my assessment. In applying that principle, I
    must balance the benefits of the proposal against the identified harm to the living
    conditions of the occupiers of No 12.
32. The proposed accommodation would be likely to provide a more domestic and less
    institutional living environment than larger residential facilities. The staffing
    arrangements indicate a level of support capable of responding to a wide range of
    medical, sensory, physical and emotional needs, allowing care and daily routines
    to be tailored to the individual. Living alongside peers in a shared home
    environment could provide opportunities for social interaction, reduce isolation and
    assist in the development of independence and everyday life skills. Dedicated
    support would also be likely to assist in maintaining continuity of education and
    access to services.
33. By operating within an established residential neighbourhood, the proposal would
    support the inclusion of disabled children within the wider community. The home
    would also be likely to generate employment opportunities across a range of care,
    management, maintenance and specialist support roles, making a modest
    contribution to the local economy. I also recognise that increasing the availability of
    specialist residential provision could, in some circumstances, reduce reliance on
    placements located further from children's families, education settings and existing
    support networks, with associated benefits for continuity of care, education and
    family relationships.
34. I have carefully considered whether the harm to the occupiers of No 12 could be
    adequately addressed through the imposition of planning conditions. The appellant
    refers generally to management arrangements and the potential for conditions to
    control the operation of the home. However, there is no detailed Operational
    Management Plan before me setting out, for example, how staff arrivals and
    departures would be managed, whether shift changeovers would be staggered,
    how vehicle movements would be controlled, how visitors would be managed, and
    what measures would be implemented to minimise noise and disturbance. Such
    information is fundamental to understanding how the development would operate
    in practice and, in turn, the likely effect on neighbouring occupiers. Nor can these
    matters reasonably be deferred to a post-decision approval process. The effect on
    neighbouring living conditions is a central issue in this appeal and is integral to
    determining whether the proposal is acceptable in this location. In the absence of
    that evidence, it has not been demonstrated that an acceptable form of
    development exists.

35. I recognise that alternative parking and access arrangements were discussed
    during the application process and that amendments were subsequently made to
    the scheme. The appellant's submissions also indicate that other parking
    arrangements were contemplated. Given the size of the appeal property and its
    wider curtilage, and notwithstanding its status as a non-designated heritage asset,
    the evidence before me does not demonstrate that the proposed access and
    parking arrangements are the only practicable means of accommodating the
    intended use.
Other Matters
36. The appeal property, known as "Myrtle Mount", is included on the Borough's list of
    locally important buildings and is therefore a non-designated heritage asset. The
    evidence before me indicates that the asset's significance is derived principally
    from its local historic interest and its contribution to the character and history of the
    area. It also possesses some architectural interest as a substantial detached
    dwelling of traditional design, the principal heritage value of which is expressed
    through its retained external appearance and architectural detailing. Its
    significance is further derived, to a limited extent, from its immediate setting, which
    allows the building to be appreciated as a prominent dwelling within a spacious
    residential plot.
37. The proposal would retain the building in a single ownership, and the property
    would continue to read largely as a substantial residential building. Whilst the
    scheme includes modest extensions, the conversion of the detached garage and
    the provision of additional car parking within the grounds, there is no evidence
    before me that these works would result in the loss of significant historic fabric or
    materially alter those features which contribute most to the asset's significance.
    Nor would they fundamentally change the way in which the building is experienced
    from the public realm.
38. In addition, the Council's heritage adviser concluded that the proposal would not
    adversely affect the significance of the non-designated heritage asset and raised
    no objection to the scheme. The Council did not raise concerns regarding the
    effect of the proposal on the setting of the nearby conservation area. Having
    regard to the evidence before me, I am satisfied that the proposed development
    would preserve the significance of the appeal building as a non-designated
    heritage asset. Consequently, this is a neutral matter in my assessment of the
    proposal.
39. The Council undertook a Habitats Regulations Assessment and consulted Natural
    England, who raised no objection. Whilst the Council concluded that the proposal
    would not adversely affect the integrity of any European site, either alone or in
    combination with other plans or projects, it is unnecessary for me to reach a
    finding on that matter because I am dismissing the appeal for other reasons.
    Consequently, this matter does not weigh either for or against the proposal.
40. Interested parties have raised a range of additional concerns, including highway
    safety, parking and congestion, drainage, flood risk, ecology, anti-social behaviour,
    pressure on local services, planning precedent, alternative sites, and matters
    relating to the appellant's operation of other facilities. However, none formed part
    of the Council's reason for refusal, and the relevant consultees raised no
    objections. Furthermore, matters relating to the regulation of care provision,

Planning Balance and Conclusion
41. The proposal would deliver important social benefits through the provision of
    specialist accommodation and care for up to five disabled children. As social care
    infrastructure of a type supported by Framework Policies S4 and HC4, it would be
    likely to promote health and wellbeing, support social inclusion and equality of
    opportunity, and provide a more domestic and less institutional living environment
    than larger residential facilities. I have also treated the best interests of children as
    a primary consideration throughout my assessment. Taken together, these are
    important material considerations to which I attach substantial weight.
42. Nevertheless, I temper that weight I attach to these benefits because the appellant
    has not provided sufficient detail to demonstrate how the home would operate in
    practice, nor has it demonstrated that the proposed access and parking
    arrangements, and the likely effects arising from them, are the only practicable
    means of accommodating the intended use on the site. Whilst I recognise the
    importance of providing specialist accommodation and care for disabled children,
    the evidence does not demonstrate that those benefits can only be realised
    through the development as proposed.
43. I have found that the proposal would cause harm to the living conditions of the
    occupiers of No 12 through noise, disturbance and activity associated with the
    operation of the home. That harm would give rise to conflict with Policies RC21
    and QP4 of the Hartlepool Local Plan. I have also found that the proposal would
    conflict with Framework Policy DP3. In this respect, the Framework is clear that
    the creation of high-quality, sustainable places is fundamental to the planning
    process and that permission should be refused where proposals fail to accord with
    those principles. I attach very substantial weight to this harm and the resulting
    conflict with development plan policy and Framework Policy DP3
44. An overall planning balance is not a mathematical exercise. I have had due regard
    to the PSED, the rights protected by Article 8 of the HRA, and the interests arising
    under the UNCRC and the UNCRPD. I recognise that dismissing the appeal would
    not advance the aims of the PSED and would interfere, to some extent, with the
    interests engaged by those instruments. Nevertheless, drawing these matters
    together, I conclude that the adverse impacts of granting planning permission
    would substantially outweigh the benefits of the proposal.
45. The material considerations do not indicate that a decision should be made other
    than in accordance with the development plan. I therefore conclude that it is
    proportionate and necessary to dismiss the appeal.
