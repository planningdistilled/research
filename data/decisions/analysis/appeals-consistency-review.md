# Appeal decisions under the 2026 NPPF: which get the Framework right?

Draft, 29 September 2026; updated 30 September 2026 with the 177 decisions added that day, and 2 October 2026 with 85 more. This review covers every appeal decision in the database: 984 Planning Inspectorate (PINS) decisions and 2 Secretary of State (SoS) decisions, dated 17 August to 2 October 2026. It tests them against the six issues in the Stratford note (`pages/authority/stratford-dc/nppf-decisions/build/note.md`). For each issue the question is the same: what does the August 2026 National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) require, and does the inspector apply that test?

- **Correct**: the letter applies the Framework test as written.
- **Differs**: the issue arises, but the letter applies a different test, skips a required step, or uses 2024 wording as the test it relies on.
- **Borderline**: the step is missing, but the letter may cover it implicitly, or the result would plainly be the same.

The per-case results, with the reason, paragraph and a verbatim quote for each, are in `appeals-review/issue-1.tsv` to `issue-6.tsv`. All 1,027 quotes were machine-checked against the letter text, and all 1,027 matched.

**What "clear justification" means is unsettled.** Inspectors read it two ways. At Didcot 6009340 ¶22 it is the benefits outweighing the harm: "There would, therefore, be clear justification for the harm that would arise, as is required by Policy DP3.3". At Polegate 6008314 ¶43 it is necessity: the tree loss "would be necessary as part of the appeal development". Either way, the DP3(3) question is stricter than the S4/S5 test. The S4/S5 test asks whether the benefits are *substantially* outweighed, which favours approval. DP3(3) asks either whether the harm is necessary (Polegate) or whether the benefits outweigh it on a level balance (Didcot). If there is no clear justification, S4(2)(c)/S5(2) then say the benefits are likely to be substantially outweighed. So a decision that allows a scheme with an unjustified design conflict after running only the S4/S5 test has skipped a step and used a more lenient test, on either reading. A dismissal that skips the step reaches the result the policy points to, so the difference there is one of route, not outcome. Two hearings decided after the first draft use one reading each: Bilsborrow 6007133 ¶59 refuses on necessity ("I do not find the specific and significant harm to the character and appearance of the countryside to be necessary to achieve the substantial public benefits"), and Aston Clinton 6008253 ¶86 allows on a level balance ("the substantial benefits are sufficient to provide clear justification for the conflict with Policy DP3 of the Framework"). No Secretary of State or court decision has settled which reading is right.

"Appeals where it arises" is the number marked correct or differs. The candidates marked not engaged are left out: 29, 0, 85, 12, 203 and 81 for issues 1 to 6.

## Patterns

- **Most differences are in dismissals, so they made no difference to the result.** 266 appeals differ on at least one issue, not counting immaterial wording. 217 differ on only one. Nine differ on three or four: Chilworth 6009745, Gray's Inn Road 6010844, Poole 6005194, Braceborough 6007771, Shenley 6008668 and the Morgan and Morecambe DCO, which are all either 17 August letters or knowingly used the 2024 version; among the letters added on 30 September, Somerford 6008864 (dismissed) and Newtown 6010537 (allowed); and, among those added on 2 October, Hildenborough 6010459 (allowed).
- **DP3(3) is the least often named test, but rarely changes a result, and it is being named more often.** 94% of design-harm letters are consistent with it. 41% name it overall, rising from 1 of 21 in August to 21 of 65 on 1–11 September, 31 of 64 on 12–22 September and 45 of 87 on 23–30 September; in the first two days of October it was 10 of 27. Almost all the others dismiss anyway. Seven allowed appeals skip the clear-justification question, five of them borderline.
- **Location is the most consistently applied test in dismissals and the least consistent in allowals.** All but one TR3 difference (Chilworth, 17 August) fall on or after 10 September, and 25 of the 36 were allowed. In letters dated 23 September to 2 October, 43 of 69 applied the test (62%), against 69 of 79 (87%) up to 22 September, with 17 allowals that differ against 8. Three of the newest are permissions in principle that leave the route, a missing footway or the whole location question to the technical details stage (Hildenborough 6010459 ¶20; Hitchin 6009720 ¶20; Market Harborough 6008177 ¶22). The later letters were taken from a complete sweep while the earlier ones partly over-sample notable schemes, so part of the drop may be selection. The shortcuts match those criticised in SDC's reports: lower rural standards, short car journeys, residents' existing habits, and a one-sentence pass on location with no route evidence.
- **2024 thinking persists in the weight given to plan policies.** Inspectors cite Annex A(2) and find a policy consistent, then cut its weight because of under-delivery, which is the 2024 "out-of-date" route.
- **2024 vocabulary is common but mostly harmless.** Many letters marked correct still say "less than substantial" or "great weight", or quote the 2024 "vary between urban and rural areas", but then apply the 2026 test.

## Corrections this review makes to earlier work

- **Published Stratford note, Hook-a-Gate 6008785.** The note cites ¶39 as a correct example, and it is: design policies keep their weight. But ¶38 of the same letter cuts the spatial strategy's weight "considering the lack of a 5-year housing land supply" (issue 3). Consider adding a caveat, or using Kingston Bagpuize 6005809 ¶76 instead.
- **`transition-and-decision-makers.md` T4.** It says 6007104 and 6010973 were allowed without the S4/S5 category step. Both applied S5 first, so their "significantly and demonstrably" slips are immaterial. Also, 6011471 is a slip; 6009443 and 6007423 are not.
- **Case coding.** 6009059 and 6008540 are coded `not-cited`, but they use 2024 tests. 6008290 is coded 2026, but relies on 2024 chapter 16. 6009068's `local_copy` points to the costs decision, not the appeal decision. Ivinghoe 6007136 ¶35 says the adverse effects "would not substantially outweigh the benefits" but the appeal was dismissed, which looks like a drafting slip.

## Method and limits

- The 177 decisions added on 30 September and the 85 added on 2 October were classified by the agents that distilled them, each covering about 30 letters, using the same verdict vocabularies. Their rows were merged into the same TSVs.
- Candidates for each issue were pre-filtered from the database's policy findings, tags and a text search of the letters. One reviewer then read the relevant paragraphs of every candidate letter. Issue 6 covered all 986.
- Each issue was classified by a single reviewer, with no second review. Treat borderline rows as judgement calls; the TSV reason column says why each was marked.
- In `issue-1.tsv`, "differs" follows a strict reading: a letter that does not name DP3(3) is marked differs even when it dismisses. The summary table counts those dismissals as consistent with the test (same result, different route) and keeps them apart from the six allowals that differ in substance.
- The database over-samples notable schemes, so the counts describe this sample, not all appeals.
