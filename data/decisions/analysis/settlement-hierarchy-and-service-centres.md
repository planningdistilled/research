# Settlement hierarchy and "service centre" labels: no standalone weight under the August 2026 NPPF

Analyst: analyst-settlement, 30 Sep 2026. Purpose: pin down a term that surfaces only incidentally in the dataset — "service centre" (and its cousins "Key Service Centre", "Local Service Village", "Main Rural Centre") — because it is easy to mistake a local development-plan label for a National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) designation. Framework text checked against `open:nppf/NPPF-August-2026.pdf` (S3–S5, TR3, Annex B glossary). Core Strategy text checked against the committed Stratford-on-Avon Core Strategy (CS) extract (`sources:stratford-dc/public-note/cs.txt`, CS.15, CS.16). Dataset scanned for every use of the term.

---

## 1. The headline proposition

**A settlement-hierarchy tier is a local plan label. It carries no independent weight in the NPPF, and it never converts a site into a "settlement" for the S4/S5 route on its own. It matters only as evidence feeding three separate tests. (Strong: the term is absent from the Framework, and the two dataset uses are both TR3 access facts.)**

- **The Framework has no settlement hierarchy and no "service centre".** The words "service centre", "service village", "rural centre" and "settlement hierarchy" do not appear anywhere in the August 2026 NPPF. The only tier-language in the document is unrelated ("two tier areas" for Mineral Consultation Areas). The Framework's spatial vocabulary is just **"settlement"** (Annex B glossary) plus the **S4 (within settlements) / S5 (outside settlements)** split, with **S2(1)(a)** (a plan-making policy) asking plans to identify "Settlements within the development plan area (applying the definition in the glossary at Annex B), whether existing or proposed, and their boundaries". (S3(1)(a) is the decision-stage routing rule: within a settlement, apply S4.)
- **"Service centre" is a development-plan term, defined per council.** Each Local Plan sets its own hierarchy. In Stratford's Core Strategy the tiers are: Stratford-upon-Avon (main town) → **eight Main Rural Centres** → **Local Service Villages** (LSVs) → villages not identified in the hierarchy. **CS.15 (Distribution of Development)** sets the hierarchy and splits LSVs into four categories; **CS.16 (Housing Development)** sets the housing requirement for each. Other councils use their own labels ("Key Service Centre", "Community Hub", and so on).

## 2. The three roles it legitimately plays

A hierarchy tier is only ever *evidence*. It bears on:

1. **Development-plan accordance.** A scheme directed to a higher-tier settlement accords with the plan's spatial strategy (here CS.15/CS.16); one in a low tier or an unlisted village conflicts with it. This is weighed under **Annex A** as a matter of the *local* plan, not the Framework. Its weight depends on the plan's consistency with the NPPF and its up-to-dateness — not on the tier label itself.
2. **The S4-vs-S5 route (whether the site is a "settlement").** A village high in the hierarchy is *more likely* to be inset and defined as a settlement, engaging S4. But the Annex B definition governs, not the tier. Annex B: a settlement "Includes cities, towns, villages and other predominantly built-up areas … This includes areas defined as a settlement in the development plan". Two carve-outs decide the marginal cases, and neither turns on service-centre status:
   - hamlets and scattered groups of houses outside built-up areas are excluded "unless specifically defined as a settlement in the development plan"; and, decisively for washed-over villages,
   - "For the purpose of this Framework they also **exclude villages which lie within and are defined as part of the Green Belt** in the development plan."
3. **TR3 (sustainable location).** Proximity to a service centre is straightforward evidence on whether occupiers can reach everyday services other than by car — **TR3 (Locating development in sustainable locations)**. This is the only way the term actually appears in the dataset (see §4).

## 3. Why this matters for Claverdon (26/01470/FUL)

Claverdon is a **Local Service Village** under CS.15/CS.16 **and** is washed over by the Green Belt. The two facts pull in opposite directions, and the Framework resolves it cleanly:

- The LSV status is a **local** label. It supports a *development-plan* argument (limited infilling in LSVs under CS.16), but it is not a Framework designation and gives the site no NPPF-level status.
- Because the village "lie[s] within and [is] defined as part of the Green Belt", the Annex B carve-out means it is **not a "settlement" for the Framework**, whatever its local tier. So the route is **S5 / Green Belt**, not S4 — and the "it's a service village, so it's a sustainable settlement" argument does not upgrade that. The service-centre point is therefore best deployed (if at all) on TR3 access, where distance and route quality decide it, not as a standalone principle.

Guard against the reverse error too: the tier label is **not** a reason to treat the location as sustainable in the face of a poor walking route. TR3 is read on the route facts (see `green-belt.md` P1–P3), not on the settlement's plan tier.

## 4. Where it appears in the dataset (and why it's easy to miss)

The term is used in **two case files, both purely as a TR3 access/distance fact**, never as a defined concept:

- **PINS-6008804** (`key_facts`): "Nearest service centre Bridgnorth via narrow lane with no footways or street lighting; very limited evidence on buses." — a car-dependence point.
- **PINS-6009745** (¶14–16): "Unlit single-track lanes with no footway over about 500 m to a Key Service Centre gave 'almost wholly dependent upon the car'." — the same access point, but decided under the December 2024 Framework (transitional, DL ¶11), so not a TR3 case. The 2026 cases that acknowledge a favourable tier and fail TR3 on the footway are Findon 6006900 (service village: "no footways or street lighting on Rogers Lane") and Welland 6006722 (Category 1 settlement: "the unlit footpath (on the opposite side of the street)", bus "only very limited weekday services").

Both are coded correctly as access facts; no recoding is needed. The point of this note is that the concept was only ever present as raw evidence in a couple of letters, with no analysis tying it to the framework — hence it felt buried.

The decision *letters* use the term more widely than the case files (a corpus search for "service village / service centre / Local Service Village" hits about twenty letters). The decisive one is **Findon, PINS-6006900** (South Downs, 18 Sep 2026, dismissed), where the inspector expressly recorded the label and then set it aside on the route: "The nearest settlement to the appeal site is Findon which is a service village. This settlement would provide some limited services … and there is a bus service to Worthing … However, accessing Findon on foot or by bike from the appeal site would involve going along an unlit, unpaved narrow lane before crossing the Findon Bypass … future occupiers would have a reliance on the private car" (¶15); "highly reliant on private cars for most journeys, irrespective of the precise distance" (¶17). See §11 for the full set.

## 5. Coding guidance

- Do **not** create a policy finding for a "service centre" / hierarchy tier. Where a decision turns on it, record the underlying test instead: development-plan accordance (the relevant local policy, e.g. `CS.15`/`CS.16`), the settlement question (Annex B → S4/S5), or `TR3`.
- Keep hierarchy labels in `key_facts` or the relevant `note`, as the two existing cases do, so the evidence is captured without implying Framework weight.
- Cross-references: Annex B "settlement" and the washed-over carve-out are discussed in `green-belt.md`; the TR3 route test is P1–P3 there.

## 6. Wording: "settlement" has two meanings

"Claverdon is in Stratford's settlement hierarchy" and "Claverdon is not a settlement" are both true, but written side by side they look contradictory. The word is doing two jobs:

- **Ordinary and local-plan sense.** A town or village. "Settlement hierarchy" is the established name for a council's ranking of them. The NPPF neither uses nor defines the phrase.
- **NPPF sense.** Annex B defines "settlement" and scopes the exclusion to the Framework: "For the purpose of this Framework they also exclude villages which lie within and are defined as part of the Green Belt in the development plan." It decides the S4/S5 route and nothing else.

Rules for anything we write:

1. **For Stratford, use the plan's own words.** CS.15 is headed "Distribution of Development" and calls Claverdon a "Local Service Village", not a settlement. Write "Claverdon is a Category 3 Local Service Village (CS.15)".
2. **Never use bare "settlement" in the NPPF sense.** Write "not a *settlement* as the NPPF defines it (Annex B)".
3. **As a neutral word for any town, village or hamlet in a hierarchy, use "place"**, its position is its **tier**, and the ranking is the council's **settlement hierarchy**. Record NPPF settlement status as a separate, derived fact.
4. **Present the tension as a finding, not an embarrassment.** The local plan directs growth to Claverdon; the Framework does not treat it as a settlement. That mismatch is itself a consistency point.

Model sentence: "Stratford's Core Strategy treats Claverdon as a Category 3 Local Service Village, a place where some growth is planned. But because the village is washed over by the Green Belt, it is not a *settlement* as the NPPF defines it (Annex B), so housing proposals there are tested under Green Belt policy rather than the NPPF's more permissive 'within settlements' route (S4)."

Caveat: if the SWLP is adopted as drafted, DS.13 insets Claverdon's built-up area from the Green Belt and DS.8C lists it as a Local Service Centre. It would then be an Annex B settlement and this distinction would fall away for sites inside its boundary.

## 7. How Stratford's LSV categorisation was actually done

**Desk-based, per-village and formulaic.** Every settlement with **at least 100 dwellings** was individually scored, but by a points scheme on four facility attributes; nothing in the method assesses how anyone reaches those facilities. Source: Core Strategy 2011–2031 (July 2016), **Appendix 1 "Local Service Villages Methodology"**, p.223 onwards **[PDF]**.

- **Purpose, in the plan's own words.** The LSV category is "an integral part of the proposed distribution of housing development"; the method applies "the proposed 'dispersal' approach"; bands were chosen so that "a different scale of housing development would be applied, according to the range of facilities available in the settlement and accessibility". It is a housing-distribution scorecard.
- **History.** The February 2012 Third Draft method "did not assess the quality of a particular service, e.g. size and opening hours of a general store, frequency of a bus service"; the Proposed Submission version refined it "by applying a qualitative assessment of three key services: general store, primary school and public transport, together with the existing size of the settlement". "Qualitative" here means grading each service's size, hours or frequency, not visiting anything.
- **Scoring.** Size: 600–1,000 dwellings 3, 250–600 2, 100–250 1. General store: large/long hours 3, medium-small with medium-long hours 2, medium-small with short hours 1. Primary school: 210+ places 3, 105–209 2, under 105 or infant/junior only 1. Public transport: at least hourly Mon–Sat 3, at least two-hourly 2, "less frequent than two hourly Mon-Sat, but includes a morning and afternoon work service" 1. A bus counts if within "a maximum distance of 500 metres between the edge of a village and the service". Size is "the overriding factor": under 100 dwellings, no LSV status "regardless of the presence of key services".
- **Bands.** Category 1 (10+): Bishop's Itchington, Harbury, Long Itchington, Quinton, Tiddington. Category 2 (8–9): Brailes, Fenny Compton, Lighthorne Heath, Napton-on-the-Hill, Salford Priors, Stockton, Tysoe, Welford-on-Avon, Wilmcote, Wootton Wawen. **Category 3 (5–7): Claverdon**, Earlswood, Ettington, Great Alne, Ilmington, Long Compton, Newbold-on-Stour, Snitterfield, Temple Herdewycke, Tredington. Category 4 (2–4): Alderminster, Alveston, Aston Cantlow, Bearley, Clifford Chambers, Gaydon, Halford, Hampton Lucy, Ladbroke, Lighthorne, Long Marston, Loxley, Mappleborough Green, Moreton Morrell, Northend, Oxhill, Pillerton Priors, Priors Marston, Tanworth-in-Arden, Wood End.
- **Claverdon's score:** Size 2, Shop 1, School 3, **Public transport 0**, total 6, Category 3 — i.e. below even the 1-point "morning and afternoon work service" threshold. Source: "Local Service Villages – Revised Categories" (2014), `sources:stratford-dc/SDC-LSV-revised-categories-2014.pdf` **[PDF]**; the same scores appear in the Appendix 1 table.
- **Where the data is, and what is not published.** The method and category lists are in Appendix 1; the per-village points are in the Appendix 1 table and the 2014 revised-categories table. The **raw inputs are not published**: the dwelling count used, which store and its hours, the school's place count, the bus timetable assessed. Appendix 1 says only that "the information about service provision will need to be checked and updated on a regular basis in order to apply the LSV categorisation accurately", so the categorisation cannot be audited from the published documents. Note that `sources:stratford-dc/LSA-LSV-2012-Claverdon-FennyCompton.pdf` is a *landscape* sensitivity study, not the LSV scoring.
- **The examining Inspector endorsed the method as a car-dependency ranking, and treated bus realism as decisive for a village's tier.** Inspector's Final Report (June 2016) **[PDF, read]** ¶271: the categorisation "is fit for purpose insofar as it takes account of the key attributes of the many villages in the District. It effectively ranks them so that housing is directed to the largest settlements with facilities, such as shop and school, and frequent public transport that would, taken together, reduce the dependency of prospective residents on the private car." ¶272: Long Marston was to "revert to a Category 4 LSV in the light of the latest bus timetable", because "the first bus does not arrive in Stratford-upon-Avon until well after 0900 hours such that public transport would not be a realistic option for prospective residents in order to gain access to things like education or employment." ¶450: the Green Belt LSVs "should not be excluded from the Green Belt", and "limited infilling within the identified Green Belt LSVs would not be inappropriate development". Two consequences: the tier is, in the Inspector's own words, a *relative ranking by car-dependency* for directing housing; and he treated the realism of the bus service as decisive for a village's tier, which is the criterion on which Claverdon scores 0.
- **What follows.** The one accessibility-flavoured criterion is settlement-level (a bus within 500 m of the village *edge*) and gave Claverdon zero. No walking route, footway, lighting, crossing or site is assessed anywhere in the method.

## 8. The drift into a decision-stage shorthand

- **The plan's own wording invites it.** CS.15 note (3): the list of LSVs is "'dynamic' in order to reflect the sustainability of a particular LSV at any point in time"; and "the status of an individual settlement could alter if the availability of services changes" **[PDF]**. That is the sustainability of the *settlement as a place to direct growth*, reassessed over time, not a site's route.
- **Officer practice.** Reports reason from the tier to the site: "the category 3 Local Service Village benefits from a shop, school, pub and doctors' surgery … not in an unsustainable location" (25/03084/PIP; similar at 22/03139/FUL and 26/00892/PIP; see a private note on SDC decisions on Claverdon sustainability (not published)). The consistency review found Stratford's reports "lean on Local Service Village status as proof of sustainability" (`appeals-consistency-review.md`, issue 5).
- **Sometimes the route analysis is dropped altogether.** At Earlswood, **26/01458/FUL**, GB7(1)(g)(iii) was "passed in one sentence because the dwelling footprint lies within the BUAB of a Category 3 LSV; no TR3 analysis of routes or services" (`cases/stratford-26-01458-FUL.md`, tag `buab-equals-sustainable`).
- **Why it took hold.** Before August 2026 there was no dedicated, codified per-application test for sustainable location. Location sustainability *was* judged on applications (22/01896/FUL, Claverdon Hall Farm: "an unsustainable location … future occupants would be principally reliant on the motor vehicle"), but diffusely, through the sustainable-transport paragraphs and the overall balance, and leaning on where the spatial strategy had put growth. In the absence of a discrete test the plan-making device filled the vacuum. The dataset is 2026-only; a pre-2024 appeal survey of this reasoning has not been done.

## 9. Since August 2026: TR3, and how the tier applies now

- **The test.** TR3(1)(a) requires a location that would "limit the need to travel, particularly by private car, and offer a genuine choice of transport modes"; TR3(2) says the Connectivity Tool should be used alongside other evidence. For a Green Belt village it is reached through GB7(1)(g)(iii) **[NPPF extract]**.
- **The structural change.** Cornerstone Barristers describe the revised NPPF as "the starkest illustration … of the Government's objective to move to a more 'rules-based' planning system", one that "eschews the trigger for the presumption of plans or policies being 'out-of-date', in favour of a locational-based presumption", with materially inconsistent plan policies given "very limited weight" **[web]**.
- **Two roles for the hierarchy now.** (i) *In the plan*, unchanged: S2(1)(a) requires plans to identify "Settlements within the development plan area (applying the definition in the glossary at Annex B) … and their boundaries", and HO6(1)(b) requires plans to "allocate sites which will support and enhance the vitality of rural communities and enable villages to grow and thrive, especially where this will support local services" — both under the plan-making headings **[NPPF extract]**. (ii) *In a decision*, as evidence of plan accordance only (inside the boundary, within the strategy; Claverdon's site is outside both), weighed under s38(6) with the plan's weight fixed by Annex A(2), plus background on what services exist. It does not choose the S4/S5 route (Annex B does) and it does not answer TR3.

## 10. Is the hierarchy "materially inconsistent" with the NPPF? No — and do not argue it

- Annex A(2) reduces weight only for the specific *part* of a policy that is materially inconsistent with a *named* national decision-making policy; discounting a whole policy without identifying the conflicting part is the error the consistency review flags (three reports did it to CS.8).
- A hierarchy does the plan-making job S2(1)(a) and HO6(1)(b) ask for. Plan-level ranking and site-level TR3 testing are complementary, not contradictory.
- Arguing discountability is self-defeating for an objector: the case relies on the plan (NDP H1 boundary, CS.15 spatial strategy, BE1/VDS). SDC has itself been declaring CS.15/CS.16 materially inconsistent and giving them very limited weight (e.g. 26/01458/FUL), which helps applicants. The objection should defend the plan's weight and, separately, deny the tier any role in the TR3 finding.
- Narrow exception: a policy whose wording literally made tier the decision test of sustainability could be inconsistent with TR3. CS.15 does not do that.

## 12. Origins and national context

- **Concept.** A settlement hierarchy is a coarse, comparative ranking of settlements by relative sustainability, chiefly services, size and accessibility, built for plan-making. It ranks settlements not sites; its "sustainability" is a broad composite, not the TR3 test; it is relative within one plan area, not absolute.
- **Origins.** Central place theory; the "key settlement" policy of English county development plans in the 1950s–60s, concentrating rural growth in chosen villages to keep services viable and limit dispersal; Paul Cloke, *Key Settlements in Rural Areas* (1979), with Warwickshire and Devon as case studies **[summary]**.
- **Other hierarchies.** West Oxfordshire (Principal Towns → Service Centres → Large → Medium → Small Villages); Central Lincolnshire (Market Towns → Large Villages 750+ → smaller); Purbeck "key service villages"; East Dorset "rural service centres"; Rossendale "Urban/Rural Local Service Centres"; West Norfolk "Key Rural Service Centres". Tier criteria are set locally, commonly with a school and GP within about 800 m of the settlement boundary **[summary; verify each plan before citing]**. Not comparable between councils.
- **National policy.** The NPPF has no hierarchy and leaves tiers to local plans. PPG Paragraph 67-009: "a wide range of settlements can play a role in delivering sustainable development in rural areas, so blanket policies restricting housing development in some types of settlement will need to be supported by robust evidence of their appropriateness" **[web]** — a plan-making caution, not a site test.
- **What drives car use.** RTPI, *Settlement Patterns, Urban Form and Sustainability* (Research Paper, May 2018): "larger settlements, higher densities and mixed land uses reducing the need to travel by car"; permissions "on edge of settlements and more rural locations" and far from stations "may result in higher levels of car use"; "housing needs to be located in close proximity to public [transport]" **[web, PDF read]**. Supports form-and-access as the driver, and edge/rural sites as car-dependent; note it argues for concentration in larger, denser settlements, the opposite of low-density edge-of-village housing.
- **Not yet read; do not cite:** *Braintree DC v SSCLG* [2018] EWCA Civ 610; *Stratford-on-Avon DC v SSLUHC* [2022] EWHC 445 (Admin); the Taylor Review (2008) "sustainability trap"; Gaydon appeal APP/J3720/W/23/3336035. (The Core Strategy Inspector's Report ¶271, ¶272 and ¶450 have now been read and are quoted in §7.)
