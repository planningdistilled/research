## Summary
A college sought to turn an occupied bookshop on St Giles' into a graduate common room, with no physical works to the Grade II listed building. The inspector found that the loss of the active shop would harm the vitality of the city centre and cause a low level of harm to the conservation area, because the retail use itself contributes to the area's significance. The moderate public benefits did not outweigh that harm under HE6(4), and under S4 the benefits were substantially outweighed. Dismissed.

## Issues and findings
- **Transition.** No switch paragraph. The letter applies the 2026 Framework by policy code (TC2, HE5, HE6, HE9, S3, S4).
- **Town centre vitality, TC2: CONFLICT.** "the proposal would reduce the range of facilities available to the wider community and … would conflict with the Framework and development plan objective of supporting the vitality of the city centre" (DL ¶10; the break is a page footnote).
- **Use as part of significance.** "the active retail use of the appeal property contributes positively to the character of this part of the city centre and, consequently, to the significance of the CA" (DL ¶11).
- **Conservation area, HE9 and HE6: HARM (low).** "the change of use of the ground floor from a commercial premises with an active ground floor frontage to a largely private, educational use would have a harmful effect on the character and significance of the CA" (DL ¶13). "Under the terms of the Framework, the proposal would result in a low level of harm to the significance of the heritage asset. It is noteworthy that even a low level of harm to the significance of a designated heritage asset carries considerable importance and weight." (DL ¶16).
- **HE6(4): FAIL.** "I have not been provided with substantive evidence demonstrating that alternative options have been fully explored or that the proposal represents the only reasonable solution" (DL ¶18). "the scheme’s public benefits, when considered in cumulative terms, attract moderate weight that would not outweigh the considerable importance and weight I ascribe to the harm" (DL ¶19).
- **Listed building.** No physical alterations, so the building and its setting are preserved (DL ¶2).

## Planning balance
HE6(4) was run first, on its own terms (DL ¶16-19). Then S4(1): "the benefits of granting planning permission would be substantially outweighed by adverse effects, when assessed against the Framework’s decision-making policies" (DL ¶24). The development plan conflict (V1, DH3) pointed the same way.

## What made the difference
The shop was occupied and trading, with no evidence that retail use was unviable, and it stood on a street the appraisal identifies as a historic shopping route. The college did not show why its existing common room could not be adapted or why no other space would do. Evidence of a failed retail use, or a scheme that kept an active public frontage secured as part of the description, would have changed both the vitality and the heritage findings.

## Transferable points
- A change of use with no physical works can harm a conservation area where the existing use contributes to its character and significance (DL ¶11-13, ¶15).
- The harm was found although the Council accepted the heritage effect; the inspector's own assessment governs (DL ¶13).
- Low harm to a conservation area still carries considerable importance and weight, and moderate public benefits did not outweigh it (DL ¶16, ¶19).
- Public benefits are discounted where they are not quantified and alternatives have not been explored (DL ¶18).
- Possible community use carries little weight where it is not part of the description of development (DL ¶19).
- TC2 can weigh against a proposal in a town centre: the loss of an occupied shop to a use that is not a main town centre use conflicts with the vitality objective (DL ¶9-10).
